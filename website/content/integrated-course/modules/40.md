# Integration and the life-practice capstone
DESCRIPTION: Combine ideas from the whole course into a bounded project, test them in life, and keep a repeatable review practice.
BOOKS: B003 B018 B026 B055 B066 B074 B133 B150 B158 B172 B183 B188 B192 B205 B221 B249 B287 B291 B293 B299

## Choose a problem worthy of integration
QUESTION: What real situation would benefit from several of the course's skills?
CORE: Select a project with a specific stake, a feasible first action, and enough complexity to require more than one lens. It might concern learning, work, relationships, community, or health literacy. Avoid choosing a crisis that needs immediate specialist care as a course exercise. Define who is affected and what success would look like without promising total transformation.
CONNECT: The course is one sequence because self-knowledge, evidence, action, and ethics meet in actual situations rather than staying in separate book chapters.
EXAMPLE: A person wants a sustainable study practice that fits caregiving duties and improves a specific work skill.
DO: State the project, people affected, baseline, and one bounded outcome.
TRY: Check that the first trial is feasible and its stakes appropriate for self-directed work.
REFLECT: Why is this project meaningful beyond completing the course?
CHECK: What makes a good capstone project?::A meaningful, bounded situation where several skills can be tested safely and reviewed.
MODEL: Choose~Identify the situation.|Bound~Set scope and stakes.|Begin~Define a feasible trial.

## Build a cross-module explanation
QUESTION: Which ideas have different jobs in this project?
CORE: Choose several lessons that contribute distinct functions: one clarifies the aim, one explains the current pattern, one guides action, one checks evidence, and one protects ethics or wellbeing. Do not select ideas merely because they sound compatible. State a real tension or limit between them and how the project will handle it.
CONNECT: The knowledge map supports integration when each link has a reason, not when every concept is connected to everything.
EXAMPLE: Values set a study goal; habit design creates a cue; attention planning protects time; decision review measures learning; family commitments limit the schedule.
DO: Link at least four lessons from different modules with a sentence explaining each job.
TRY: Add one disagreement or limit and how it affects the plan.
REFLECT: Which concept am I using as decoration rather than as a decision aid?
CHECK: What makes integration more than collecting ideas?::Each idea has a defined role, a reason for connection, and a stated limit or tension.
MODEL: Select~Choose relevant lessons.|Connect~Assign jobs.|Limit~Preserve disagreement.

## Create a baseline and decision record
QUESTION: What do I know before acting, and what am I choosing under uncertainty?
CORE: Record present behavior, constraints, resources, affected people, alternatives, and reasons for the chosen trial. Include what would count as improvement and what could go wrong. This record prevents a later result from rewriting the original reasoning. Keep the measurement proportionate to the project rather than collecting data for its own sake.
CONNECT: The first module's starting brief becomes stronger after evidence, systems, and ethics have been studied.
EXAMPLE: A study project records actual weekly sessions, interruptions, and learning checks before changing the schedule.
DO: Write a one-page baseline and decision record for your capstone.
TRY: Ask a trusted person whether one important constraint or stakeholder is missing.
REFLECT: Which assumption would I most like to leave unmeasured?
CHECK: Why record the baseline before acting?::It gives later review an honest comparison and preserves the reasons available at the time.
MODEL: Baseline~Record current conditions.|Choice~State the plan and reasons.|Risk~Name uncertainty and limits.

## Design a reversible experiment
QUESTION: What small action can teach me whether this approach helps?
CORE: Choose an action, a period, a relevant signal, and a condition for stopping or changing it. Keep obligations and consent clear. Change few variables at once so the result is interpretable. A trial can produce useful learning even if it does not meet the hoped-for outcome, provided the evidence is recorded honestly.
CONNECT: Habits, strategy, and causal reasoning converge in the design of a practical experiment.
EXAMPLE: For two weeks, a team tests a shorter meeting with written prework and compares decision quality and staff burden, not only meeting length.
DO: Specify action, duration, signal, review date, and stop condition.
TRY: Explain why the trial is safe and reversible enough for this setting.
REFLECT: What would I learn if the trial fails?
CHECK: What makes a useful experiment?::A specific action, relevant observation, clear review, and a feasible way to change course.
MODEL: Design~Define the intervention.|Observe~Gather relevant signals.|Stop~Protect a revision path.

## Run the trial and record what happened
QUESTION: What actions and results occurred, including unexpected ones?
CORE: Carry out the plan as agreed and record deviations, context, feedback, and consequences for other people. Do not hide misses or change the success criterion after seeing the outcome. If safety or ethics change, stop or revise appropriately and record why. A small honest record is more valuable than an impressive but selective story.
CONNECT: Practice logs, financial records, and scientific notebooks share an ethic of preserving actual events for later judgment.
EXAMPLE: A routine works on two days and fails on a third because caregiving changes the schedule. The record reveals a need for a fallback window.
DO: Keep a dated log of each capstone attempt and relevant conditions.
TRY: Note one unplanned effect on a person or responsibility outside the main metric.
REFLECT: What would my memory omit if I had not written it down?
CHECK: Why record deviations?::They help explain the outcome and reveal whether the plan fits real conditions.
MODEL: Act~Run the trial.|Record~Capture results and context.|Protect~Respond to new risks.

## Review outcome and decision quality separately
QUESTION: What worked, what did not, and what remains uncertain?
CORE: Compare results with the baseline, then evaluate the process used to choose the trial. A good decision may have an unlucky result; a favorable result may hide a weak method or shifted cost. Ask what alternative explanations fit and whose experience is missing. Preserve strengths and name one targeted change rather than judging the whole person or project.
CONNECT: The course's core distinction between event, inference, and judgment returns at a higher level of complexity.
EXAMPLE: The new schedule increases study time but reduces sleep. The outcome is mixed, and the next cycle needs a different allocation.
DO: Write separate paragraphs for observed outcome and quality of the original decision.
TRY: Identify one alternative explanation and one unseen cost.
REFLECT: Which result am I tempted to call a total success or failure?
CHECK: Why separate result from decision process?::Chance, context, and unintended effects can make an outcome a poor verdict on the original reasoning.
MODEL: Outcome~Compare with baseline.|Process~Review reasons and actions.|Uncertainty~Consider alternatives.

## Make a field guide for the next cycle
QUESTION: What should I keep, change, stop, or investigate next?
CORE: A field guide is a concise account another person, or your future self, can use: the situation, useful distinctions, tested actions, evidence, limits, and next decision. Do not write universal advice from one personal trial. State the conditions under which the result may transfer and the questions it leaves open.
CONNECT: Learning becomes contribution when the result is communicated honestly without pretending a single experience solves everyone's life.
EXAMPLE: A learner writes that a short morning session worked when materials were ready and caregiving was covered; the guide includes the failure conditions too.
DO: Create a two-page field guide with evidence, limits, and next action.
TRY: Ask a reader which statement sounds more certain than the record permits.
REFLECT: What is genuinely useful to carry forward?
CHECK: What makes a field guide responsible?::It shares tested insight with context, uncertainty, and limits instead of a universal guarantee.
MODEL: Explain~Describe the situation and method.|Evidence~Show what happened.|Transfer~State when it may help.

## Build a continuing review practice
QUESTION: How will I keep learning after the course's final lesson?
CORE: Choose a realistic rhythm for reviewing values, commitments, evidence, and changing conditions. Keep the practice light enough to maintain and useful enough to affect a decision. Return to lessons when a real problem makes them relevant; do not treat completion as proof of mastery. Invite feedback and retain the right to revise a framework that no longer serves the people involved.
CONNECT: The final outcome is a way of living with attention, care, and correction, assembled from the course rather than any single book.
EXAMPLE: A monthly review checks one goal, one relationship commitment, one evidence question, and one change for the next period.
DO: Set a review rhythm and a short list of questions you will actually use.
TRY: Schedule the first post-course review and identify one person or record that can challenge your view.
REFLECT: What will make my practice more honest and more humane over time?
CHECK: What continues after course completion?::A repeatable cycle of action, observation, feedback, and revision guided by values and limits.
MODEL: Return~Review real conditions.|Learn~Use evidence and feedback.|Renew~Choose the next action.
