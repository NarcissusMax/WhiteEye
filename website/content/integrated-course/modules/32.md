# Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices
DESCRIPTION: Reason about duties, consequences, character, power, and competing obligations without pretending every dilemma has a simple formula.

## Separate what is from what ought to be
QUESTION: Which part of this argument describes a fact, and which part recommends a value?
CORE: A description of common behavior does not by itself establish that the behavior is right. An ethical conclusion needs a value, principle, or account of what matters. State both the facts and the normative premise. People can agree on the facts and disagree about the value, or agree on the value and disagree about facts.
CONNECT: Science informs consequences; ethics judges which consequences and duties matter. Neither can silently replace the other.
EXAMPLE: “Most teams work late” does not establish that late work is fair. The missing question concerns obligations, consent, and costs.
DO: Identify the factual and value premises in one everyday moral claim.
TRY: Ask which premise a reasonable dissenter might reject.
REFLECT: Where have I hidden a value judgment inside a statement of fact?
CHECK: Why cannot a descriptive claim settle an ethical question alone?::A conclusion about what ought to be done requires a normative premise or value.
MODEL: Facts~State what happens.|Value~Name what matters.|Judgment~Show the bridge.

## Compare consequences, duties, and character
QUESTION: Which ethical lens reveals what another might miss?
CORE: Consequence-focused reasoning asks what effects an action has. Duty-focused reasoning asks what obligations and limits apply. Virtue-focused reasoning asks what kind of conduct expresses good character. Each can identify a different issue; none is an automatic calculator. Compare their questions and where they conflict in a concrete case.
CONNECT: Ethical theory becomes useful when it improves the questions you ask of a real decision.
EXAMPLE: Sharing a colleague's private story might produce short-term support for a project, violate confidentiality, and express disloyalty. All three lenses matter.
DO: Analyze one modest decision through outcomes, duties, and character.
TRY: Identify a case where the lenses pull in different directions.
REFLECT: Which lens do I use to avoid an uncomfortable consideration?
CHECK: What do three major ethical lenses emphasize?::Consequences, obligations, and qualities of action or character.
MODEL: Effects~Consider outcomes.|Duties~Identify limits.|Character~Examine the conduct.

## Use reciprocity without erasing differences
QUESTION: Would I accept the treatment I am giving, after considering the other person's actual needs?
CORE: Reciprocity encourages consistency and perspective-taking, but people differ in needs, power, and preferences. “I would be fine with it” cannot replace asking the affected person. Use the principle to catch double standards, then check context and consent. A fair rule should be one you can explain to everyone it affects.
CONNECT: Golden-rule traditions and modern care ethics overlap but differ on how much attention to individual circumstances is required.
EXAMPLE: You would welcome public praise; a colleague strongly prefers private acknowledgment. Reciprocity prompts care, while listening determines the form.
DO: Test one planned action from the other person's position.
TRY: Ask what they actually prefer rather than projecting your own preference.
REFLECT: Where does my imagined perspective fail to represent theirs?
CHECK: What is a limit of simple reciprocity?::Others may have different needs and preferences, so perspective-taking should be checked with them.
MODEL: Reverse~Consider your treatment.|Ask~Learn their context.|Revise~Choose a fair response.

## Treat consent and dignity as real constraints
QUESTION: Does this action use another person merely as a means to my goal?
CORE: A desirable outcome does not automatically justify deception, coercion, or disregard of a person's agency. Explain relevant information, allow meaningful refusal, and account for vulnerability and power. Duties of care and confidentiality may remain even when a shortcut would improve a metric. In complex cases, legal and professional obligations may require current qualified guidance.
CONNECT: Kantian and rights-based arguments put limits on the instrumental tactics found in some persuasion and strategic texts.
EXAMPLE: A manager cannot justify secretly using employee private data by saying it might improve productivity.
DO: Identify whose consent or dignity a proposed action affects.
TRY: Redesign the action so those people can participate knowingly or decline.
REFLECT: Whose choice disappears in my plan?
CHECK: What ethical limit does dignity place on a plan?::People must not be treated only as tools whose informed choice can be bypassed for an outcome.
MODEL: People~Identify those affected.|Choice~Protect informed agency.|Plan~Redesign the means.

## Consider distribution, not only totals
QUESTION: Who bears the cost of the outcome I call good?
CORE: An action can improve an average while concentrating harm on a smaller group. Examine gains and losses separately, including people absent from the decision. Consider whether burdens fall on those with less power or fewer alternatives. Aggregate benefit matters, but it does not erase a question about fairness, rights, or repair.
CONNECT: Systems thinking and political philosophy make ethical review more complete than a single efficiency number.
EXAMPLE: A service change speeds most requests but leaves people with limited internet access unable to participate. The distribution changes the evaluation.
DO: Make a stakeholder map for one proposed improvement.
TRY: Identify a mitigation or redesign for the group bearing the largest cost.
REFLECT: Which person disappears in the average?
CHECK: Why examine distribution?::A good total can conceal serious unequal burdens or exclusion.
MODEL: Gains~Identify benefits.|Burdens~Locate costs.|Justice~Consider redesign and repair.

## Face a duty conflict honestly
QUESTION: Which obligations collide, and what can be preserved?
CORE: Some dilemmas involve genuine competing duties: loyalty and truthfulness, safety and privacy, individual need and group fairness. Name each obligation in its strongest form and examine available options. Avoid resolving the conflict by pretending one duty never existed. A choice may still involve regret and a responsibility to explain or repair.
CONNECT: Epic and philosophical texts often dramatize duty conflicts. Their value lies in sharpening judgment, not handing down an effortless rule for every setting.
EXAMPLE: A colleague asks you to conceal an error that affects others. Loyalty matters, but so does preventing harm; the response should protect both as far as possible.
DO: Write two conflicting duties in one case without caricaturing either.
TRY: Generate an option that preserves part of each, then state what it cannot preserve.
REFLECT: What cost will remain even after the best available choice?
CHECK: What makes a duty conflict genuine?::Two legitimate obligations cannot both be fully satisfied under the available conditions.
MODEL: Duties~Name each obligation.|Options~Compare possible actions.|Account~Explain remaining cost.

## Learn from ethical failure in history
QUESTION: How did a respectable idea or institution become harmful?
CORE: Historical harms can be enabled by prestigious authorities, group incentives, exclusion, and claims that a policy serves a greater good. Examine the actual evidence, who lacked power to object, and how dissent was handled. Avoid reducing the lesson to “bad people in the past”; identify mechanisms that could recur. Historical context explains how a view arose but does not excuse the harm.
CONNECT: Texts on eugenics, race, state violence, and public management show why ethical review needs evidence and accountable power.
EXAMPLE: A movement dresses coercive policy in scientific language while excluding affected people from decision-making. The legitimacy of the institution does not validate the policy.
DO: Analyze a historical case by claim, authority, evidence, affected people, and dissent.
TRY: Identify a present-day safeguard that addresses one mechanism, without claiming the situations are identical.
REFLECT: Which authority cue would have made the claim feel safe to me?
CHECK: Why study ethical failure historically?::It reveals mechanisms by which authority, incentives, and exclusion can normalize harm.
MODEL: Context~Understand the period.|Mechanism~Identify how harm spread.|Safeguard~Design accountability.

## Write an ethical decision record
QUESTION: How can I explain a difficult choice to those affected?
CORE: State the facts, uncertainty, values, duties, likely consequences, affected people, rejected options, and plan for review. A record will not make the choice painless or universally accepted. It makes the reasoning visible and revisable. Include how you will listen to those bearing costs and what repair would be owed if assumptions prove wrong.
CONNECT: This joins decision quality to moral accountability before moving into broader philosophical worldviews.
EXAMPLE: A team records why a resource decision was made, the groups it may burden, and when the outcome will be reviewed.
DO: Create a one-page ethical decision record for a modest real or hypothetical choice.
TRY: Ask who would reasonably challenge the record and on what grounds.
REFLECT: What would I rather leave unsaid because it weakens my preferred conclusion?
CHECK: What belongs in an ethical decision record?::Facts, values, duties, consequences, affected people, alternatives, uncertainty, and review.
MODEL: Explain~Show the reasoning.|Listen~Hear affected people.|Revise~Correct from consequences.
