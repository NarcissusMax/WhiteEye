# Money, records, and financial judgment
DESCRIPTION: Understand cash flow, debt, risk, and behavior so money decisions can be made from records rather than slogans.
BOOKS: B123 B143 B170 B216 B236 B259 B270 B274 B284 B288 B290 B295

## Define what money is for in your plan
QUESTION: Which needs and choices should this money support?
CORE: Money is a resource for obligations, safety, opportunity, and chosen experiences. State the purpose of a financial goal before choosing a tactic. Income, assets, and wealth are different measures; a high-status purchase can reduce flexibility even when it signals success. Include people who depend on the decision and constraints you cannot wish away.
CONNECT: Wealth psychology and accounting describe different sides of the same life. Values prevent the numbers from becoming an end without purpose.
EXAMPLE: You choose a savings target because irregular income makes a buffer valuable, not because a generic milestone says everyone needs the same amount.
DO: Write three financial priorities and the life purpose each serves.
TRY: Identify one current spending pattern that supports or contradicts them.
REFLECT: Which number am I chasing without knowing what it would change?
CHECK: Why define a money goal's purpose?::It guides trade-offs and keeps financial measures tied to actual needs and values.
MODEL: Purpose~Name the need.|Resource~Identify the money involved.|Trade~Choose its use.

## Track cash flow without moralizing it
QUESTION: What comes in, what goes out, and when?
CORE: A cash-flow record shows income, essential expenses, flexible expenses, debt payments, and timing. Start with actual transactions rather than an ideal budget. Categories help only when they reveal decisions you can make. A deficit may reflect insufficient income or unavoidable costs, not simply lack of discipline. Review the record with that context.
CONNECT: The accounting texts supply the record; behavior texts help explain recurring spending. Neither should erase structural limits.
EXAMPLE: Monthly totals look balanced, but rent is due before two late payments arrive. The timing problem becomes visible only in a dated cash-flow view.
DO: Record one month of income and spending with dates and clear categories.
TRY: Identify one timing issue and one category whose cost is genuinely adjustable.
REFLECT: What does the record show that my general impression did not?
CHECK: Why record timing as well as totals?::Cash shortages can occur when obligations arrive before income even if monthly totals seem adequate.
MODEL: Record~Capture actual flows.|Classify~Separate needs and choices.|Review~Find timing and options.

## Build a budget as a revisable plan
QUESTION: How will future income meet known obligations and priorities?
CORE: A budget estimates income and planned uses over a period. Compare it with actual results and revise assumptions. Include irregular costs and a margin where possible rather than assigning every unit to an optimistic plan. Avoid treating a missed estimate as personal failure; investigate whether the model or circumstances changed. A budget is useful if it improves decisions, not if it merely looks tidy.
CONNECT: Planning and systems thinking meet in the cycle of forecast, observation, and adjustment.
EXAMPLE: A household repeatedly exceeds a food estimate. Reviewing receipts reveals a recurring schedule problem and an unrealistic initial amount, prompting a different plan.
DO: Draft a budget based on actual records and upcoming obligations.
TRY: Compare one period of planned and actual spending, then change one assumption.
REFLECT: Which expense is irregular only because I keep forgetting it?
CHECK: What is a budget?::A future income-and-expense plan that should be compared with actual results and revised.
MODEL: Forecast~Estimate flows.|Compare~Inspect actuals.|Revise~Update the plan.

## Understand assets, liabilities, and net position
QUESTION: What do I own, owe, and have available?
CORE: An asset is a resource with value; a liability is an obligation. A balance-sheet view can reveal that an impressive purchase is financed by a large debt. Net position is a snapshot, not a measure of character or a full account of cash needs. Valuations can be uncertain, so keep records and note assumptions.
CONNECT: Accounting vocabulary helps test wealth claims that focus only on income or visible possessions.
EXAMPLE: A vehicle has resale value and a loan attached. Counting only the vehicle's value overstates the household's financial position.
DO: Create a simple personal snapshot of assets and liabilities with dated estimates.
TRY: Mark which values are uncertain and which resources are readily available.
REFLECT: What does the snapshot omit about future cash obligations?
CHECK: What does a balance-sheet view compare?::Resources with value and obligations owed at a point in time.
MODEL: Assets~List resources.|Liabilities~List obligations.|Context~Note liquidity and uncertainty.

## Compare borrowing by full cost and purpose
QUESTION: What future obligation does this borrowing create?
CORE: Borrowing moves purchasing ability across time and adds terms, interest, fees, and risk. Read the full repayment schedule, total cost, and consequences of missed payments. Compare the financed use with alternatives and your likely cash flow. A low monthly payment can hide a longer and more expensive obligation. Avoid relying on a book's dated product examples for current decisions.
CONNECT: Present bias and framing can make a payment feel affordable while its future cost remains invisible.
EXAMPLE: Two loans have similar monthly payments but different terms and total costs. The longer one costs more despite appearing easier in the first month.
DO: For a hypothetical loan, compare principal, term, fees, payment schedule, and total repayment.
TRY: Identify the cash-flow condition under which the obligation would become difficult.
REFLECT: Am I comparing the whole contract or only the first payment?
CHECK: What belongs in a borrowing comparison?::Purpose, full repayment cost, timing, terms, and the ability to meet future obligations.
MODEL: Terms~Read the agreement.|Cash~Test future payments.|Alternatives~Compare other options.

## Treat investing as risk management, not a prediction contest
QUESTION: What risk am I taking, and when might I need the money?
CORE: Investing outcomes are uncertain. Match any consideration of risk to the goal's time horizon, capacity for loss, liquidity needs, and total costs. Diversification can reduce concentration risk but does not remove market losses. Fees compound as a cost. Beware promises of high returns with little risk, and use current regulated guidance and qualified advice for actual products.
CONNECT: Behavioral finance explains why stories of recent winners can dominate judgment. A decision record helps separate plan from excitement.
EXAMPLE: Money needed soon for an essential payment has a different risk profile from money set aside for a distant goal. The same product need not fit both.
DO: For a hypothetical goal, state time horizon, liquidity need, possible loss, and fees to examine.
TRY: Write two questions you would ask before trusting an investment offer.
REFLECT: Which recent success story is doing more work than my own risk constraints?
CHECK: Does diversification eliminate investment risk?::No. It can reduce concentration, while broad market and other risks remain.
MODEL: Goal~Name timing and need.|Risk~Assess loss and liquidity.|Cost~Inspect fees and terms.

## Notice behavioral traps around money
QUESTION: Is this choice driven by evidence or by a recent gain, loss, or comparison?
CORE: Money decisions can be shaped by loss aversion, sunk costs, overconfidence, and social comparison. Name the specific choice and available alternatives before applying a bias label. A feeling of regret is real but does not change a future payoff. Use records and precommitted review rules for consequential choices, while allowing that changed circumstances can justify changing course.
CONNECT: The same biases studied in observation and decision modules become sharper when money and identity are involved.
EXAMPLE: You keep an unsuitable service because you paid for a year. The past payment cannot be recovered; the relevant question is whether continuing adds value or costs.
DO: Review one financial choice for sunk cost, framing, or overconfidence.
TRY: Rewrite the decision using future costs and benefits only.
REFLECT: What would I choose if nobody knew what I had already spent?
CHECK: Why ignore a sunk cost in a forward decision?::A past irrecoverable payment cannot be changed by continuing an unsuitable choice.
MODEL: Past~Identify irrecoverable cost.|Future~Compare remaining options.|Choose~Decide from current consequences.

## Know the limits of general money advice
QUESTION: Which parts of this decision depend on current rules and personal facts?
CORE: Taxes, credit, investing, and insurance vary by jurisdiction and change over time. A general book can teach questions and categories, not determine your present legal entitlement or ideal product. Keep accurate records, identify the decision, and consult current official or qualified sources when the stakes require it. A promise that a technique makes wealth inevitable deserves special scrutiny.
CONNECT: Financial literacy combines self-knowledge, accounting, evidence, and appropriate specialist help.
EXAMPLE: Before applying a deduction described in an older book, you check current local rules and your eligibility with a qualified source.
DO: Separate general principles from current-rule questions in one financial topic.
TRY: Create a short checklist of facts and documents a qualified adviser would need.
REFLECT: Which confident rule in an old source might no longer apply?
CHECK: Why are old financial books insufficient for current specific choices?::Rules, products, risks, and personal circumstances can change materially.
MODEL: General~Learn the concept.|Specific~Identify current facts.|Verify~Use qualified, current guidance.
