## Choose a sport context and learning goal
QUESTION: What skill belongs to this sport, rule set, and level of experience?
CORE: Football, martial arts, and strength events reward different decisions. Even within one sport, equipment, contact rules, and coaching standards change what is appropriate. Define the actual setting before transferring a drill from a book. A technique that appears in a manual is not automatically safe or legal in your practice context.
CONNECT: The course's transfer lesson matters here: shared ideas about timing and balance do not make sports interchangeable.
EXAMPLE: A football passing drill can teach scanning under pressure; it does not teach the same bodily risks or rules as a contact sport.
DO: Name one sport's rules, skill target, current level, and supervision needs.
TRY: Check whether a proposed drill fits that context.
REFLECT: Which attractive drill belongs to a different setting than mine?
CHECK: Why define the sport context first?::Rules, equipment, contact, skill level, and supervision determine what practice is appropriate.
MODEL: Context~Define the setting.|Skill~Name the target.|Fit~Choose suitable practice.

## Progress from isolated technique to live decisions
QUESTION: When should a drill become more realistic?
CORE: Simple practice lets a learner notice a movement or choice. Add timing, partners, opposition, and uncertainty gradually when the skill is stable and the setting is safe. A drill may look fluent yet fail during live play if it never required perception or choice. More realism must be matched with readiness and supervision, especially where contact is possible.
CONNECT: Deliberate practice and soccer drill collections both show the value of progression; contact training adds safety requirements.
EXAMPLE: Players first pass accurately without pressure, then choose among passing options as defenders close lanes.
DO: Arrange three stages for one non-harmful sport skill: simple, variable, and game-like.
TRY: Define a sign that the learner is ready for the next stage.
REFLECT: Is this drill teaching a decision or only repeating a movement?
CHECK: Why progress drills?::To move from controlled execution toward perception and choice under realistic conditions.
MODEL: Isolate~Learn the action.|Vary~Add one demand.|Apply~Use it in play.

## Scan before receiving or acting
QUESTION: What information is available before the ball or task reaches me?
CORE: Team play rewards noticing teammates, opponents, space, and the next likely option before receiving. The exact scanning behavior depends on sport and role. Practice linking observation to a choice rather than turning the head mechanically. Review whether the player noticed useful information, not only whether the final pass succeeded.
CONNECT: Observation and strategy become visible in sport because conditions change quickly and teammates depend on one another.
EXAMPLE: A midfielder checks nearby passing lanes before receiving and can choose a safer first touch when pressure arrives.
DO: Watch a short play and pause before one player's action. List visible options.
TRY: In a supervised drill, practice noticing one relevant cue before acting.
REFLECT: Which option became invisible because I looked only at the immediate object?
CHECK: What is the purpose of pre-action scanning?::To gather relevant information early enough to choose an appropriate response.
MODEL: Scan~Look for people and space.|Choose~Select a feasible option.|Review~Check the quality of the decision.

## Understand spacing and support
QUESTION: How can teammates create useful options for one another?
CORE: Passing options depend on distance, angle, timing, and the position of opposition. A triangle can create multiple connections, but its usefulness changes with movement and pressure. Communicate and adapt rather than standing in an ideal shape while play moves elsewhere. Respect roles and rule constraints in the chosen sport.
CONNECT: Systems thinking shows how individual movement can change the options available to everyone else.
EXAMPLE: A teammate moves into a visible support lane, allowing the player with the ball to avoid a forced risky pass.
DO: Draw a simple possession with three teammates and two defenders; mark available lanes.
TRY: Change one player's location and explain the new options and costs.
REFLECT: Did my movement help the group even if I never touched the ball?
CHECK: Why does spacing matter?::It changes available connections, decisions, and pressure across the whole team.
MODEL: Position~Locate players.|Options~Map lanes.|Move~Create or protect a useful connection.

## Compare tactical formations by purpose
QUESTION: What does a formation make easier, and what does it expose?
CORE: A formation is an initial organization of roles and spaces, not a guarantee of behavior. Compare how arrangements support possession, pressing, transition, and defensive cover under particular players and opponents. A number label alone says little about the movements that follow. Use match evidence and coaching feedback rather than treating one system as universally best.
CONNECT: Strategy and team leadership meet in the assignment of responsibilities and trade-offs.
EXAMPLE: A wider arrangement may create passing options on the flank while leaving a central gap if players do not coordinate transitions.
DO: Compare two formations by one strength, one vulnerability, and one player demand.
TRY: Watch how the shape changes when possession is lost.
REFLECT: Am I judging a formation by its diagram or its behavior?
CHECK: What is a formation?::A starting organization whose value depends on roles, movement, opponents, and transitions.
MODEL: Shape~See the initial roles.|Behavior~Observe movement.|Trade~Evaluate gains and exposure.

## Learn balance, coordination, and breath under supervision
QUESTION: What supports controlled movement across a changing task?
CORE: Balance depends on posture, base of support, movement, and the task at hand. Breath and pacing affect how a person manages effort. Study principles through low-risk exercises appropriate to your body and sport, then seek qualified supervision for contact or advanced techniques. Static diagrams cannot assess an individual's movement or injury risk.
CONNECT: Martial arts anatomy, core stability, and sport training share movement concepts, though their applications and hazards differ.
EXAMPLE: In a coached non-contact drill, a learner moves slowly through a change of direction and notices whether they can regain stable posture.
DO: Describe one safe movement's base, direction, and balance demands.
TRY: Ask a qualified coach for feedback on one observable feature.
REFLECT: What does my body actually do that the book's illustration cannot show?
CHECK: Why use supervision for complex movement?::A qualified observer can adapt feedback and safety to the person and actual performance.
MODEL: Setup~Choose a safe task.|Move~Observe control.|Feedback~Adjust with guidance.

## Respect the boundary between sport and harm
QUESTION: Which material is appropriate for a course about better living?
CORE: The collection includes competitive sport, historical martial traditions, weapon manuals, and harmful targeting instructions. These cannot all become unsupervised practice. Study cultural context, training ethics, safety, and conflict avoidance where relevant. Keep techniques involving weapons, incapacitation, or serious injury outside the course's how-to exercises. If a person faces real danger, qualified local guidance and immediate safety matter more than a book-derived maneuver.
CONNECT: Discipline and restraint are valuable themes across martial writing; operational harm is a different category with different risks.
EXAMPLE: A learner chooses an accredited, safety-conscious club and asks about protective rules, coaching, and injury procedures rather than practicing hazardous manual sequences alone.
DO: Compare two training environments by supervision, rules, consent, and injury response.
TRY: Write the questions you would ask before joining a contact class.
REFLECT: Is this practice building skill under rules or rehearsing avoidable harm?
CHECK: Why does training context matter?::Contact, weapons, and injury risk require rules, consent, qualified instruction, and appropriate safeguards.
MODEL: Context~Identify risk and rules.|Coach~Seek qualified supervision.|Restraint~Keep practice within safe bounds.

## Review team and individual learning together
QUESTION: What did the session change for the learner and the group?
CORE: Track a small number of skill and decision signals: technique under suitable challenge, quality of choices, communication, and recovery. Ask the athlete for their account and compare it with observation. Team outcomes can hide an individual's improvement; a personal success can still create a problem for teammates. Use the review to choose the next drill.
CONNECT: Feedback loops, leadership, and physical recovery combine in a good practice review.
EXAMPLE: Passing accuracy improves in an isolated exercise, but the player still hesitates under pressure. The next session varies the decision context rather than repeating only static passes.
DO: Review a practice session using one individual and one team signal.
TRY: Choose the next drill because it addresses the observed limit.
REFLECT: What did we measure because it was easy rather than because it mattered?
CHECK: What should a sport practice review include?::Relevant individual and team signals, the learner's perspective, and a specific next practice target.
MODEL: Observe~Gather performance signals.|Discuss~Compare perspectives.|Design~Choose the next challenge.
