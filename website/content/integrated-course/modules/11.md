# Evidence, science, and causal claims
DESCRIPTION: Examine how knowledge is built, distinguish association from intervention, and use evidence with appropriate humility.
BOOKS: B027 B046 B059 B079 B106 B115 B188 B207 B227 B289 B291 B302

## Ask what a claim would predict
QUESTION: If this explanation is useful, what should we expect to observe?
CORE: A scientific claim gains force when it helps explain observations and exposes itself to possible correction. Specify what the claim predicts, under what conditions, and what observation would trouble it. Some ideas are valuable as meanings or stories without making testable empirical claims. Keep those roles distinct. An explanation that can accommodate every possible result without revision offers little empirical guidance.
CONNECT: Philosophy of science examines how theories, models, and evidence relate. Everyday inquiry can borrow this discipline without requiring a laboratory for every decision.
EXAMPLE: “This new schedule improves focus” predicts a measurable difference in completed work under comparable conditions, not just a stronger feeling of intention.
DO: Choose one claim and write a possible observation it predicts.
TRY: Identify a result that would make you revise the claim.
REFLECT: Have I protected this explanation from every possible test?
CHECK: What makes an empirical claim examinable?::It specifies observable implications and conditions under which evidence could require revision.
MODEL: Claim~State the explanation.|Prediction~Name an observable implication.|Test~Define a possible challenge.

## Distinguish correlation from intervention
QUESTION: Would changing this factor change the outcome?
CORE: Two things can vary together because one affects the other, a third factor affects both, or the pattern is coincidental or distorted by selection. A causal question asks what would happen if one factor were changed while relevant alternatives were addressed. Observing an association is a starting clue, not a completed causal argument.
CONNECT: The ladder from observation to intervention helps organize personal experiments, scientific studies, and policy debate. The relevant design depends on stakes and feasibility.
EXAMPLE: People who keep journals report better clarity. They may journal because they are already motivated, or journaling may help, or both. A careful trial would compare what changes when the practice is introduced.
DO: Draw three possible causal diagrams for one observed association.
TRY: Identify a feasible comparison that would distinguish at least two diagrams.
REFLECT: Which direction of cause did I assume without checking?
CHECK: Why does association alone not establish causation?::Several causal structures and selection effects can produce the same observed pattern.
MODEL: Association~Notice the pattern.|Alternatives~Draw possible causes.|Intervention~Ask what changing one factor would do.

## Look for confounding variables
QUESTION: What third factor might influence both the presumed cause and result?
CORE: A confounder can create or distort an observed relationship. Before recommending an intervention, ask what else differs between groups. Not every variable should be controlled mechanically: some lie on the causal path you want to study. Draw the proposed relationships and explain why a comparison is fair. This is more informative than simply listing every factor you can imagine.
CONNECT: Causal diagrams make hidden assumptions explicit. They also reveal why a statistically sophisticated analysis can still answer the wrong question if the causal structure is mistaken.
EXAMPLE: A program's graduates earn more than nonparticipants. Prior experience may affect both admission and earnings, so the raw difference may overstate the program's effect.
DO: For one causal claim, name one plausible common cause and how it affects both sides.
TRY: Sketch a simple diagram and describe the comparison you would need.
REFLECT: Am I controlling for a relevant cause or adding variables without a theory?
CHECK: What is a confounder?::A factor that affects both the proposed cause and outcome, distorting the observed association.
MODEL: Draw~Map proposed relationships.|Identify~Find common causes.|Compare~Design a fairer test.

## Understand comparison groups and selection
QUESTION: Who was included, excluded, or assigned differently?
CORE: A result depends on the group studied and the process that put people into conditions. Random assignment can reduce some biases, but it may be impossible, unethical, or unrepresentative. Observational comparisons need careful attention to baseline differences. Check sample size, follow-up, missing results, and whether the studied group resembles the people to whom the claim is applied.
CONNECT: Survivorship bias and causal inference meet at selection. A precise average can still mislead if it describes the wrong group.
EXAMPLE: A training method looks effective among volunteers who complete it. People who left may have found it unhelpful or impractical; their outcomes matter too.
DO: Find a study summary and identify its groups, selection process, and missing cases.
TRY: State one population to which the result may not transfer easily.
REFLECT: Which excluded outcome would most change my conclusion?
CHECK: Why inspect selection?::Who enters and remains in a comparison can change the apparent effect and its applicability.
MODEL: Population~Who is studied?|Assignment~How did groups form?|Transfer~Who else is the result meant to describe?

## Separate model, mechanism, and reality
QUESTION: What does this model illuminate, and what does it leave out?
CORE: A model simplifies a complex situation to explain or predict selected features. A proposed mechanism describes how a change might produce an effect. Neither is the thing itself. Compare the model's assumptions with the setting in which you use it, and examine whether its predictions hold. A useful model can be incomplete; a memorable metaphor can be misleading when treated as a literal mechanism.
CONNECT: Economic, psychological, biological, and spiritual frameworks in the collection vary greatly in evidence and purpose. Calling each a model does not make them equally tested.
EXAMPLE: A habit loop helps locate prompts and rewards. It does not by itself explain every effect of poverty, illness, workplace rules, or relationships on behavior.
DO: Choose one model and list what it represents and omits.
TRY: Find one case where it offers a useful question and one where it offers little guidance.
REFLECT: Have I confused a simple diagram with a complete account?
CHECK: What is a model's proper role?::To simplify selected features for explanation or prediction while keeping assumptions and omissions visible.
MODEL: Scope~Identify represented features.|Test~Compare predictions with cases.|Limit~Name omissions.

## Weigh anecdotes and systematic evidence
QUESTION: What can one vivid story establish?
CORE: A personal account can show that an experience is possible, convey meaning, and generate a hypothesis. It rarely tells you how common the outcome is or whether one factor caused it. Systematic evidence can answer broader questions but may miss important individual context. Use each for the question it can actually address, and look for independent checks when a claim is consequential.
CONNECT: The collection contains memoirs, testimonials, experimental discussions, and professional references. Their evidential roles differ even when the prose is equally compelling.
EXAMPLE: One person reports recovery after a routine. The account matters, but it does not establish that the routine caused recovery for others.
DO: Identify the exact claim supported by one anecdote you encountered.
TRY: Write the stronger claim the anecdote does not yet establish and the evidence it would require.
REFLECT: Am I treating emotional force as a substitute for comparison?
CHECK: What can an anecdote establish most directly?::That a reported experience occurred for someone, while broader frequency and causal claims need further evidence.
MODEL: Story~Identify the experience.|Claim~Limit the conclusion.|Check~Seek evidence suited to the broader question.

## Consider replication, uncertainty, and revision
QUESTION: Has a result held up under repeated and independent checks?
CORE: A single finding can be affected by chance, measurement choices, or a special context. Replication, transparent methods, and converging evidence increase confidence, though they do not create absolute certainty. When a claim is revised, ask what changed and whether the new account explains the evidence better. Do not interpret revision as proof that all methods are equally unreliable.
CONNECT: Scientific humility means confidence can increase or decrease for reasons. It differs from reflexive skepticism that refuses to learn from any evidence.
EXAMPLE: A small initial study suggests a strong effect, while later broader work finds a modest one. The updated conclusion may still support a limited use without the original promise.
DO: For one claim, look for independent tests and note differences in population and method.
TRY: State your current confidence and the evidence that would change it.
REFLECT: Am I preserving my first impression after the evidential picture changed?
CHECK: Why does independent replication matter?::It checks whether a result survives new samples, methods, and investigators.
MODEL: Initial~Describe the first result.|Repeat~Inspect independent checks.|Update~Revise confidence.

## Match evidence to the stakes
QUESTION: How much confidence does this decision require?
CORE: The amount of evidence needed depends on potential harm, reversibility, cost, and the availability of alternatives. A low-cost personal experiment may begin with modest evidence; medical treatment, large investments, or public policy require stronger scrutiny and qualified input. Acting under uncertainty can be responsible when the decision process reflects its stakes. Do not let a dramatic claim bypass that assessment.
CONNECT: This connects epistemology to agency: the point of evaluating evidence is to make better choices, not to collect doubt indefinitely.
EXAMPLE: Trying a new note-taking layout is easy to reverse. Replacing prescribed care on the basis of a testimonial is a very different decision.
DO: Rank three decisions by harm, reversibility, and required confidence.
TRY: Choose a proportionate next step for each, such as a small trial, more information, or qualified advice.
REFLECT: Where have I demanded certainty for a trivial change or accepted little evidence for a consequential one?
CHECK: What should set the evidence threshold?::The decision's stakes, reversibility, alternatives, and potential harms.
MODEL: Stakes~Assess cost and harm.|Evidence~Seek proportionate support.|Action~Choose a reversible or qualified path when needed.
