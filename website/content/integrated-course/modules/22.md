# Planning, collaboration, and delivery
DESCRIPTION: Turn a tested idea into coordinated work with clear roles, dependencies, quality checks, and a responsible finish.
BOOKS: B133 B202 B216 B270 B272 B287 B293 B306

## Define the deliverable in the recipient's terms
QUESTION: What must exist at the end, and how will the intended recipient use it?
CORE: A plan becomes clearer when it names the output, the person who needs it, and the conditions under which it will be useful. 'Finish the project' is too vague if nobody can tell whether the result is ready. Describe a deliverable and an acceptance check that reflects its purpose. Avoid promising a perfect product before you have learned what is needed. The person receiving the work should have a chance to clarify the result.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] helps test whether the proposed output addresses the problem. [[24 Business, customers, and useful value]] asks whether delivery creates value rather than only activity.
EXAMPLE: A neighborhood group needs a sign-up form. The deliverable is a form people can complete on a phone, with responses reaching the organizer securely; a visually polished mockup alone would not meet the need.
DO: State one project deliverable in one sentence naming recipient, use, and acceptance check.
TRY: Ask an intended recipient what would make it useful and what would make it fail.
REFLECT: Was I planning to complete work or to deliver something another person could use?
CHECK: What defines a useful deliverable?::A concrete output tied to a recipient's use and an acceptance check based on that purpose.
MODEL: Name~Specify the output.|Confirm~Learn the recipient's use.|Check~Define what ready means.

## Map dependencies before assigning dates
QUESTION: Which tasks cannot begin until another result exists?
CORE: A list of tasks does not show their order. Draw the dependencies: which decision, resource, approval, or component must arrive before another task can proceed. Estimate dates only after the path is visible and include ordinary delays. Identify work that can happen in parallel and work that is truly sequential. An early missed dependency deserves attention even if many easy tasks are on schedule.
CONNECT: [[06 Attention, time, and a workable day]] helps make individual capacity realistic. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] shows how a chain of connected tasks can limit the whole result.
EXAMPLE: A public event needs a venue before invitations can give a confirmed location. Designing the invitation can begin earlier, but sending it depends on the booking.
DO: Draw five to eight tasks for a current project and connect the true dependencies.
TRY: Identify one dependency with high uncertainty and a backup route if it is late.
REFLECT: Which deadline did I set without checking the work it depends on?
CHECK: Why draw dependencies before dates?::Dates are only credible when the order of required work and likely delays are visible.
MODEL: List~Name tasks and results.|Link~Draw prerequisites.|Schedule~Assign dates with capacity and uncertainty.

## Limit work in progress
QUESTION: How does starting too many tasks slow the work that matters?
CORE: Each open task consumes attention, coordination, and unfinished follow-up. A team can look busy while every deliverable waits. Choose a small number of active tasks, make blocked work visible, and finish or deliberately pause one item before adding another. The right limit depends on role and urgency; it is a way to expose overload, not a rule that every person must do only one thing.
CONNECT: [[06 Attention, time, and a workable day]] addresses the individual's day. [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] addresses the team's system. A work-in-progress limit is useful when it improves flow and honest prioritization.
EXAMPLE: Three people each start five improvements and none is ready for users. They agree to finish two shared priorities first and park the others with named review dates.
DO: Count active tasks in one project and identify which have no realistic next step this week.
TRY: Set a temporary limit for a week and track completions, delays, and urgent exceptions.
REFLECT: Did starting a new task feel easier than finishing a difficult one?
CHECK: What does a work-in-progress limit help reveal?::Whether too many simultaneous commitments are slowing completion or hiding blocked work.
MODEL: Count~Make active work visible.|Limit~Choose a feasible number.|Finish~Complete or pause before starting more.

## Give each handoff a clear owner
QUESTION: Who knows when a task is ready for the next person?
CORE: Handoffs fail when the sender assumes work is done and the receiver discovers missing information later. Name the owner of the current step, the recipient, the format, and what must be checked before transfer. Keep the handoff light enough to use. Do not make one person responsible for an outcome they cannot control; give them a route to report a blocked dependency.
CONNECT: [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] links authority with accountability. [[17 Influence, persuasion, and negotiation]] helps agree on terms rather than relying on unspoken expectations.
EXAMPLE: A writer sends a draft to a designer. The handoff includes the approved text, image rights, required dimensions, and a contact for unresolved questions. Without those details, the designer cannot safely finish.
DO: Identify one recurring handoff and list what the receiver needs to start confidently.
TRY: Agree on a short handoff check with the next person and test it once.
REFLECT: Which missing detail repeatedly returned work to the previous stage?
CHECK: What makes a handoff complete?::A named owner and recipient, usable materials, an acceptance check, and a route for unresolved issues.
MODEL: Prepare~Check required inputs.|Transfer~Name the recipient and status.|Confirm~Verify the next step can begin.

## Communicate progress by exceptions and decisions
QUESTION: What does a teammate need to know to act, rather than to admire an update?
CORE: A useful status update tells others what changed, what is blocked, what decision is needed, who owns it, and when the next update will arrive. It need not narrate every hour of work. Be especially clear when a forecast changes; early notice protects options for everyone else. A status ritual should fit the project's pace and avoid consuming more time than the information is worth.
CONNECT: [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] makes the message concise. [[04 Agency, responsibility, and boundaries]] supports raising a limit before a promise silently fails.
EXAMPLE: 'The prototype is mostly done' is hard to use. 'The form works on desktop; mobile validation is blocked by missing test accounts; Priya will provide access Tuesday; the release date may move one day' supports action.
DO: Rewrite one vague project update as progress, blocker, decision, owner, and next date.
TRY: Ask a teammate which part of the update helped them act and which part was unnecessary.
REFLECT: Was I reporting reality or trying to sound on schedule?
CHECK: What belongs in an actionable progress update?::Relevant change, blocker, needed decision, owner, and next check point.
MODEL: State~Report what changed.|Escalate~Name blockers and decisions.|Return~Set the next update.

## Protect quality with an acceptance check
QUESTION: How do I know a deliverable is ready for its actual use?
CORE: Quality is not only absence of obvious defects. It includes whether the work serves the user, meets agreed constraints, and can be corrected if something was missed. Turn the deliverable's purpose into a short check that someone other than the maker can perform. Include important accessibility, privacy, safety, or accuracy requirements where relevant. A check should not become so elaborate that it prevents a small reversible pilot.
CONNECT: [[23 Creative work and sustained projects]] distinguishes release from endless polishing. [[35 Science, models, and technical literacy]] asks whether the test matches the claim being made. Quality is contextual and observable.
EXAMPLE: A weekly report is ready when its numbers reconcile, its conclusion matches them, and the recipient can locate the action needed. Decorative formatting alone is insufficient.
DO: Write three acceptance checks for one deliverable, including one from the recipient's perspective.
TRY: Ask someone unfamiliar with the work to attempt the intended use and note where they struggle.
REFLECT: Which check protected the purpose, and which only protected my preference?
CHECK: What should an acceptance check test?::Whether the deliverable serves its intended use and meets the important agreed constraints.
MODEL: Derive~Turn purpose into checks.|Test~Have another person try the use.|Correct~Fix failures before or after a bounded release.

## Handle a change request without losing the plan
QUESTION: What should happen when the goal or constraints change during delivery?
CORE: Change is normal, but unexamined additions can overload a project. Describe the requested change, the reason for it, its effect on time, cost, quality, and other commitments, and who may decide. Consider what would be removed or delayed to make room. A necessary change should be welcomed and planned, not hidden under the old deadline. Record the decision so the team shares the new target.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] uses opportunity cost. [[17 Influence, persuasion, and negotiation]] helps discuss terms with the requester. A project plan is a living agreement, not an excuse to ignore evidence.
EXAMPLE: A client adds a second language to a brochure. The team can accept with a revised date and translation review, or schedule it for a later release. Pretending the original timeline still applies makes errors more likely.
DO: Take one change request and write what it adds, what it displaces, and who must agree.
TRY: Present two honest options with different time or scope consequences.
REFLECT: Did I treat a request as free because I wanted to appear helpful?
CHECK: What should accompany a project change?::A clear description of its effects on scope, time, cost, quality, and commitments, plus an authorized decision.
MODEL: Describe~Clarify the requested change.|Trade~Show consequences and alternatives.|Record~Agree on the revised plan.

## Close the work and keep the learning
QUESTION: When is a project finished, and what should be learned before the team moves on?
CORE: Closing a project includes confirming delivery, handing over maintenance or support, settling open commitments, and recording what was learned. Review the original problem, decisions, surprises, and effects on users. Ask what to repeat and what to change in the next cycle. Do not use a retrospective to assign easy blame after the outcome is known; look at conditions and information available at the time.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] supplies the test record. [[40 Integration and the life-practice capstone]] turns review into a continuing practice. A completed artifact and a completed learning cycle are related but distinct.
EXAMPLE: After launching a community guide, the team confirms who updates it, gathers first-use feedback, closes unpaid invoices, and records that review started too late for one important translation issue.
DO: Write a closeout list for one project: deliverable accepted, remaining owner, obligations, results, and lessons.
TRY: Ask one participant what they would keep and one thing they would change next time.
REFLECT: Did I leave a usable result and a clear owner for what follows?
CHECK: What is included in responsible project closeout?::Confirmed delivery, handover of remaining duties, settled commitments, and a review that improves the next cycle.
MODEL: Deliver~Confirm acceptance.|Handover~Assign ongoing responsibility.|Review~Record evidence and lessons.
