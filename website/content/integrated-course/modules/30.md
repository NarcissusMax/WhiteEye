# Digital security and privacy
DESCRIPTION: Protect accounts and data, recognize deceptive requests, and understand authorized security learning without reproducing attack playbooks.
BOOKS: B053 B153 B159 B162 B186 B223 B240 B252 B278 B313

## Begin with a threat model
QUESTION: What am I protecting, from what plausible risk, and at what cost?
CORE: List important accounts, information, devices, and relationships. Consider who might seek access and how, using the actual context rather than every imaginable threat. Prioritize protections that reduce likely harm without making ordinary life unworkable. Review the model when your situation changes.
CONNECT: Safety planning and digital privacy share the need for proportionate preparation. The most dramatic scenario is not always the most relevant.
EXAMPLE: A student's main risks may be losing access to schoolwork and falling for a fake login message, not a sophisticated surveillance operation.
DO: List three digital assets, plausible risks, and consequences.
TRY: Rank one protective action by likely benefit and effort.
REFLECT: Which risk have I ignored because it is ordinary?
CHECK: What is a threat model?::A context-specific account of what needs protection, from whom or what, and which safeguards are proportionate.
MODEL: Assets~Name what matters.|Threats~Identify plausible harms.|Controls~Choose fitting protections.

## Protect accounts with distinct credentials
QUESTION: Would one compromised password expose several accounts?
CORE: Reusing a password links otherwise separate accounts. Use unique strong credentials, a trustworthy manager where suitable, and additional authentication when available. Protect recovery methods too; a locked account can be as disruptive as an exposed one. Follow current service guidance because authentication options and threats change.
CONNECT: Habit design can make secure behavior less effortful by reducing the need to invent and remember many passwords.
EXAMPLE: A reused password appears in a breach. Unique credentials limit the damage to one service, while a prepared recovery method helps restore access.
DO: Audit whether important accounts share credentials without writing passwords in this course.
TRY: Prioritize one account for stronger authentication and recovery review.
REFLECT: Which recovery route would fail when I need it?
CHECK: Why use distinct credentials?::A breach of one service should not automatically unlock unrelated accounts.
MODEL: Separate~Use unique credentials.|Strengthen~Add suitable authentication.|Recover~Check fallback access.

## Verify a message through a known channel
QUESTION: Is this request really from the person or service it claims to represent?
CORE: Deceptive messages can imitate branding, urgency, authority, or a familiar contact. Avoid relying only on appearance. Use a known address, app, or phone number to verify requests for money, codes, credentials, or unusual files. A real sender should tolerate reasonable independent checking. Report suspicious messages through available channels.
CONNECT: Propaganda and persuasion literacy become practical defenses against phishing and impersonation.
EXAMPLE: A message says your account will close unless you enter a code. You open the service through your saved route rather than the message's link.
DO: Compare the claims and requested action in two example messages.
TRY: Identify the independent channel you would use to verify each.
REFLECT: Which emotion is the message trying to make urgent?
CHECK: Why verify independently?::The request's branding and apparent sender can be imitated, while a known channel reduces reliance on the suspicious message.
MODEL: Pause~Do not follow the prompt.|Verify~Use a known route.|Report~Use the service's channel if suspicious.

## Keep software and backups usable
QUESTION: Could I restore important work after a device or account problem?
CORE: Updates can repair known weaknesses; backups reduce the damage from loss, failure, or malicious change. Prioritize important data and test whether a backup can actually be restored. Plan where recovery information is kept and who may need access. A backup that has never been checked may provide false confidence.
CONNECT: Recovery is part of risk management, not a sign that prevention has failed.
EXAMPLE: A writer keeps a separate copy of current drafts and periodically opens a restored file to confirm it is usable.
DO: Identify your most important files and how they are currently backed up.
TRY: Test recovery of one non-sensitive file using your existing system.
REFLECT: What would I discover only after losing the device?
CHECK: Why test a backup?::A stored copy is useful only if it can be found and restored when needed.
MODEL: Protect~Maintain software and access.|Copy~Back up important data.|Restore~Test the process.

## Understand privacy as a set of choices
QUESTION: Who can see, retain, and connect my data?
CORE: Privacy varies by service, device, relationship, and setting. Examine what information is collected, how it is shared, and whether the default fits your purpose. Encryption and anonymity tools have different roles and limits. Avoid promising perfect invisibility from a single app or tactic; focus on actual risks and informed settings.
CONNECT: Digital privacy and autonomy meet when a person understands the trade-offs in sharing information.
EXAMPLE: A location feature is useful for a trip but need not remain available to every app afterward. You review its permissions when the trip ends.
DO: Review permissions for one service or device feature you use.
TRY: Change one unnecessary sharing setting and check that the service still meets your needs.
REFLECT: Which data flow did I agree to without noticing?
CHECK: Why is privacy context-specific?::Different tools, audiences, data types, and purposes create different exposure and trade-offs.
MODEL: Data~Identify what is shared.|Audience~Know who receives it.|Choice~Set access to fit the purpose.

## Learn network and system basics defensively
QUESTION: What path does information take when I use a service?
CORE: Understand devices, networks, accounts, permissions, and the difference between public and private information at a conceptual level. This helps you ask better questions about protection and failure. Technical books include attack tools and outdated platform details; use them only in authorized training environments and update practice from current documentation. No general reading course grants permission to probe someone else's system.
CONNECT: Technical literacy makes security less mystical and improves collaboration with specialists.
EXAMPLE: Knowing that a shared account weakens individual accountability helps a team design separate access for staff.
DO: Draw a simple map of your own authorized service: device, network, account, stored data.
TRY: Identify one access or permission question for its administrator or current documentation.
REFLECT: What do I assume is “secure” because I cannot see the path?
CHECK: What is the purpose of defensive technical literacy?::To understand systems and risks well enough to protect and ask informed questions within authorized boundaries.
MODEL: Map~See components.|Access~Identify permissions.|Protect~Choose appropriate controls.

## Handle an incident without improvising secrecy
QUESTION: What would I do if an important account or device might be compromised?
CORE: Identify what happened, preserve relevant evidence, use the service's official recovery process, and notify people who may be affected when appropriate. Avoid clicking further suspicious prompts or trying unverified fixes under pressure. Organizations need defined roles and current procedures for response and recovery. The exact steps depend on the incident and service.
CONNECT: NIST's govern, identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover sequence expresses a full lifecycle; the course applies its broad logic without pretending to be an incident manual.
EXAMPLE: After noticing unauthorized account activity, a user goes through the service's official recovery channel and checks connected accounts rather than answering the suspicious sender.
DO: List the official recovery channels for one important account.
TRY: Write a short incident checklist covering evidence, access, affected people, and help.
REFLECT: What information would I need to recover without making the incident worse?
CHECK: Why plan response before an incident?::A known recovery route and roles reduce confusion and delay under pressure.
MODEL: Detect~Notice the anomaly.|Respond~Use official help and preserve facts.|Recover~Restore access and review controls.

## Distinguish ethical security training from intrusion
QUESTION: Do I have clear authority to test this system?
CORE: Security learning can use owned devices, purpose-built labs, and written authorization with scope. Real accounts and third-party systems remain outside that scope without permission. Methods from offensive manuals can cause harm or violate rules even when a learner claims educational intent. Focus course practice on defensive understanding and consent-based labs.
CONNECT: This is an applied lesson in ethics and boundaries: capability does not establish authority.
EXAMPLE: A student uses a controlled training environment to understand a vulnerability class and documents protective lessons; they do not test the same idea on a public service.
DO: Define owner, scope, allowed actions, and reporting path for a hypothetical training lab.
TRY: Identify what would make a planned test unauthorized.
REFLECT: Am I treating curiosity as permission?
CHECK: What makes security testing ethical?::Clear authorization, defined scope, controlled practice, and responsible reporting.
MODEL: Authority~Confirm permission.|Scope~Stay within agreed limits.|Learning~Extract defensive lessons.
