# Decisions, strategy, and systems
DESCRIPTION: Make choices with clear aims, coordinated actions, feedback, and attention to second-order effects.
BOOKS: B018 B026 B055 B061 B123 B133 B172 B188 B199 B269 B287 B291 B293

## Define the decision before using a model
QUESTION: What choice is actually before me?
CORE: Decision models are useful only after the options, timing, and stakes are clear. Name the decision owner and what is still negotiable. Some apparent decisions are information gaps or conflicts over aims. A framework should help compare options, not become a way to delay an uncomfortable commitment.
CONNECT: Reflective questioning opens possibilities; structured decision tools narrow them. Both need a clearly stated problem.
EXAMPLE: “Should I change careers?” becomes “Should I apply for this training program this year, given cost and current obligations?”
DO: Rewrite one broad dilemma as a decision with options and a date.
TRY: Identify missing information that could change the choice.
REFLECT: Am I avoiding the actual decision by debating a much larger one?
CHECK: What should be clear before choosing a decision model?::The actual choice, available options, stakes, owner, and timing.
MODEL: Frame~State the choice.|Inform~Name missing facts.|Decide~Set a reasonable decision point.

## Use opportunity cost and reversibility
QUESTION: What do I give up by choosing this option, and can I change course?
CORE: Every allocation of time, money, or attention excludes another use. Compare the best realistic alternative, not an imaginary perfect path. Reversibility matters: a small trial can generate information without locking in the full cost, while an irreversible commitment deserves more scrutiny. Include opportunity costs borne by others when they are affected.
CONNECT: Economics clarifies trade-offs; practical experimentation reduces the cost of learning about them.
EXAMPLE: Before paying for a long program, you try a short introductory project and compare it with the alternative use of that time and money.
DO: Name the best realistic alternative to one preferred choice.
TRY: Design a smaller reversible step that would produce useful information.
REFLECT: Which excluded commitment have I treated as if it costs nothing?
CHECK: What is opportunity cost?::The value of the best realistic alternative forgone when a choice uses a limited resource.
MODEL: Option~Describe the choice.|Alternative~Identify what it displaces.|Trial~Learn before committing where possible.

## Separate decision quality from outcome quality
QUESTION: Was the process sound given what was known at the time?
CORE: A good process can lead to a poor outcome because uncertainty remains. A poor process can be rewarded by luck. Review the information available, alternatives considered, values applied, and risk accepted before judging only by the result. Then use the outcome to update future decisions without rewriting what you could have known earlier.
CONNECT: This distinction connects behavioral bias, project review, business decisions, and personal learning.
EXAMPLE: You prepared carefully for an interview and did not receive an offer. The result matters, but it does not prove that every preparation choice was wrong.
DO: Review a past decision using the information actually available then.
TRY: Record one process strength, one process weakness, and one piece of new information from the outcome.
REFLECT: Am I granting luck too much credit or too much blame?
CHECK: Why separate decision quality from outcome?::Uncertainty can produce success from a poor process or failure from a sound one.
MODEL: Before~Inspect available information.|Choice~Evaluate process and risk.|After~Update from the result.

## Build a strategy kernel
QUESTION: What obstacle does my plan address, and how do its actions fit together?
CORE: A useful strategy identifies the central challenge, chooses an overall approach, and coordinates actions that implement it. A goal alone says what you want; it does not diagnose the obstacle or specify how to overcome it. Avoid adding many initiatives that compete for the same resources. Check whether each action supports the chosen approach.
CONNECT: This structure applies across work and personal projects, though the ethics and stakes change by domain.
EXAMPLE: A course completion problem is diagnosed as unclear practice time, not low ambition. The approach is to protect two sessions weekly, supported by calendar agreements and smaller lesson batches.
DO: Write diagnosis, guiding approach, and three coherent actions for one project.
TRY: Remove an action that does not serve the approach or conflicts with another.
REFLECT: Have I called a wish a strategy?
CHECK: What are the three parts of a strategy kernel?::A diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding approach, and coordinated actions that implement it.
MODEL: Diagnose~Find the real obstacle.|Guide~Choose an approach.|Coordinate~Align actions.

## Use feedback loops to see change over time
QUESTION: What effect might this action create that later feeds back into the situation?
CORE: A system contains interacting parts, delays, and feedback. A reinforcing loop amplifies a pattern; a balancing loop resists change. Map a few important relationships instead of drawing every possible arrow. Check timing: an intervention may appear ineffective before its effect becomes visible, or appear successful while creating a later cost.
CONNECT: Habit loops describe local repetition; systems thinking expands the view to relationships, organizations, and resource constraints.
EXAMPLE: Faster replies create an expectation of immediate availability, increasing interruptions and reducing time for substantive work. A new response agreement changes the loop.
DO: Draw a small loop for one recurring organizational or personal problem.
TRY: Identify one delay and one unintended response your intervention might trigger.
REFLECT: What pattern is being maintained by the current arrangement?
CHECK: Why include feedback and delays?::Actions can change later conditions and responses, so immediate effects may not represent the full result.
MODEL: Map~Identify key relationships.|Delay~Mark when effects appear.|Intervene~Choose a leverage point and review it.

## Beware local optimization
QUESTION: Could improving one measure make the whole situation worse?
CORE: A department, habit, or metric can improve while the larger purpose suffers. State the overall aim and the dependencies among parts. Ask who bears the cost of the local improvement and what information the measure omits. Do not treat more of a good thing as always better; beyond a point, it may consume resources needed elsewhere.
CONNECT: Systems thinking and ethics meet when a narrow performance measure hides transferred costs.
EXAMPLE: A team reduces average response time by sending brief answers, but users need follow-up clarifications. The local speed metric rises while total service worsens.
DO: Choose one metric and list two outcomes it fails to capture.
TRY: Add a balancing measure or qualitative check to the review.
REFLECT: Who pays for the improvement I am celebrating?
CHECK: What is local optimization?::Improving a part or metric in a way that may worsen the wider system or purpose.
MODEL: Metric~Name what is optimized.|System~Identify dependencies and costs.|Balance~Check the overall result.

## Use scenarios rather than one confident forecast
QUESTION: What would I do if different plausible futures occur?
CORE: A forecast is uncertain, especially when several systems interact. Build a few plausible scenarios around the variables that matter most, then identify robust actions and early signals. Scenarios are not predictions that each future will happen. They help expose assumptions and prepare responses without claiming control over events.
CONNECT: Strategic surprise literature and personal decision-making both benefit from considering more than one path while avoiding endless speculation.
EXAMPLE: You plan for stable income, a temporary reduction, and a major disruption. The scenarios point to different spending choices and a common need for clear records.
DO: Write three plausible scenarios for one consequential plan.
TRY: Identify one action useful across scenarios and one signal that would trigger revision.
REFLECT: Which future have I silently treated as guaranteed?
CHECK: What is the purpose of scenario planning?::To expose assumptions and prepare adaptable choices across plausible futures.
MODEL: Vary~Choose key uncertainties.|Imagine~Describe plausible futures.|Prepare~Define robust actions and signals.

## Make a decision reviewable
QUESTION: How will I know whether to continue, adjust, or stop?
CORE: Before acting, state the rationale, expected signals, review date, and conditions for changing course. This prevents hindsight from rewriting your reasons and protects against sunk-cost thinking. A review should allow for learning, including the possibility that the initial aim no longer matters. Do not use a single number as a verdict when qualitative consequences are important.
CONNECT: The complete decision cycle joins values, evidence, strategy, and systems. Later modules apply it to relationships, work, money, and safety.
EXAMPLE: You commit to a three-month learning project with a monthly review of skill demonstrations, time cost, and its effect on other commitments.
DO: Write a decision record for one upcoming choice.
TRY: Set a review date and two signals that would justify a change.
REFLECT: What evidence would I be reluctant to see after committing?
CHECK: What makes a decision reviewable?::A recorded rationale, relevant signals, a review point, and conditions for revising the choice.
MODEL: Record~State reasons and expectations.|Act~Follow the plan.|Review~Compare evidence and revise.
