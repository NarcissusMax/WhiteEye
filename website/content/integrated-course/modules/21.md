# Practical problem solving and experiments
DESCRIPTION: Define a real problem, test competing explanations, and choose changes that can be evaluated without pretending one framework solves everything.
BOOKS: B018 B133 B287 B291 B293 B188

## Describe the gap before naming a solution
QUESTION: What result differs from what is needed, and for whom?
CORE: A proposed fix can arrive before the problem is clear. Describe the current condition, the desired condition, who is affected, and when the gap appears. Include what still works. Avoid a definition that already smuggles in your preferred solution. A specific problem statement helps you choose evidence and prevents a tool, meeting, purchase, or rule from becoming the goal by default.
CONNECT: [[01 Self-knowledge and your starting point]] uses a baseline for personal change. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] uses a diagnosis for strategy. The same discipline applies to a team problem with more stakeholders.
EXAMPLE: 'We need a new app' is a solution. 'Volunteers miss changes to the rota because updates arrive through three channels' describes a gap that might have several fixes.
DO: Rewrite one proposed solution as a statement of current result, needed result, affected people, and context.
TRY: Ask one affected person whether your statement captures their experience and what it misses.
REFLECT: Which solution had I treated as if it were the problem itself?
CHECK: What belongs in a useful problem statement?::The current and desired conditions, affected people, context, and evidence of the gap, without assuming one fix.
MODEL: Describe~Name the observed gap.|Locate~Identify who and when.|Check~Confirm the description before solving.

## Set the boundary of the problem
QUESTION: Which connected factors belong in this attempt, and which must be noted but handled elsewhere?
CORE: A problem can expand until it includes an entire life or institution. Draw a boundary around the process you can study and change now. Record inputs, outputs, people affected, and links to larger systems. A narrow boundary can make an experiment feasible, but a boundary that excludes crucial effects will mislead. State what you are leaving outside and why; revisit it if the intervention causes effects there.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] treats system boundaries as choices. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] asks whether the boundary hides costs borne by someone else.
EXAMPLE: A school studies late homework submissions. It can examine its assignment process while noting that home access to devices and care obligations also affect students. A school-only fix should not pretend those conditions vanish.
DO: Draw a simple boundary around a recurring problem, with three factors inside and two connected factors outside.
TRY: Ask who might bear a cost if the outside factors are ignored.
REFLECT: Did the boundary make action possible while still keeping important consequences visible?
CHECK: Why make a problem boundary explicit?::It defines a feasible investigation while exposing connected factors and costs that the chosen scope may omit.
MODEL: Bound~Choose a workable process.|Connect~Mark outside influences.|Revisit~Expand the view if results demand it.

## Separate symptoms from candidate causes
QUESTION: What might produce the visible problem, and what evidence would distinguish explanations?
CORE: A symptom is what you observe; a cause is an explanation of why it occurs. Generate at least two plausible explanations before selecting one. Look for timing, exceptions, and points in the process where the outcome changes. Avoid blaming the nearest visible person when the system may set them up to fail. A causal hypothesis should imply a testable difference: if it is true, what else would you expect to see?
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] clarifies confounding and intervention. [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] reminds you that incentives and role design can produce repeated individual errors.
EXAMPLE: Orders arrive late. One explanation is slow packing; another is late information from sales. Timestamps at each handoff can distinguish where delay accumulates.
DO: For one problem, list two candidate causes and one observation each would predict.
TRY: Find a case where the symptom did not occur and compare its conditions with a typical case.
REFLECT: Which explanation felt satisfying before I had checked it?
CHECK: How is a cause hypothesis different from a symptom description?::It proposes a mechanism that should predict additional observations or responses to change.
MODEL: Observe~Describe the symptom.|Hypothesize~List competing causes.|Discriminate~Choose evidence that separates them.

## Break a complex problem into linked parts
QUESTION: Which smaller questions must be answered before the whole problem can improve?
CORE: Decomposition makes a complex challenge workable, but dividing it badly can hide the connections that matter. Separate stages, decisions, resources, and failure points, then draw how they affect one another. Choose a part that can be tested while keeping a measure of the whole outcome. If improving the part does not improve the whole, the original division or assumed link may be wrong.
CONNECT: [[12.06 Beware local optimization]] warns about optimizing one part at the system's expense. [[20 Strategy, competition, and cooperation]] asks which constraint is decisive rather than merely easy to see.
EXAMPLE: A community event has low attendance. Possible parts include awareness, location, timing, cost, and relevance. Posting more advertisements tests only awareness; the overall attendance measure shows whether that part mattered.
DO: Split one problem into three to five linked components and mark a plausible bottleneck.
TRY: Select one component to test and one whole-outcome measure to protect.
REFLECT: Did my chosen part have a credible route to the result I cared about?
CHECK: What is the risk of decomposition?::A part can improve while the whole problem remains or worsens if important connections are ignored.
MODEL: Divide~Identify components.|Link~Show how they affect the outcome.|Test~Watch both the part and the whole.

## Compare fixes against the same criteria
QUESTION: How can I compare attractive solutions fairly?
CORE: Generate several feasible options before committing to one. Judge each against criteria chosen from the problem: likely effect, cost, reversibility, time, people affected, and uncertainty. Do not let a precise score disguise weak evidence. A small pilot may be better than a full commitment when the leading options are uncertain. Record why you chose one route so later review can improve your judgment.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] offers opportunity cost and reversibility. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] adds distribution and consent. A solution is not good simply because it is clever.
EXAMPLE: A group can hire an administrator, simplify a form, or reduce the number of approvals. The best first test may be the simpler form if evidence suggests the form causes most delays and the change is easy to reverse.
DO: List three feasible responses to one problem and five criteria tied to the real outcome.
TRY: Compare options in plain words, including what you do not know and who bears each cost.
REFLECT: Which criterion did my favorite solution encourage me to overlook?
CHECK: Why choose criteria before comparing fixes?::It reduces the chance of changing the standard to favor the first solution you liked.
MODEL: Generate~List distinct options.|Compare~Apply shared criteria and uncertainty.|Choose~Record the reason and review point.

## Design a small test that could fail informatively
QUESTION: What can I try that will teach me something even if it does not work?
CORE: A useful pilot has a clear change, a relevant measure, a time boundary, and a plausible comparison. State what result would support the idea and what would weaken it. Keep the test proportionate to risk and avoid experimenting on people without appropriate consent or oversight. A pilot is not proof from one success; it is a way to learn before committing more resources.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] explains why comparison matters. [[40 Integration and the life-practice capstone]] applies the same design to a bounded personal project.
EXAMPLE: A library tests a new reminder message for one month and compares return rates with a similar period, while noting seasonal differences. The result may justify a longer trial rather than a sweeping conclusion.
DO: Write a one-page pilot brief: change, group or setting, measure, duration, and stop condition.
TRY: State one result that would make you revise the explanation rather than simply try harder.
REFLECT: Would the test teach me something if the outcome disappointed me?
CHECK: What makes a pilot informative?::A defined change, relevant observation, comparison where possible, time boundary, and result that could challenge the hypothesis.
MODEL: Specify~Define the intervention.|Observe~Measure relevant effects.|Learn~Use both success and failure to revise.

## Watch for side effects and displaced costs
QUESTION: Did the fix improve the target while moving the burden somewhere else?
CORE: A change can make a visible metric better by shifting work, risk, or delay to another person or stage. Before and after a pilot, identify who does more work, who waits longer, and which errors become less visible. Ask affected people about consequences the original measure cannot capture. Side effects do not always make the change unacceptable, but they belong in the decision.
CONNECT: [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] makes hidden labor part of accountability. [[26 Economics, incentives, and public choices]] asks about external costs and distribution. A good local result is not the whole result.
EXAMPLE: A team reduces call time by asking customers to complete a longer form first. Staff workload falls, but customers with limited access may abandon the service. Both effects belong in the review.
DO: For one proposed fix, list three people or stages that might receive displaced work or risk.
TRY: Add one side-effect measure and one question for affected people to your review.
REFLECT: Whose experience was missing from my original success criterion?
CHECK: What is a displaced cost?::A burden moved to another person, stage, or time while the chosen measure appears to improve.
MODEL: Anticipate~List possible recipients of burden.|Observe~Track side effects.|Decide~Judge the whole outcome.

## Decide whether to continue, change, or stop
QUESTION: What should the result of a trial cause me to do next?
CORE: Review the pilot against the problem statement and criteria, not only against enthusiasm for the team that ran it. Separate implementation failure from a weak idea: did the change actually happen as intended, and did it affect the outcome? Decide to continue, revise, or stop with a reason and next review date. If evidence is too thin, say so and design the next test. A stopped idea can still be a useful result when it prevents a larger mistake.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] guards against hindsight and sunk costs. [[23 Creative work and sustained projects]] carries the lesson into revision and release.
EXAMPLE: A new intake process saves staff time but confuses users. The team can revise the instructions and run a second pilot rather than declaring total success or abandoning the entire approach.
DO: Make a three-column review for one trial: intended change, observed implementation, and observed outcome.
TRY: Choose continue, revise, or stop, and state the evidence that would change that decision later.
REFLECT: Did I learn from the trial or defend the effort already spent?
CHECK: What two questions belong in a pilot review?::Whether the change was implemented as intended and whether it improved the relevant outcome without unacceptable side effects.
MODEL: Verify~Check implementation.|Evaluate~Compare outcome and costs.|Decide~Continue, revise, or stop.
