# Economics, incentives, and public choices
DESCRIPTION: Understand trade-offs, markets, incentives, and uncertainty without mistaking an economic model for a complete moral answer.
BOOKS: B002 B068 B123 B147 B170 B188 B195 B201 B255 B268 B269 B274 B284 B287

## Begin with scarcity and trade-offs
QUESTION: What limited resource is being allocated?
CORE: Time, money, labor, land, and attention have competing uses. An economic analysis identifies alternatives and the costs of choosing among them. Scarcity does not mean every valuable thing has a market price, and economic efficiency does not by itself decide what is fair. State whose costs and benefits count.
CONNECT: Personal opportunity cost scales into institutional and public decisions, where affected groups may have unequal power.
EXAMPLE: A city uses a site for parking, housing, or a park. Each choice creates different benefits and exclusions.
DO: Map three uses of one scarce resource and their opportunity costs.
TRY: Identify a person or group missing from the first comparison.
REFLECT: Whose cost becomes invisible when I use one headline number?
CHECK: What is the economic starting point of a choice?::Limited resources have alternative uses, each with costs and benefits.
MODEL: Resource~Name the scarce input.|Alternatives~List realistic uses.|Distribution~Identify who gains and loses.

## Compare marginal changes
QUESTION: What would one additional unit change?
CORE: Many decisions concern an increment rather than all-or-nothing adoption. Compare the added benefit and added cost of the next unit while noticing thresholds and fixed costs. Average results can conceal the decision at the margin. Marginal thinking does not mean every value can be reduced to a precise number; it clarifies the next step.
CONNECT: Habit and systems thinking also ask whether more of a good action remains useful as conditions change.
EXAMPLE: The first hour of extra study may help greatly; a fifth late-night hour may reduce sleep and next-day performance.
DO: For one repeated activity, list the likely benefits and costs of one more unit.
TRY: Identify a threshold beyond which the answer might change.
REFLECT: Am I using a favorable average to justify an unfavorable next increment?
CHECK: What does marginal analysis examine?::The added benefit and added cost of a small change from the current position.
MODEL: Current~Describe the baseline.|Increment~Add one unit.|Compare~Assess new benefit and cost.

## Understand incentives without reducing people to them
QUESTION: What behavior does this rule make easier or more rewarding?
CORE: Prices, rewards, penalties, defaults, and social expectations can change behavior. The response depends on context, information, and values; people are not simple machines. Examine unintended incentives as well as stated aims. A rule can meet a target while shifting costs to someone else.
CONNECT: Institutional leadership and economics both need to look at actual behavior after a rule changes.
EXAMPLE: A school rewards only test scores; teachers may spend less time on difficult but valuable learning that the test misses.
DO: Identify the incentive created by one policy or target.
TRY: List two behaviors it might encourage beyond the intended one.
REFLECT: Which behavior am I surprised by even though the rules reward it?
CHECK: Why study incentives?::Rules can change actual choices, including unintended responses and shifted costs.
MODEL: Rule~Name the incentive.|Response~Consider plausible behavior.|Effect~Check intended and unintended results.

## See how exchange can create value
QUESTION: Why might both sides prefer a voluntary exchange?
CORE: People value goods, time, and skills differently. Exchange can help each obtain something they value more, given their alternatives. This does not mean every observed exchange is fair or fully informed; unequal information, coercion, and external costs matter. Compare the real terms and the option to decline.
CONNECT: Negotiation reveals possible gains from trade; ethics asks whether the conditions respect all participants.
EXAMPLE: One person has spare editing time and needs web help; another has the opposite needs. An explicit exchange can benefit both if scope and expectations are clear.
DO: Explain a voluntary exchange from both parties' points of view.
TRY: Identify a hidden cost or power difference that could undermine the apparent gain.
REFLECT: Am I assuming consent simply because a transaction occurred?
CHECK: When can exchange create value?::When parties value alternatives differently and can choose on informed, acceptable terms.
MODEL: Differences~Find distinct needs.|Terms~Clarify the exchange.|Check~Assess information and power.

## Read prices as signals with limits
QUESTION: What does a price reveal, and what does it conceal?
CORE: A price can coordinate decisions about scarcity and demand, but it does not capture every social, environmental, or personal cost. Changes can reflect supply, demand, market power, rules, or expectations. Do not infer a single cause from a rising number. Compare quantities, alternatives, and relevant context.
CONNECT: Causal reasoning prevents a price signal from becoming a total explanation; systems thinking widens the view to external effects.
EXAMPLE: A food price rises after both a supply disruption and a change in transport cost. Blaming only one factor would miss the combined effect.
DO: For one price change, list at least three plausible contributors.
TRY: Name one important cost or benefit that the market price may omit.
REFLECT: What story am I attaching to the number without evidence?
CHECK: What is the limit of a price signal?::It reflects some market conditions but does not reveal every cause or external cost by itself.
MODEL: Signal~Observe the change.|Causes~Compare explanations.|External~Look for omitted effects.

## Use simple accounting for public and business claims
QUESTION: Where do resources enter, move, and leave?
CORE: Public and organizational debates can use impressive totals without showing flows, timing, or who bears the obligation. Ask for a balance of inputs, outputs, assets, liabilities, and opportunity costs at the appropriate level. Accounting does not settle a moral choice, but it can reveal whether a proposal's resources are real and whether costs are deferred or transferred.
CONNECT: The money module's record-keeping scales into policy and business evaluation.
EXAMPLE: A program is called cheap because its upfront cost is low, while ongoing maintenance falls on local teams. The full resource map changes the debate.
DO: Trace the main flows in one simple public or organizational proposal.
TRY: Identify an off-budget or delayed cost that should be discussed.
REFLECT: Which cost has disappeared because it is paid later or by someone else?
CHECK: What can accounting add to public reasoning?::A clearer view of resource flows and obligations, while leaving value judgments explicit.
MODEL: Inflows~Identify resources.|Outflows~Track uses and costs.|Obligations~Mark future commitments.

## Interpret averages, risk, and distribution
QUESTION: Who does the headline number describe?
CORE: An average may conceal variation, outliers, or unequal effects. Examine the spread and the groups being combined. A risk estimate also needs a time period and relevant population. Do not invent precision where data are weak. Historical statistics, including sport records, can teach denominator awareness but need context before supporting a general claim.
CONNECT: Statistics and ethics meet when a policy improves an average while harming a vulnerable group.
EXAMPLE: Average income rises while most households see little change and a small group gains greatly. The average alone gives an incomplete picture.
DO: Find a headline average and ask for median, range, and subgroup information.
TRY: Explain how the conclusion changes under a different denominator.
REFLECT: Which group is lost inside the aggregate?
CHECK: Why inspect distribution?::An average can hide who experiences gains, losses, or exceptional outcomes.
MODEL: Average~Read the headline.|Spread~Inspect variation.|Group~Check who is represented.

## Read national economic stories as hypotheses
QUESTION: Which evidence supports this account of growth or crisis?
CORE: National economic narratives combine policies, demographics, institutions, external shocks, and historical sequence. A single-country story does not prove a policy will transfer elsewhere. Compare alternative explanations, timing, and differences between countries. Recognize that older books reflect the data and debates available when they were written.
CONNECT: Japan's economic case studies and political history are useful when treated as situated analysis rather than a universal recipe.
EXAMPLE: A slowdown is attributed to monetary policy alone. You also examine productivity, population, external demand, and institutional responses.
DO: Take one economic narrative and list its proposed causes and missing alternatives.
TRY: Identify one condition that would limit transfer to another country.
REFLECT: Which country analogy am I using as if contexts were identical?
CHECK: Why be cautious with national analogies?::Outcomes arise from interacting conditions, and an explanation may not transfer unchanged to another setting.
MODEL: Story~State the account.|Alternatives~Map other causes.|Transfer~Check contextual differences.
