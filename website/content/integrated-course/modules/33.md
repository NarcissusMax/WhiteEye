## Ask what kind of question you are asking
QUESTION: Is this question empirical, conceptual, ethical, or existential?
CORE: Questions about measurement, the meaning of a term, what is right, and how to live require different kinds of reasoning. They can interact, but a result in one category may not settle another. State the question precisely and identify what would count as a relevant answer. This prevents a scientific finding from being treated as a complete moral argument or a personal conviction from being presented as a measurement.
CONNECT: The course has used these distinctions throughout; philosophy makes the underlying method explicit.
EXAMPLE: Whether a practice lowers stress is empirical; whether a life should be organized around calm is a value question.
DO: Classify four questions from your own life by kind and explain your choice.
TRY: Identify one question that contains two kinds and separate them.
REFLECT: Which method have I used on a question it cannot answer alone?
CHECK: Why classify a question's kind?::Different questions need different evidence, concepts, and standards of argument.
MODEL: Question~State it clearly.|Kind~Identify the inquiry.|Method~Choose fitting reasoning.

## Examine how knowledge is possible
QUESTION: What must I assume in order to claim I know this?
CORE: Knowledge involves more than confidence. Ask how the belief was formed, what justifies it, whether the source is reliable, and what could correct it. Philosophers disagree on the exact account. For practical purposes, make your reasons visible and separate direct experience from inference and testimony. Radical doubt can reveal assumptions, but it cannot be the only activity of a life that requires action.
CONNECT: Kantian questions about the conditions of experience and scientific questions about evidence are related without being identical.
EXAMPLE: You know a meeting happened from direct participation; you know a distant event through reports whose sources and methods need examination.
DO: Trace one belief to direct observation, inference, or testimony.
TRY: Identify a condition under which you would revise it.
REFLECT: Where have I mistaken certainty of feeling for strength of justification?
CHECK: What should a knowledge claim make visible?::Its source, supporting reasons, limits, and possible correction.
MODEL: Source~How was the belief formed?|Reason~What supports it?|Revision~What would change it?

## Distinguish appearance, concept, and thing
QUESTION: Am I treating my way of organizing experience as the whole of reality?
CORE: Perception gives a perspective shaped by attention and concepts. We classify what we encounter, and classifications help while leaving features out. This does not imply that nothing is real or that every interpretation is equally good. Compare perspectives, test consequences, and ask which categories serve the question. Difficult philosophical accounts of experience should be studied as arguments, not reduced to slogans.
CONNECT: Ontology and epistemology meet where a useful category is mistaken for a complete description of an individual or event.
EXAMPLE: Calling someone “unreliable” may summarize several missed promises, but it can hide the differing conditions and possible changes.
DO: Choose one category you use often and list what it captures and omits.
TRY: Describe the same case from another relevant perspective.
REFLECT: What have I treated as the thing itself because my label feels precise?
CHECK: What is a limit of a concept?::It selects features for a purpose and may omit other relevant aspects of the thing described.
MODEL: Perceive~Notice experience.|Classify~Choose a concept.|Check~Test omissions and alternatives.

## Compare freedom and social obligation
QUESTION: What kind of freedom is at stake, and whose obligations support it?
CORE: Freedom can mean lack of interference, capacity to act, participation in collective decisions, or self-direction. These meanings can conflict. A rule may limit one choice while protecting another person's security; a formal right may be unusable without resources. State the conception of freedom and the affected people before debating whether a policy increases it.
CONNECT: Aristotle, Rousseau, modern political thought, and self-help speak of agency in different contexts. Their differences matter.
EXAMPLE: A workplace gives flexible hours but no childcare support. Formal flexibility may not create equal practical freedom for every worker.
DO: Describe one policy through two meanings of freedom.
TRY: Identify the obligation or resource each meaning requires.
REFLECT: Which person's freedom is absent from my first account?
CHECK: Why define freedom in an argument?::Different meanings can support different judgments about the same rule or institution.
MODEL: Meaning~Specify the freedom.|People~Map affected choices.|Conditions~Identify supporting resources and limits.

## Study a philosophical tradition in context
QUESTION: What problem was this tradition responding to?
CORE: Philosophical traditions emerge in particular histories and use concepts with meanings that change in translation. Read a claim with its question, opponents, and intended practice. Avoid extracting a line from Stoicism, Buddhism, or samurai ethics and making it a universal productivity rule. Compare how a tradition explains a problem and the costs of adopting its view.
CONNECT: Historical context preserves real disagreements across the collection instead of smoothing them into a motivational blend.
EXAMPLE: A Stoic distinction about judgment may help a modern decision, but it does not automatically settle a debate about unjust institutions.
DO: Choose one tradition and identify its central question, proposed answer, and historical setting.
TRY: Explain one useful transfer and one limit of that transfer.
REFLECT: Which appealing phrase have I detached from the argument it belonged to?
CHECK: Why read traditions in context?::Their claims address specific questions and assumptions that may differ from present concerns.
MODEL: Context~Locate the tradition.|Argument~Understand the reason.|Transfer~Check fit and limits.

## Use thought experiments to expose commitments
QUESTION: What does this imagined case reveal about my principle?
CORE: A thought experiment simplifies a situation so a principle can be tested. Specify which feature the case isolates and which real-world details it removes. Your reaction can reveal an intuition, but an intuition is not the final argument. Change one feature at a time and see whether your judgment shifts for a reason you can explain.
CONNECT: Ethical reasoning, logic, and philosophy of mind use imagined cases to clarify concepts before confronting more complicated evidence.
EXAMPLE: A promise-keeping case becomes harder when keeping the promise would cause serious harm. The change reveals whether you think the duty is absolute.
DO: Create a simple case testing one principle you hold.
TRY: Change one relevant feature and explain any change in your judgment.
REFLECT: Which detail did I leave out that real decisions would require?
CHECK: What is a thought experiment for?::To isolate and test a concept or principle, while recognizing the simplification's limits.
MODEL: Principle~State the rule.|Case~Isolate a feature.|Variation~Test the boundary.

## Compare pessimism, hope, and meaning
QUESTION: What picture of human life does this worldview offer?
CORE: Some texts emphasize suffering, alienation, or the instability of achievement. Others emphasize purpose, progress, faith, or creative action. Ask what evidence and values underlie each picture, what it sees clearly, and what it neglects. A worldview should not be accepted merely because it is comforting or rejected because it is difficult. It also should not be used to diagnose another person's life from afar.
CONNECT: Literature, existential memoir, religious traditions, and social critique offer different ways to understand a good life.
EXAMPLE: A critique of consumer status helps you question a purchase, while a story about meaningful work may help you choose what to build instead.
DO: Compare two worldviews by human problem, proposed response, and neglected condition.
TRY: Identify a lived case that complicates each.
REFLECT: Which worldview gives me clarity, and which gives me an excuse?
CHECK: How should worldviews be compared?::By their questions, reasons, explanatory strengths, omissions, and practical consequences.
MODEL: Problem~Name what it sees.|Response~Understand its proposal.|Limit~Find what it misses.

## Form a provisional philosophy of practice
QUESTION: Which principles will guide action while remaining open to challenge?
CORE: Choose a small set of principles you can explain and test: how you seek truth, treat others, use effort, and respond to uncertainty. Identify tensions among them rather than pretending they are perfectly consistent. A practical philosophy should shape actual commitments and allow revision when it harms people or fails to explain experience.
CONNECT: The next modules on religion, science, and literature will widen the material from which this provisional account can learn.
EXAMPLE: You commit to intellectual honesty and care, then notice a case where blunt disclosure would cause avoidable harm. The tension calls for judgment about timing and form, not abandonment of either principle.
DO: Write four provisional principles and one action each would change.
TRY: Identify one conflict between two principles and how you would deliberate.
REFLECT: What would make me revise a principle rather than defend it for identity's sake?
CHECK: What makes a practical philosophy provisional?::It guides action through explicit reasons while remaining open to evidence, conflict, and revision.
MODEL: State~Choose principles.|Apply~Use them in decisions.|Revise~Learn from tensions and consequences.
