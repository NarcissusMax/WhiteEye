# Influence, persuasion, and negotiation
DESCRIPTION: Make proposals people can evaluate freely, prepare trade-offs, and reach clear agreements.
BOOKS: B006 B063 B113 B126 B130 B151 B185 B190 B213 B239 B268 B306 B308 B311

## Define ethical influence
QUESTION: What would help someone make an informed choice about my proposal?
CORE: Influence is part of ordinary cooperation: we explain, recommend, request, and negotiate. Its ethical quality depends on honesty, respect for autonomy, and the consequences for everyone affected. Present reasons and relevant limits, invite questions, and permit refusal. A successful agreement obtained through confusion or fear may fail the relationship and the task.
CONNECT: Persuasion manuals often measure the immediate yes; relationship and ethics frameworks ask whether the choice was informed and sustainable.
EXAMPLE: You propose a new workflow, explain its expected benefit and setup cost, and offer a small trial with a review date.
DO: Rewrite one persuasive pitch as a proposal with benefit, cost, uncertainty, and choice.
TRY: Ask what evidence the other person would need to evaluate it.
REFLECT: Would I still endorse this method if its effect on the recipient were visible?
CHECK: What makes influence ethical?::Truthful information, room for informed choice, and attention to consequences for the affected people.
MODEL: Explain~State the proposal.|Inform~Show costs and uncertainty.|Choose~Respect the response.

## Prepare interests and alternatives
QUESTION: What matters to each side, and what happens without agreement?
CORE: Before negotiating, identify your aims, constraints, and best realistic alternative. Ask what the other side might value without assuming you know. An alternative helps you evaluate a proposal without pretending a deal is necessary at any price. Include relationships and implementation costs as well as money. Do not invent a threat to create leverage.
CONNECT: Opportunity cost gives negotiation a clear baseline. Listening reveals where interests may be aligned or traded.
EXAMPLE: Before discussing a project deadline, you map your available capacity, the client's launch needs, and a smaller scope that would fit both.
DO: Write your interests, limits, and realistic alternative for one negotiation.
TRY: List questions that would reveal the other party's priorities.
REFLECT: Which assumption about their interests is still untested?
CHECK: Why identify an alternative to agreement?::It provides a realistic comparison for evaluating offers and limits.
MODEL: Prepare~Know your needs and alternative.|Ask~Learn their interests.|Compare~Judge possible agreements.

## Listen before proposing a solution
QUESTION: What problem is the other person actually trying to solve?
CORE: A polished pitch can miss the need if it arrives before inquiry. Ask about the desired outcome, obstacles, previous attempts, and decision criteria. Summarize your understanding and accept correction. Use only information the person chooses to share for the stated purpose; curiosity is not permission to exploit a vulnerability.
CONNECT: Sales, coaching, and negotiation all benefit from discovery, but the ethical limit is whether inquiry serves the person's informed decision.
EXAMPLE: A client asks for more features. You learn that the underlying concern is delayed reporting, which a simpler change could address.
DO: Create four questions that reveal purpose, constraint, alternatives, and success criteria.
TRY: Summarize the answers before giving your recommendation.
REFLECT: Was I learning what helps or gathering points of pressure?
CHECK: Why inquire before pitching?::The proposed solution should address the person's actual aim and constraints.
MODEL: Ask~Explore the need.|Confirm~Check understanding.|Propose~Match the solution.

## Make value concrete
QUESTION: What useful difference would this proposal create?
CORE: A benefit becomes meaningful when it connects to a specific need and can be compared with its cost. Avoid vague adjectives such as transformative or essential without evidence. Describe what changes, for whom, under what conditions, and how you would know. Be candid about uncertainty and about people for whom the offer is a poor fit.
CONNECT: Business value, practical service, and evidence-based explanation share the need to connect a promise to a real outcome.
EXAMPLE: “This template reduces duplicate entry for the three weekly reports” is more evaluable than “This tool revolutionizes productivity.”
DO: State the mechanism and relevant cost of one offer.
TRY: Identify a case where you would advise against the offer.
REFLECT: What would an informed buyer or partner need to know that my favorite pitch omits?
CHECK: What makes a value claim evaluable?::It specifies who benefits, what changes, under which conditions, and at what cost.
MODEL: Need~Identify the problem.|Change~Describe the expected effect.|Cost~Show the trade-off.

## Use empathy without pretending to agree
QUESTION: Can I recognize a concern while maintaining my position?
CORE: Acknowledging another person's feeling or constraint can make a conversation more accurate. It does not require endorsing every conclusion they draw. Label the concern tentatively, invite correction, and state your own relevant facts. Avoid empathy as a theatrical device to induce a yes; use it to understand what a workable agreement must address.
CONNECT: Tactical empathy in negotiation overlaps with genuine listening, though its strategic framing can create tension. Judge it by accuracy and respect.
EXAMPLE: “It sounds like the deadline feels risky because the last handoff slipped. Is that right?” opens discussion of reliability without promising an impossible date.
DO: Draft one tentative acknowledgment and one honest statement of your constraint.
TRY: Use both in a low-stakes negotiation and invite correction.
REFLECT: Would I care about the concern if it did not help me win?
CHECK: What does empathic acknowledgment establish?::Understanding of a concern, not automatic agreement with every requested outcome.
MODEL: Notice~Identify the concern.|Check~Invite correction.|Respond~State the truthful constraint.

## Generate options before dividing a fixed pie
QUESTION: Can a different arrangement meet more of each side's interests?
CORE: Some negotiations are distributive: one side receives more of a limited item. Others allow changes in timing, scope, risk, information, or responsibility that create value. Explore these dimensions before assuming every gain is another person's loss. Do not force a creative solution when the central conflict remains a real incompatibility.
CONNECT: Systems thinking reveals dependencies; negotiation turns them into explicit options and trade-offs.
EXAMPLE: A client cannot meet the price for the full package but values the core service. A narrower scope at a sustainable price may serve both sides.
DO: List three adjustable dimensions in one negotiation besides headline price.
TRY: Develop two options and state what each side would gain and give up.
REFLECT: Am I avoiding a hard limit by endlessly inventing options?
CHECK: When can negotiation create value?::When different priorities allow changes in scope, timing, risk, or other terms that improve the fit for both sides.
MODEL: Interests~Identify priorities.|Options~Vary the arrangement.|Trade~State costs and benefits.

## Handle an objection as information
QUESTION: What part of the proposal does this objection challenge?
CORE: An objection may reveal a missing fact, a real cost, a past injury, a different priority, or a decision already made. Ask a clarifying question and answer the actual concern. Avoid treating every no as a hidden yes that must be overcome. Sometimes the correct outcome is a revised proposal or respectful withdrawal.
CONNECT: Sales scripts may encourage looping through objections; good negotiation asks whether new information changes the offer or the decision.
EXAMPLE: A colleague objects that a process adds work to their team. You map the workload and discover a needed handoff that your first plan omitted.
DO: Categorize an objection by evidence, cost, priority, trust, or clear refusal.
TRY: Draft a response that addresses it without repeating the original pitch.
REFLECT: What would make me accept that the other person's no is final?
CHECK: What can an objection reveal?::Information about facts, costs, priorities, trust, or a choice that needs to be respected.
MODEL: Hear~Take the objection seriously.|Clarify~Find its basis.|Respond~Revise, answer, or stop.

## Close with terms that can be delivered
QUESTION: What exactly are we agreeing to do?
CORE: A good close names the deliverable, owner, timing, cost, conditions, and review or exit process. Invite the other person to correct misunderstandings. Agreement obtained in a warm conversation can still fail if terms remain ambiguous. Record the decision at an appropriate level of formality, and follow through on what you promised.
CONNECT: Negotiation ends where reliability begins. Trust is supported by execution and honest updates, not only persuasive skill.
EXAMPLE: Both sides agree to a two-week trial, one reporting measure, and a date to decide whether to continue.
DO: Turn one tentative agreement into clear terms and dependencies.
TRY: Ask the other party to confirm or correct the summary.
REFLECT: Which unclear term might create conflict later?
CHECK: What makes an agreement implementable?::Defined actions, owners, timing, conditions, and a way to review or change it.
MODEL: Specify~Write the terms.|Confirm~Check mutual understanding.|Deliver~Follow through and review.
