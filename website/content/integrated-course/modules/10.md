# Logic, arguments, and fair disagreement
DESCRIPTION: Reconstruct arguments, test what follows, and recognize reasoning errors without using labels as substitutes for understanding.
BOOKS: B001 B014 B019 B027 B033 B059 B079 B188 B233

## Find the claim and its support
QUESTION: What is this person asking me to believe, and why?
CORE: An argument contains a conclusion and reasons offered for it. In conversation, both may be implicit. State the conclusion in a fair form and identify the strongest available support before evaluating it. A vivid example, an instruction, and an insult are not automatically reasons for the conclusion. Avoid attacking a weaker version because it is easier to defeat.
CONNECT: Socratic questioning and formal logic begin with accurate reconstruction. Fairness makes disagreement more informative, not less rigorous.
EXAMPLE: A colleague proposes changing the meeting time because late sessions exclude caregivers. The claim concerns scheduling, and the reason concerns participation; it is not simply a dislike of meetings.
DO: Take one disputed statement and write its conclusion and supporting reasons separately.
TRY: Ask the speaker whether your reconstruction is fair.
REFLECT: Did I answer the claim they made or an easier one I supplied?
CHECK: What must happen before evaluating an argument?::Its conclusion and supporting reasons should be reconstructed fairly.
MODEL: Claim~State the conclusion.|Support~List the reasons.|Confirm~Check the reconstruction.

## Distinguish validity from truth
QUESTION: Would the conclusion follow if the premises were true?
CORE: Validity concerns the relationship between premises and conclusion. Truth concerns whether the premises accurately describe the world. A valid argument can begin from a false premise; a true conclusion can arise from a poor argument. Test the structure by asking whether you can imagine the premises true and the conclusion false. Then check the premises separately.
CONNECT: Logical rigor and evidence review solve different parts of the problem. Neither can substitute for the other.
EXAMPLE: All birds can fly; penguins are birds; therefore penguins can fly has a valid form but a false first premise.
DO: Separate the structure and factual premises of one short argument.
TRY: Construct a counterexample if you think the conclusion does not follow.
REFLECT: Have I accepted a conclusion only because it happens to be true?
CHECK: What does validity test?::Whether the conclusion follows from the premises if those premises are assumed true.
MODEL: Form~Test the inference.|Premises~Check the facts.|Conclusion~Judge both together.

## Work carefully with if and only if
QUESTION: Does this condition guarantee the result, or is it merely required?
CORE: If A then B says A is sufficient for B, not that B proves A. A necessary condition is something the result cannot occur without. Everyday speech blurs these relationships. Write the direction explicitly and test the reversed claim with an example. This prevents inferring a single cause from an observed result.
CONNECT: Clear conditionals support causal reasoning, eligibility decisions, and honest promises.
EXAMPLE: If the alarm is triggered, the light turns on. Seeing the light does not prove the alarm was triggered; a manual switch might also turn it on.
DO: Rewrite a real rule as an if-then statement.
TRY: Test its converse and identify another route to the same result.
REFLECT: Which result have I treated as proof of one particular cause?
CHECK: Does “if A then B” imply “if B then A”?::No. B may have other sufficient causes.
MODEL: Direction~Write A leads to B.|Converse~Reverse it explicitly.|Counterexample~Find another route to B.

## Interpret and, or, not, and all
QUESTION: What exactly does the statement include or exclude?
CORE: Small logical words can change a claim greatly. And requires both parts. Or can allow one or both unless the context specifies an exclusive choice. Not changes the scope of what follows. All claims cover every member of a specified group, while some claims require at least one. Translate a vague sentence into examples before arguing over it.
CONNECT: Grammar and logic work together: an ambiguous sentence can produce an apparent disagreement even when people mean the same thing.
EXAMPLE: “Not every proposal failed” means at least one did not fail; it does not mean every proposal succeeded.
DO: Choose three ambiguous statements and rewrite their exact logical meaning.
TRY: Find a case that would make each statement false.
REFLECT: Which small word changed the dispute?
CHECK: What does “not all” mean?::At least one member of the group does not have the stated property.
MODEL: Parse~Identify scope and connectives.|Example~Test a concrete case.|Revise~State the intended claim.

## Test an argument with a counterexample
QUESTION: Can the reasons be true while the conclusion fails?
CORE: A counterexample challenges an inference by showing a possible case in which the stated reasons hold and the conclusion does not. It does not automatically prove the opposite conclusion. Choose a counterexample that respects the opponent's actual terms rather than changing the subject. A strong response may narrow a claim rather than abandon it entirely.
CONNECT: Counterexamples serve logic, personal self-stories, and ethical arguments. They are tools for improving precision.
EXAMPLE: “Every productive person rises at dawn” is challenged by a productive night worker. The result is a narrower question about what schedule supports particular work.
DO: Write a universal claim you encounter and a relevant possible counterexample.
TRY: Revise the original claim to survive the test without becoming empty.
REFLECT: Did the counterexample expose the rule or just an irrelevant exception?
CHECK: What does a counterexample show?::That a universal claim or inference needs revision when a relevant case satisfies its conditions but defeats its conclusion.
MODEL: Claim~State the rule.|Case~Find a relevant exception.|Revision~Narrow the claim.

## Identify a reasoning error without name-calling
QUESTION: What step of the reasoning fails?
CORE: Fallacy labels can save time when used precisely, but the label alone does not explain the defect. Identify the missing link: an irrelevant attack, a distorted opponent's claim, an unsupported causal leap, or a limited set of options presented as exhaustive. State why the move matters to the conclusion. A genuine personal conflict may coexist with a valid argument, so do not use a fallacy label to dismiss everything a person says.
CONNECT: Guides to dishonest argument can teach recognition, but fair reasoning also requires checking your own side.
EXAMPLE: A speaker says a policy is wrong because its proponent is unpopular. You ask whether there is an independent problem with the policy itself.
DO: Find one argument you dislike and describe its exact weak inference before using any label.
TRY: Apply the same test to an argument you agree with.
REFLECT: Am I using a label to investigate or to end the conversation?
CHECK: What makes a fallacy diagnosis useful?::It identifies the exact inferential problem and explains how it affects the conclusion.
MODEL: Locate~Find the weak step.|Explain~Show why it fails.|Repair~Ask what evidence would help.

## Ask questions that open the reasoning
QUESTION: Which question would clarify an assumption or reveal a consequence?
CORE: Ask for definitions, evidence, alternatives, and implications in a tone suited to genuine inquiry. A question can be used as a disguised accusation; its form alone does not make it fair. Choose the question that addresses the current uncertainty rather than launching an interrogation. Be willing to answer the same question yourself.
CONNECT: Socratic questioning and active listening meet when questions are precise and reciprocal.
EXAMPLE: “What would count as a successful trial?” advances a proposal discussion more than “How can you possibly believe that?”
DO: Write one clarification, one evidence, and one implication question for a live issue.
TRY: Use only the most relevant question in conversation and listen to the answer.
REFLECT: What answer would I actually be willing to learn from?
CHECK: What distinguishes inquiry from rhetorical questioning?::A genuine question allows the answer to affect the conversation and is applied fairly to both sides.
MODEL: Uncertainty~Identify the gap.|Question~Ask precisely.|Listen~Use the answer.

## Disagree with the strongest version
QUESTION: What would make the opposing view reasonable to someone informed?
CORE: Before objecting, reconstruct the strongest relevant reasons for another view. Acknowledge what those reasons explain well, then identify the point where evidence or values lead you elsewhere. This does not require saying all views are equal. It makes the real disagreement visible and reduces wasted effort on caricatures.
CONNECT: Ethical and political texts in the collection disagree deeply. Studying them together requires preserving tensions rather than declaring a single winner by tone.
EXAMPLE: One person prioritizes efficiency in a process; another worries about exclusion. A fair discussion asks whether a design can meet both or which cost should be accepted.
DO: Write the best case for a view you oppose, using reasons its advocate would recognize.
TRY: State your disagreement as one specific contested premise or value.
REFLECT: Which part of the opposing case did I initially omit?
CHECK: Why steelman an opposing view?::It directs disagreement toward the strongest real reasons instead of an easily defeated substitute.
MODEL: Reconstruct~State the best reasons.|Locate~Find the true disagreement.|Respond~Address that point.

## Use logic in an everyday decision
QUESTION: What conclusion am I drawing, and what would make it warranted?
CORE: Bring a real decision into a short argument: list premises, note what follows, test alternatives, and separate factual uncertainty from value disagreement. Logic will not decide every trade-off; it clarifies where judgment is required. A valid conditional can still rely on uncertain data or an aim you later reject.
CONNECT: The next module examines empirical evidence, and the one after it examines interacting systems. Logic makes their claims legible without pretending form alone settles the world.
EXAMPLE: You consider changing jobs. Your reasons include pay, learning, health, and obligation. Writing them separately shows that a pay increase alone does not entail an overall improvement.
DO: Write a five-line argument for one upcoming decision.
TRY: Ask what evidence or changed value would reverse your conclusion.
REFLECT: Where did a hidden assumption enter the argument?
CHECK: What can logic contribute to a decision?::It clarifies implications and assumptions while leaving factual and value judgments visible.
MODEL: Premises~List reasons and values.|Inference~Check what follows.|Decision~Act with uncertainty visible.
