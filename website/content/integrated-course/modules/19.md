# Leadership, teams, and institutions
DESCRIPTION: Coordinate work, create reliable feedback, share authority, and understand how institutions shape behavior.
BOOKS: B025 B026 B074 B096 B127 B133 B134 B141 B151 B173 B174 B201 B202 B225 B247 B268 B272 B273 B287 B293 B310 B312

## Define the work leadership must enable
QUESTION: What can the group accomplish together that individuals cannot do alone?
CORE: Leadership begins with a shared purpose and the coordination needed to pursue it. A title grants formal authority but does not automatically supply judgment or trust. Identify the work, dependencies, decisions, and information people need. Ask whose interests the goal serves and whether the goal is worthy of the group's effort.
CONNECT: Service-oriented and competitive leadership texts emphasize different aims. Both require clarity about the actual work and its consequences.
EXAMPLE: A team needs to publish accurate guidance. The leader defines review responsibilities and an escalation path instead of merely demanding enthusiasm.
DO: State a team's purpose as an outcome for the people it serves.
TRY: Map one dependency that currently slows the work.
REFLECT: Am I asking for effort toward an aim that the group understands and endorses?
CHECK: What is a leadership purpose?::To enable coordinated work toward an explicit, worthwhile shared aim.
MODEL: Purpose~Clarify the aim.|Dependencies~Map needed cooperation.|Enable~Remove a real barrier.

## Match authority with accountability
QUESTION: Who can decide, and who is answerable for the result?
CORE: Unclear authority produces delays, duplicated effort, and hidden blame. Specify decisions a person can make, information they need, and when they must consult others. Accountability should correspond to actual control and resources; holding someone responsible for an outcome while withholding necessary authority is unfair and ineffective.
CONNECT: Organizational behavior and agency both distinguish chosen action from external conditions. Apply that distinction to roles, not only individuals.
EXAMPLE: A project owner may adjust a schedule within agreed limits but must consult the team before changing another department's workload.
DO: Map owner, consultation, and escalation for one recurring decision.
TRY: Identify one mismatch between responsibility and authority.
REFLECT: Who is expected to deliver without the ability to resolve the obstacle?
CHECK: Why align authority and accountability?::People need the decision rights and resources required for the outcomes they are held responsible for.
MODEL: Decide~Define authority.|Support~Provide information and resources.|Review~Match results to actual control.

## Build trust through dependable behavior
QUESTION: What repeated action would make my commitments credible?
CORE: Trust grows when promises, information, and behavior align over time. State limits honestly, deliver what you can, and communicate changes before others are surprised. Warmth and confidence can help relationships, but they cannot substitute for reliability. Trust also depends on protecting private information and acknowledging mistakes.
CONNECT: Charisma books focus on impression; service and organizational books focus on repeated conduct. Treat impression as secondary to dependable action.
EXAMPLE: A manager admits that an estimate was wrong, explains the revised plan, and asks what the team needs to meet it.
DO: Identify one promise you can make more specific and realistic.
TRY: Update an affected person early when a commitment changes.
REFLECT: Which part of my reputation depends on appearance rather than conduct?
CHECK: What makes trust durable?::Repeated alignment among commitments, communication, action, and repair.
MODEL: Promise~Be specific.|Deliver~Act consistently.|Repair~Address errors promptly.

## Invite dissent before a decision
QUESTION: What concern might the group be reluctant to say aloud?
CORE: Agreement in a meeting can reflect genuine consensus, fatigue, status differences, or fear. Make room for objections before a decision hardens. Ask for alternative explanations and implementation risks, especially from people closest to the work. Do not demand performative disagreement when a decision is already made; be clear about what input can still change.
CONNECT: Groupthink and bias research suggest that leadership quality depends partly on whether relevant information can travel upward.
EXAMPLE: Before a launch, a leader asks each role to name one failure mode and records which ones need a response.
DO: Design one question that invites a substantive objection to your current plan.
TRY: Explain which parts of the plan are open and how feedback will be used.
REFLECT: Have I rewarded agreement more visibly than accuracy?
CHECK: Why invite dissent?::It can reveal missing information and reduce decisions shaped by status or pressure to agree.
MODEL: Open~Clarify what can change.|Invite~Ask for risks and alternatives.|Respond~Show how input affects the decision.

## Set goals without letting metrics replace judgment
QUESTION: What result matters, and what signals progress toward it?
CORE: An objective states the desired direction; key results indicate observable progress. Choose measures close enough to the purpose that improving them helps the real work. Include balancing checks for quality, fairness, or unintended effects. A target can focus effort, but it can also encourage gaming if people are rewarded for the number alone.
CONNECT: OKR frameworks and systems thinking meet when measures are treated as tools subject to review.
EXAMPLE: A support team aims to resolve issues well, tracking repeat contacts and user comprehension alongside response time.
DO: Write one objective and two observable indicators for a team project.
TRY: Add one measure of a harm that could rise while the main number improves.
REFLECT: Could someone hit this target while failing the underlying purpose?
CHECK: What keeps a metric useful?::Its connection to the real aim, plus review of quality and unintended effects.
MODEL: Aim~Define the outcome.|Measure~Choose relevant signals.|Balance~Check hidden costs.

## Lead change as an experiment with people
QUESTION: How will affected people learn, contribute, and adapt?
CORE: Change alters routines, power, information, and sometimes identity. Explain the reason, name what is uncertain, invite local knowledge, and test at a manageable scale where possible. Provide time and support for new capability. Resistance may reveal a real flaw, a loss, or simply a need to understand; investigate rather than assigning a motive automatically.
CONNECT: Leadership agility and systems thinking emphasize adaptation over one perfect top-down plan.
EXAMPLE: A new reporting process is piloted with one team, whose feedback reveals a duplicate entry the designers missed.
DO: Map stakeholders, expected benefits, expected costs, and unknowns for a proposed change.
TRY: Design a reversible pilot and a feedback point.
REFLECT: What would I learn if resistance were partly accurate?
CHECK: Why pilot a change?::It reveals practical effects and allows revision before the whole system bears the cost.
MODEL: Explain~Share purpose and uncertainty.|Pilot~Test with affected people.|Adapt~Revise from feedback.

## Understand institutional incentives
QUESTION: What behavior does this system reward, even if its stated values differ?
CORE: Policies, promotion criteria, reporting lines, and informal norms influence behavior. Examine what is rewarded, punished, ignored, and made difficult. Individual character matters, but a repeated organizational pattern may require structural change. Be cautious about attributing every action to a private motive when an incentive is visible.
CONNECT: Political and organizational texts widen the view from personal habits to institutions, power, and historical context.
EXAMPLE: A workplace says it values careful review but rewards only fast output. Errors increase despite repeated reminders to “be more diligent.”
DO: Compare one institution's stated aim with its actual rewards and constraints.
TRY: Propose one structural adjustment that would better support the stated aim.
REFLECT: Which behavior am I criticizing individually while the system repeatedly encourages it?
CHECK: Why study incentives?::They can explain recurring behavior and identify changes that individual exhortation cannot achieve.
MODEL: State~Read the official aim.|Observe~Identify real rewards.|Redesign~Align incentives with purpose.

## Review power and responsibility
QUESTION: Who benefits, who bears costs, and who can challenge the decision?
CORE: Leadership uses power, even when framed as service. Map the people affected by a decision, including those absent from the meeting. Explain the rationale, provide a way to question it, and review unequal effects. Historical cases of institutions misusing scientific or moral authority show why good intentions and prestigious status are insufficient safeguards.
CONNECT: Ethics, political history, and organizational design meet in accountability to people with less power.
EXAMPLE: A new attendance rule improves managerial visibility but burdens caregivers with unpredictable schedules. The team examines the trade-off and revises the rule.
DO: Make a stakeholder and cost map for one group decision.
TRY: Identify a safe channel through which affected people can challenge or correct it.
REFLECT: Whose experience is invisible in the metric or meeting?
CHECK: What should responsible power include?::Attention to affected people, visible reasons, correction channels, and review of unequal consequences.
MODEL: Map~Identify who is affected.|Explain~Make reasons visible.|Correct~Create a route for challenge.
