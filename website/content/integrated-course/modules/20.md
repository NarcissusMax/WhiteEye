# Strategy, competition, and cooperation
DESCRIPTION: Diagnose challenges, allocate scarce effort, respond to change, and know when a competitive frame is inappropriate.
BOOKS: B002 B018 B061 B074 B076 B084 B096 B109 B133 B171 B193 B195 B198 B202 B231 B251 B268 B269 B287 B292

## Distinguish goal, plan, tactic, and strategy
QUESTION: What kind of statement am I making about the work?
CORE: A goal is a desired result. A plan organizes actions. A tactic is a local move. A strategy explains how a set of actions addresses a central challenge under constraints. Confusing them leads to long activity lists without a reason they should succeed. A strategy can change when its diagnosis changes; it is not a slogan carved in stone.
CONNECT: Business, political, and military texts use the words differently. The useful common question is whether actions fit the situation and each other.
EXAMPLE: “Grow membership” is a goal. A strategy identifies why people do not return and coordinates changes in onboarding and ongoing value.
DO: Classify five statements from one project as goal, plan, tactic, or strategy.
TRY: Rewrite a goal as a diagnosis and coordinated approach.
REFLECT: What obstacle have my actions failed to address?
CHECK: What does strategy add to a goal?::A diagnosis of the challenge and a coherent approach to overcoming it.
MODEL: Goal~Name the desired result.|Diagnosis~Identify the obstacle.|Strategy~Coordinate actions under constraints.

## Find the decisive constraint
QUESTION: Which obstacle, if changed, would make the largest useful difference?
CORE: A complex problem may have many difficulties but only a few that currently limit progress. Map dependencies and find what must be true before other actions can help. Do not assume the most visible complaint is the main constraint. A bottleneck can move after an intervention, so recheck it after change.
CONNECT: Systems thinking and strategic diagnosis both focus attention where effort can change the whole pattern.
EXAMPLE: A team buys faster software but still waits weeks for approvals. Approval time, not computing speed, is the present bottleneck.
DO: Draw the steps of one process and identify where work waits or fails most often.
TRY: Test a small change at that point and inspect downstream effects.
REFLECT: What attractive improvement leaves the real constraint untouched?
CHECK: What is a bottleneck?::A limiting step or condition that constrains the performance of the larger process.
MODEL: Flow~Map the process.|Constraint~Find the limiting point.|Test~Change it and remeasure.

## Allocate effort where it matters
QUESTION: Which actions deserve resources first?
CORE: Resources are limited. Compare the expected effect, cost, dependencies, and reversibility of candidate actions. Concentration can make a strategy coherent, but it can also neglect necessary maintenance or people. Name what you are postponing and protect essential obligations. Do not choose a priority merely because it sounds bold.
CONNECT: Opportunity cost gives strategy its discipline; ethical analysis checks whose needs are excluded.
EXAMPLE: A small organization focuses on fixing repeated service errors before funding a broad publicity campaign, because reliability is limiting retention.
DO: Rank three candidate actions by likely effect on the diagnosed challenge.
TRY: State the cost and excluded alternative for the top action.
REFLECT: What essential maintenance might this focus displace?
CHECK: What makes strategic allocation defensible?::A clear connection between the main challenge, expected effect, resource cost, and excluded alternatives.
MODEL: Diagnose~Know the constraint.|Rank~Compare actions.|Protect~Preserve necessary commitments.

## Use position and timing
QUESTION: How do current conditions change which move is useful?
CORE: A good action in one position can be poor in another. Consider available resources, other participants, timing, and the sequence of likely responses. Do not apply a favorite tactic simply because it worked before. Wait when information or preparation will improve the option; act when delay itself has a cost. Explain the reason for timing rather than calling all speed decisive.
CONNECT: Strategy texts emphasize position; decision science adds uncertainty and reversibility.
EXAMPLE: Announcing a process change before training staff creates avoidable resistance. A short pilot and support plan alter the position before wider rollout.
DO: Identify one action whose value depends strongly on timing or prerequisites.
TRY: State the conditions under which you would take it and the cost of waiting.
REFLECT: Am I reacting to urgency or to the actual position?
CHECK: Why does position matter?::The usefulness of a move depends on current resources, constraints, timing, and likely responses.
MODEL: Position~Assess conditions.|Timing~Compare acting and waiting.|Move~Choose with prerequisites visible.

## Anticipate responses without pretending to predict people
QUESTION: What might other participants do after my action?
CORE: Strategy includes response, but people are not fixed pieces. Consider several plausible reactions, their incentives, and the information they have. Avoid treating one imagined reaction as certain or designing plans around stereotypes. Where cooperation is possible, ask directly instead of inferring everything through a competitive lens.
CONNECT: Game theory and negotiation offer structured alternatives to a single forecast, while listening supplies information those models may miss.
EXAMPLE: A price change may lead customers to leave, accept it, or change usage. A pilot and interviews are more informative than choosing one assumed response.
DO: List three plausible responses to one proposed action and what would motivate each.
TRY: Identify a low-cost way to learn which response is more likely.
REFLECT: Have I turned an uncertain human response into a fact in my plan?
CHECK: What should strategic anticipation include?::Several plausible responses, incentives, uncertainty, and opportunities to gather information.
MODEL: Action~State the move.|Responses~Map plausible reactions.|Learning~Seek early signals.

## Know when competition is the wrong frame
QUESTION: Are we facing a rival, a shared problem, or both?
CORE: Competitive framing can clarify scarce resources and conflicting interests. It can also invent an enemy where coordination would help. Identify whether gains truly require another person's loss. Look for shared aims, repeat interactions, and external costs. Preserve legitimate boundaries even when seeking cooperation; collaboration is not the same as surrender.
CONNECT: War metaphors appear across business and self-help texts. Their transfer to relationships or civic life can mislead when it erases mutual benefit or human dignity.
EXAMPLE: Two departments compete for a budget, but both lose from duplicated data collection. A shared system may free resources before the budget decision.
DO: Reframe one rivalry as a shared problem and note what conflict still remains.
TRY: Propose one cooperative option without concealing the genuine trade-off.
REFLECT: Who benefits from my treating this situation as a zero-sum contest?
CHECK: When is a competitive frame incomplete?::When shared interests, repeat relationships, or joint value are ignored.
MODEL: Conflict~Name the scarce resource.|Common~Identify shared aims.|Choice~Use cooperation where real.

## Compare strategic traditions in context
QUESTION: What does this framework assume about people and the setting?
CORE: Different traditions emphasize deception, virtue, flexibility, duty, decisive action, or institutional legitimacy. Read each within its original context before applying it. A tactic for armed conflict is not automatically a guide to friendship or leadership. Compare the problem each tradition addresses and its moral costs. Preserve a disagreement if the texts rest on different values.
CONNECT: Integrating Sun Tzu, Clausewitz, samurai writing, and modern business requires explicit limits, not a blended list of slogans.
EXAMPLE: “Win without fighting” can inspire conflict avoidance, but it does not settle what a fair negotiation requires when the other party is vulnerable.
DO: Compare two strategic ideas by context, aim, and acceptable costs.
TRY: Identify one domain where each idea should not be transferred without major revision.
REFLECT: Which metaphor is doing more work than the evidence allows?
CHECK: Why study strategic traditions in context?::Their aims, assumptions, and moral limits differ, so transfer needs justification.
MODEL: Context~Identify original use.|Assumption~Name what it presumes.|Transfer~Check fit and ethics.

## Write a strategic brief
QUESTION: How will I turn this analysis into coherent action?
CORE: State the situation, objective, decisive constraint, guiding approach, coordinated actions, likely responses, ethical limits, and review signals. Keep the brief short enough to use. A brief is a working theory, not a guarantee. If evidence changes the diagnosis, change the strategy rather than defending its wording.
CONNECT: This prepares the problem-solving and delivery modules, where choices can be tested against real constraints and their consequences for other people.
EXAMPLE: A community project identifies low return attendance as the constraint, tests a clearer welcome process, and reviews both attendance and participant experience.
DO: Write a one-page strategic brief for a real, non-harmful project.
TRY: Ask someone affected which assumption is least credible.
REFLECT: What observation would cause me to abandon the current approach?
CHECK: What makes a strategic brief useful?::It links diagnosis, coordinated action, ethical limits, and evidence that can revise the plan.
MODEL: Diagnose~Find the constraint.|Coordinate~Choose actions that fit.|Review~Update from outcomes.
