# Health literacy and responsible care
DESCRIPTION: Ask better health questions, read claims carefully, and use qualified care without turning a reading course into diagnosis or treatment.

## Turn a symptom into a care question
QUESTION: What am I experiencing, and what information would a clinician need?
CORE: Record the concern, onset, frequency, severity, relevant conditions, medicines, and effect on daily life as accurately as you can. Avoid deciding the diagnosis from a list of possible causes. A professional can combine your account with examination and appropriate tests. Seek timely help when symptoms are severe, persistent, changing, or concerning in context.
CONNECT: Observation and uncertainty skills support safer conversations about health when they are used to prepare for care rather than replace it.
EXAMPLE: Instead of “I have a circulation disease,” you record when a symptom began, what accompanies it, and what changed before seeking assessment.
DO: Create a neutral symptom record for a hypothetical case.
TRY: Write three clear questions a clinician could answer.
REFLECT: Which conclusion have I reached before gathering the basic facts?
CHECK: Why record symptoms without self-diagnosis?::The record improves professional assessment while leaving causal interpretation open.
MODEL: Observe~Describe the concern.|Context~Record timing and effects.|Consult~Seek appropriate assessment.

## Evaluate a treatment promise
QUESTION: Who was studied, what changed, and what harms or alternatives were considered?
CORE: A confident title or testimonial does not establish that a method cures everyone. Identify the exact outcome, comparison, duration, adverse effects, and whether evidence applies to the person's circumstances. Consider existing treatment and the risk of delay. Do not stop prescribed care because an older book or advertisement promises a permanent solution.
CONNECT: Causal reasoning, persuasion literacy, and ethics converge when a claim affects health decisions.
EXAMPLE: A book claims to eliminate a common sexual-health problem forever. You examine the causes it addresses and seek qualified assessment instead of assuming one method fits all.
DO: Write the PICO-like elements of one health claim: population, intervention, comparison, outcome.
TRY: Identify a possible harm or delayed-care cost omitted from the pitch.
REFLECT: What makes the promise feel certain beyond its evidence?
CHECK: What should a treatment claim specify?::The relevant people, intervention, comparison, outcomes, harms, and limits of the evidence.
MODEL: Claim~Define the promise.|Evidence~Inspect comparison and outcomes.|Care~Check harms and alternatives.

## Understand sexual health as wellbeing and choice
QUESTION: Does this advice respect health, comfort, consent, and the people involved?
CORE: Sexual wellbeing includes physical, emotional, relational, and social aspects. People vary in bodies, preferences, and circumstances. Discuss concerns and boundaries openly, respect ongoing consent, and use current qualified information for contraception, infection prevention, or persistent difficulties. Avoid gender stereotypes and products that convert embarrassment into a guaranteed-cure sale.
CONNECT: Relationship skills and medical literacy both matter; neither replaces the other.
EXAMPLE: Partners discuss comfort and a recurring concern, then seek appropriate clinical advice rather than interpreting the issue as proof of lost attraction.
DO: Draft three respectful questions for a hypothetical sexual-health conversation.
TRY: Separate a relational expectation from a medical question that needs qualified input.
REFLECT: Which assumption comes from a stereotype rather than the people involved?
CHECK: What belongs in sexual health literacy?::Respect, consent, accurate information, and appropriate care for physical and relational concerns.
MODEL: Talk~Discuss preferences and concerns.|Protect~Respect consent and safety.|Care~Use qualified information.

## Distinguish wellness practice from clinical care
QUESTION: Is this a general habit or a response to a health condition?
CORE: Sleep routines, movement, and reflective practices may support wellbeing, but an individual condition can require diagnosis and treatment. Avoid treating ordinary advice as a cure for persistent symptoms. A clinical workbook may contain useful language but also assume a practitioner, assessment, and safeguards. Use the text for understanding and questions while seeking fitting care when needed.
CONNECT: Mental performance and mental health share some vocabulary while differing in stakes and support requirements.
EXAMPLE: A person practices a calming routine and also seeks professional help when anxiety repeatedly disrupts work and relationships.
DO: Classify three pieces of advice as general habit, clinical method, or uncertain.
TRY: Identify what assessment or support would be needed before applying a clinical method.
REFLECT: Am I calling a condition a mindset problem because that feels easier to solve?
CHECK: Why distinguish wellness from clinical care?::General habits cannot diagnose or replace individualized treatment for a condition.
MODEL: Advice~Identify its scope.|Concern~Assess persistence and impact.|Care~Use appropriate expertise.

## Read imaging and test results with a professional
QUESTION: What does this result show, and what does it not establish by itself?
CORE: A scan, lab value, or report must be interpreted with symptoms, history, test quality, and clinical context. A professional reference can teach that normal variation and incidental findings exist; it cannot turn a general reader into a diagnostician. Ask the clinician what the finding means, how certain it is, and what follow-up is appropriate.
CONNECT: Technical literacy and uncertainty help patients ask better questions without self-interpreting complex medical data.
EXAMPLE: A scan report mentions an incidental feature. The clinician explains whether it is relevant to the presenting concern and whether follow-up is needed.
DO: Write questions about meaning, uncertainty, alternatives, and follow-up for a hypothetical report.
TRY: Identify what context a specialist would require to interpret it.
REFLECT: Have I treated one image or number as the whole diagnosis?
CHECK: Why is a medical result not self-interpreting?::Its significance depends on clinical context, test limits, and professional interpretation.
MODEL: Result~Read what was measured.|Context~Add symptoms and history.|Meaning~Discuss with a qualified clinician.

## Read drug and poison information by role
QUESTION: Is this reference intended for a patient, clinician, analyst, or emergency responder?
CORE: Drug and toxicology references contain specialized measurements and procedures. Their use depends on training, setting, and current protocols. A lay reader should not infer dosing, identification, treatment, or safety from a technical entry. For a suspected exposure or adverse reaction, use appropriate local medical or emergency services promptly.
CONNECT: Specialist references can reveal how experts structure evidence while still being unsuitable as a personal instruction manual.
EXAMPLE: A monograph lists laboratory tests for a compound. This does not permit home identification or treatment of an exposure.
DO: Identify the intended user and limits of one technical reference entry.
TRY: Write the information an emergency professional would need from a caller, without inventing a treatment.
REFLECT: Which technical detail gives me confidence I have not earned?
CHECK: Why does a toxicology reference require context?::Its data and methods assume professional training, equipment, and current protocols.
MODEL: Role~Identify the reader.|Limit~Know what cannot be inferred.|Help~Use appropriate services for actual exposure.

## Ask about benefits, harms, and alternatives together
QUESTION: What are all reasonable options, including doing nothing for now?
CORE: A care decision should consider expected benefit, possible harm, uncertainty, personal circumstances, and alternatives. The right balance depends on the situation and the person's preferences. Ask what happens with observation, a different approach, or no immediate intervention. A dramatic “natural versus conventional” frame can hide useful options on both sides.
CONNECT: Decision records and informed consent become practical through shared questions.
EXAMPLE: A patient asks about the likely benefit, common harms, and other options for a proposed treatment, then decides with their clinician.
DO: Make a question list for a hypothetical choice covering benefits, harms, alternatives, and timing.
TRY: Identify which answer depends on individual medical history.
REFLECT: Which option have I overlooked because the discussion was framed as a two-way contest?
CHECK: What belongs in an informed care choice?::Benefits, harms, uncertainty, alternatives, timing, and the person's circumstances and preferences.
MODEL: Options~List realistic paths.|Evidence~Compare outcomes and harms.|Choice~Decide with appropriate care.

## Keep a personal health-information boundary
QUESTION: Which information should I record, share, or keep private?
CORE: Health information can be sensitive. Record enough for useful care and continuity, but consider who can access notes, apps, and shared devices. Ask how information will be used when enrolling in a service or study. Privacy should not become a reason to withhold critical facts from qualified care; choose appropriate channels and permissions.
CONNECT: Digital privacy and health literacy meet in the practical management of sensitive information.
EXAMPLE: A person brings a concise symptom history to an appointment while avoiding unnecessary posting of test results in a public forum.
DO: Review where your health records and self-tracking data are stored.
TRY: Identify one sharing choice to change or clarify.
REFLECT: Am I sharing to obtain useful help or because a platform makes sharing easy?
CHECK: What should guide health-data sharing?::The purpose of care, sensitivity of the information, who can access it, and the person's informed choice.
MODEL: Purpose~Know why data is needed.|Channel~Choose appropriate access.|Review~Revisit permissions.
