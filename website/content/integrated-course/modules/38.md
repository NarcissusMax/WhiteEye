# Craft, perception, and material practice
DESCRIPTION: Learn through making, close observation, sensory vocabulary, and feedback while respecting tools, materials, and safety.
BOOKS: B044 B137 B142 B161 B169 B181 B203 B256 B271 B296 B297

## Begin a craft with observation
QUESTION: What does the material or object actually do?
CORE: Craft begins with looking closely at shape, texture, behavior, and change under ordinary conditions. Record what you see before imposing a favored style or explanation. Different crafts use different senses and standards, but each benefits from precise attention. A description can become more useful when tied to a comparison and a purpose.
CONNECT: Observation skills move from arguments into hands-on practice without losing the need to distinguish fact and interpretation.
EXAMPLE: A drawing student observes how a cup's rim changes shape from their viewpoint before applying a memorized oval.
DO: Describe one ordinary object through shape, texture, and one surprising detail.
TRY: Compare it with a second object and state what changed in your description.
REFLECT: Which feature did I draw or name from memory instead of seeing?
CHECK: Why begin craft with observation?::Material and viewpoint can challenge assumptions that a generic template hides.
MODEL: Look~Notice specific features.|Compare~Find differences.|Make~Respond to what is present.

## Learn perspective as a constructed view
QUESTION: From where is the scene being seen?
CORE: Perspective systems map three-dimensional relationships onto a surface. A change in viewpoint alters apparent size and direction. Curvilinear, isometric, and other systems make different choices about distortion and use. Learn the rule and its intended effect, then test it against a real scene. An unusual projection is not an error if it serves the design knowingly.
CONNECT: The earlier model lesson applies visually: a representation selects relationships rather than copying reality without interpretation.
EXAMPLE: An isometric drawing keeps parallel edges parallel for clarity, while a fisheye view emphasizes a wide field with visible distortion.
DO: Sketch one simple room from two viewpoints or projection conventions.
TRY: State what each drawing preserves and distorts.
REFLECT: Which visual rule am I using without knowing its purpose?
CHECK: What does a perspective system do?::It defines how spatial relationships are represented on a surface, preserving some features and distorting others.
MODEL: Viewpoint~Choose where the observer is.|System~Apply its rule.|Effect~Judge what the view communicates.

## Build sensory vocabulary through comparison
QUESTION: What difference can I describe without pretending everyone experiences it identically?
CORE: Taste, smell, texture, and visual character are often described through analogy. Compare samples under similar conditions and use specific words for differences in intensity, duration, and association. Sensory judgments include individual variation and cultural learning. A rich description helps another person imagine an experience but cannot guarantee they will perceive it the same way.
CONNECT: Writing and observation meet in sensory criticism. The aim is communicable attention, not false objectivity.
EXAMPLE: Two fragrances both seem floral, but one becomes powdery and soft while the other remains sharp and brief. The comparison gives the words meaning.
DO: Compare two harmless everyday sensory experiences using at least three precise differences.
TRY: Ask another person what they noticed and keep both accounts.
REFLECT: Which adjective describes the object, and which describes my memory or preference?
CHECK: Why compare sensory samples?::Comparison makes descriptive differences clearer while allowing for individual perception.
MODEL: Sample~Experience under similar conditions.|Compare~Describe differences.|Share~Test whether language communicates.

## Understand tools and material constraints
QUESTION: What can this tool or material do safely and reliably?
CORE: A craft method depends on material properties, tool limits, protective practices, and the maker's skill. Read safety information before attempting a new process. Some technical books include heat, blades, chemicals, or other hazards that require qualified training and proper facilities. The course can teach planning and material literacy without turning hazardous procedures into casual home projects.
CONNECT: Engineering and safety modules become concrete when a physical process has consequences beyond a diagram.
EXAMPLE: A learner examines the properties of different materials and chooses a supervised introductory workshop before using unfamiliar powered tools.
DO: For one harmless craft, list material behavior, tool function, and likely failure modes.
TRY: Identify what training or safety setup would be needed for an advanced version.
REFLECT: Am I choosing a tool because I understand it or because the finished object looks appealing?
CHECK: What should precede unfamiliar material practice?::Understanding material and tool limits, relevant safety requirements, and the level of supervision needed.
MODEL: Material~Know its behavior.|Tool~Know its limits.|Setting~Match risk to training.

## Use prototypes to answer a question
QUESTION: What uncertainty could a small version resolve?
CORE: A prototype is an intentionally incomplete version used to test a specific assumption. It may reveal fit, shape, comfort, comprehension, or production difficulty. Choose the cheapest safe form that can answer the question. Do not confuse a polished sample with a useful test if it never exposes the uncertain part.
CONNECT: Business experiments and craft prototypes share a logic, though physical materials add different constraints.
EXAMPLE: Before building an entire object, a maker creates a cardboard size model to test whether the dimensions fit its intended space.
DO: Name one design uncertainty and a safe prototype that could resolve it.
TRY: Define what observation would lead you to change the design.
REFLECT: Which expensive step am I taking before testing the central assumption?
CHECK: What is a prototype for?::To answer a specific design question before committing to a full version.
MODEL: Unknown~Name the assumption.|Prototype~Build the simplest test.|Revision~Change from observation.

## Practice one technical component at a time
QUESTION: Which movement or judgment is limiting the finished result?
CORE: A whole craft can overwhelm a beginner. Isolate one component, practice it under manageable conditions, and return it to the full work. Seek feedback from someone who can observe the process, not only the finished artifact. Repetition may build control, but change the drill when it no longer teaches.
CONNECT: Sport and creative practice use the same cycle of isolate, vary, and integrate while retaining domain-specific safety.
EXAMPLE: A drawing learner practices a series of ellipses from observation, then uses that control in a still-life study.
DO: Identify one component skill in a craft you want to learn.
TRY: Design five safe repetitions with one feedback criterion and a full-task application.
REFLECT: Did the isolated drill improve the real artifact?
CHECK: Why return an isolated skill to a whole task?::A drill can improve one component without guaranteeing it works in context.
MODEL: Isolate~Focus on one skill.|Practice~Use feedback.|Integrate~Apply it to the full work.

## Judge workmanship by use and context
QUESTION: What does a well-made result need to do for its user or viewer?
CORE: Quality can include function, durability, clarity, beauty, accessibility, and fit for context. The criteria differ by object and audience. Ask what matters before applying a fashionable style or one maker's preferred standard. A beautiful result can fail its use; a plain result can be carefully made for a demanding purpose.
CONNECT: Values and business service help determine craft standards without reducing art to efficiency.
EXAMPLE: A field guide prioritizes legible diagrams and durable pages, while an exhibition piece may explore ambiguity deliberately.
DO: Write three quality criteria for one artifact and rank them by purpose.
TRY: Ask an intended user or viewer which criterion you missed.
REFLECT: Which standard comes from status rather than the work's actual use?
CHECK: What determines craft quality?::The artifact's purpose, context, and relevant standards of function, experience, and care.
MODEL: Purpose~Know the use.|Criteria~Choose fitting standards.|Test~Get contextual feedback.

## Preserve a maker's learning record
QUESTION: What should I carry from this attempt into the next one?
CORE: Keep sketches, prototypes, measurements, choices, and a short account of what changed. Record mistakes without hiding successful details. Over time, the record reveals both technique and taste: what you can execute and what you choose to make. A craft practice grows through a sequence of informed attempts rather than a single finished object.
CONNECT: The course's capstone uses this same principle across life projects: show the process, evidence, and revision.
EXAMPLE: A maker documents why a material failed, what replacement was tried, and how the final object performed in use.
DO: Create a one-page record for a recent making attempt.
TRY: Choose the next test based on the record's most important unresolved question.
REFLECT: What did the material teach me that planning alone did not?
CHECK: What should a maker's record preserve?::Process choices, observations, failures, successful features, and the next test.
MODEL: Record~Capture the attempt.|Interpret~Explain results.|Continue~Choose the next experiment.
