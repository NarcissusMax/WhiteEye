## Rehearse a safe exit before a tense moment
QUESTION: Where could I go and whom could I contact if a situation changed?
CORE: A useful safety plan identifies practical options before stress narrows attention. In an unfamiliar setting, notice accessible exits, ordinary staff or support, and a way to contact help. This is preparation for choice, not a demand to scan every person for threat. Adapt to mobility, local conditions, and the actual risk. In an immediate emergency, use local emergency services and follow qualified responders rather than a memorized script from a book.

## Respond to repeated unwanted contact
QUESTION: When should an unwanted pattern move from private discomfort to documented support?
CORE: Repeated boundary crossing deserves attention even when each incident is ambiguous alone. Record dates, behavior, and any messages without escalating contact to prove a point. Tell a trusted person or relevant service what is happening, and use workplace, school, platform, or local support channels as appropriate. The right response depends on context and current law; no course can classify a particular situation from a few details. The burden of another person's behavior belongs to the person doing it.
CONNECT: [[04 Agency, responsibility, and boundaries]] distinguishes a person's choices from blame for harm. [[18 Manipulation, propaganda, and independent judgment]] helps recognize pressure without assuming a diagnosis.
EXAMPLE: A colleague continues contacting someone after a clear request to stop. The recipient saves messages and asks a workplace contact about available options.

## Help as a bystander without taking over
QUESTION: What can a witness do that increases someone's options and safety?
CORE: A bystander may be able to alert staff, create a chance to leave, call appropriate help, or check privately what the affected person wants. Choose an action that fits the risk and your capability; direct confrontation is not always safest. Do not make a public spectacle of the person receiving help or demand a detailed account on the spot. Afterward, let them decide what support they want where possible, while responding to immediate danger appropriately.
CONNECT: [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] starts with the other person's perspective. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] weighs care, consent, and likely consequences together.
EXAMPLE: At a social event, a witness notices someone repeatedly being blocked from leaving a conversation and asks staff to assist while checking whether the person wants to move away.

## Review a safety event without hindsight blame
QUESTION: How can we learn from an event without pretending everyone had the later facts?
CORE: After an incident, separate what was known at the time from what became clear afterward. Ask which supports, exits, rules, or communications worked and which failed. Do not turn a victim's ordinary action into the cause of another person's harmful choice. A useful review improves future options and institutional response; it does not require the affected person to retell details for others' education. Some events call for trauma-informed professional support rather than a group exercise.

EXAMPLE: A venue reviews whether staff knew how to respond to a report and whether contact information was visible, without assigning fault to the person who sought help.
