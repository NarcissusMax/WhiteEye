## Map the customer's whole task
QUESTION: What is the customer trying to complete before and after using the offer?
CORE: A product or service occupies one part of a longer task. Understand how someone recognizes a need, finds help, begins, pays, uses the result, and resolves a problem. Friction in any stage can make a technically good offer unusable. Interview about recent real attempts rather than imagined preferences, and include people who abandon the process. A customer map should expose points where the business can help honestly, not become a pretext to pressure someone.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] asks for the actual gap. [[15 Relationships, consent, and mutual choice]] reminds us that another person's interests cannot be reduced to a conversion target.
EXAMPLE: A tutoring service learns that parents need reliable scheduling and progress communication as much as the lesson itself.
DO: Draw six steps in one customer's task from need to follow-up.
TRY: Identify a point where the current offer creates avoidable work or uncertainty.
REFLECT: Which part of the customer's experience was invisible from inside the business?
CHECK: Why map the whole customer task?::Value depends on the surrounding steps of finding, using, and resolving an offer, not only its central feature.
MODEL: Observe~Trace a real recent task.|Locate~Find consequential friction.|Improve~Test one helpful change.

## Separate revenue from cash available
QUESTION: Does a sale provide money in time to pay the bills it creates?
CORE: Revenue records sales; cash flow records when money enters and leaves. A profitable-looking order can still strain a small operation if payment arrives after wages, materials, or rent are due. List payment terms, recurring obligations, reserves, and the cost of delivering each order. Use a simple time-based cash view and get professional help for formal accounts and taxes. Avoid treating a larger sales total as proof of a healthier business.
CONNECT: [[25 Money, records, and financial judgment]] separates balances from timing. [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] shows how delays in one dependency affect others.
EXAMPLE: A workshop books many corporate sessions but pays instructors before clients settle invoices; bookings rise while available cash falls.
DO: Sketch expected cash in and out over the next four periods for a hypothetical small service.
TRY: Name one payment term or cost that could create a temporary shortage.
REFLECT: Was I measuring activity, profit, or usable cash?
CHECK: Why can more sales create a cash problem?::Delivery costs may be due before customers pay, so timing matters as well as total revenue.
MODEL: Record~List dated inflows and outflows.|Forecast~Find gaps.|Adjust~Change terms or capacity responsibly.

## Test a channel by customer fit
QUESTION: Does this way of reaching people help the right people understand the offer?
CORE: A marketing channel should be judged by who it reaches, what people understand, what it costs, and whether resulting customers are well served. Platform popularity and follower counts are weak substitutes for useful demand. Run a small, truthful message test and observe the path from attention to appropriate use. If people arrive with the wrong expectation, correct the message rather than celebrating the click count. Rules and platform behavior change, so treat old playbooks as context rather than evergreen instructions.
CONNECT: [[18 Manipulation, propaganda, and independent judgment]] distinguishes explanation from pressure. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] cautions against vanity metrics.
EXAMPLE: A repair shop finds a clear local search listing brings more suitable enquiries than frequent broad social posts, even though the posts receive more likes.
DO: Choose one channel and define a useful outcome beyond views or followers.
TRY: Ask a new customer what they expected before contacting the business.
REFLECT: Was the channel helping customers choose well or only helping me count attention?
CHECK: What makes a marketing channel useful?::It reaches relevant people with accurate expectations at a cost the business can sustain.
MODEL: Match~Choose a fitting audience and channel.|Explain~State the offer honestly.|Check~Measure useful enquiries and outcomes.

## Design a fair response to a complaint
QUESTION: How does a business learn and repair when its promise is not met?
CORE: A complaint can reveal a defect in delivery, expectation, or fit. Hear what happened without making the customer prove a moral case, check the relevant record, and offer remedies within stated terms and obligations. Avoid a policy that rewards the loudest person while leaving quieter users with the same problem. Track repeated patterns and feed them back into product, communication, or operations. Some disputes need specialist legal or consumer advice.
CONNECT: [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] helps hear the person's account. [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] turns recurring defects into process changes.
EXAMPLE: Several customers cannot understand setup instructions. The firm helps those affected and revises the guide rather than labeling each case as user error.
DO: Write a complaint response that acknowledges the report, checks facts, and offers a clear next step.
TRY: Identify one repeated complaint that should trigger a system review.
REFLECT: Did I focus on defending the business before understanding the failure?
CHECK: How should a complaint improve a business?::It should prompt fair repair for the person and investigation of any recurring delivery or expectation problem.
MODEL: Hear~Clarify the experience.|Repair~Offer an appropriate response.|Learn~Change the cause where possible.
