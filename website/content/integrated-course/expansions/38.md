## Build a sample before committing material
QUESTION: What can a small model reveal before the full piece is made?
CORE: A sample lets a maker test proportion, appearance, feel, or process at lower cost. Choose the uncertainty first and make a sample that represents the relevant condition. A sketch may answer a composition question but not a durability question; a material swatch may show finish but not final scale. Record what changed in your plan. Use appropriate instruction and protective procedures for tools, heat, chemicals, or sharp materials rather than treating a book diagram as a safety course.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] designs bounded tests. [[23 Creative work and sustained projects]] uses drafts to expose uncertainty.
EXAMPLE: An artist makes three perspective thumbnails before beginning a large drawing; a maker tests a finish on scrap before applying it to a finished object.
DO: Name one uncertainty in a making project and a low-cost sample that tests it.
TRY: State one important property the sample cannot test.
REFLECT: Was I buying or cutting material before answering a cheaper design question?
CHECK: What makes a craft sample useful?::It represents the specific property under question while limiting cost and revealing what remains untested.
MODEL: Question~Name the uncertain property.|Sample~Make a small representative test.|Revise~Change the plan using its limits and findings.

## Train observation with paired comparisons
QUESTION: What difference can I describe without calling one version better too soon?
CORE: Perception improves when two examples are compared under similar conditions. Name features such as proportion, contrast, texture, timing, or scent without reducing them immediately to like and dislike. Then ask what the difference does for the intended use or experience. Language helps memory and communication, but a term is useful only when it points to something observable or reliably felt. Respect sensory differences between people.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] separates description from judgment. [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] supplies precise words without pretending they settle taste.
EXAMPLE: A student compares two drawings of the same room and describes horizon placement and distortion before choosing the one that serves a dramatic scene.
DO: Compare two safe examples in a craft you know and list five descriptive differences.
TRY: Explain which difference matters to the intended purpose and why.
REFLECT: Did my first preference prevent me from observing the actual differences?
CHECK: How do paired comparisons train craft perception?::They make specific differences easier to notice, name, and connect to an intended effect.
MODEL: Pair~Choose comparable examples.|Describe~Name observable differences.|Judge~Relate them to use.

## Keep a process record that can teach the next attempt
QUESTION: What should I write down before memory turns a trial into a story?
CORE: A maker's record captures materials, settings, sequence, conditions, result, and changes considered. Include failures and unexpected successes. A short photograph or sketch may preserve a detail better than prose; explain what the image shows. Avoid recording only the final polished object, since the decisions that produced it are what future work can use. Protect private client or collaborator information where relevant.
CONNECT: [[08 Practice, performance, and adaptation]] learns from feedback. [[40 Integration and the life-practice capstone]] uses records to compare cycles.
EXAMPLE: A maker notes which paper, tool angle, and lighting were used in a drawing study, then compares the resulting lines a week later.
DO: Create a one-page process log for a hypothetical craft attempt.
TRY: Add one observation that would help you repeat a successful detail.
REFLECT: Which part of a past result can I no longer explain because I did not record it?
CHECK: Why keep a craft process record?::It preserves conditions and choices so later attempts can reproduce, revise, or learn from the result.
MODEL: Capture~Record materials and steps.|Observe~Describe the result.|Iterate~Change one relevant factor next time.

## Judge a made object in its real setting
QUESTION: Does the work serve the person, place, and use it was made for?
CORE: Workmanship includes fit to purpose, not just visible complexity. Test how an object or artifact is handled, read, maintained, and experienced in the setting where it belongs. Ask intended users for specific observations while retaining responsibility for safety and technical limits. A display photograph can flatter features that fail in use; a plain object may excel at its task. Consider repairability and the cost of upkeep where they matter.
CONNECT: [[24 Business, customers, and useful value]] measures value in use. [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] includes acceptance and handover.
EXAMPLE: A hand-drawn sign looks elegant up close but cannot be read from the path where visitors must choose a direction.
DO: Write three real-use checks for a hypothetical made object.
TRY: Ask how a person would clean, update, or repair it after delivery.
REFLECT: Was I judging the work where it would be used or where it looked best?
CHECK: What does fit to purpose add to craft judgment?::It asks whether the made thing works for its intended user, setting, maintenance, and consequences.
MODEL: Place~Test in the actual setting.|Use~Observe handling and understanding.|Improve~Revise for function and care.
