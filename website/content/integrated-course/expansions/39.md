## Trace a public decision to its authority
QUESTION: Who had the power to decide, and how could the decision be challenged?
CORE: Institutions act through roles, procedures, and legal powers that differ by place and time. When evaluating a public decision, identify the body responsible, the rule it claims to use, the record of its reasoning, and available review routes. Do not assume that a persuasive official statement proves authority or that every unpopular outcome is unlawful. Current local rules require direct verification. Understanding process helps citizens ask better questions and makes accountability more concrete.
CONNECT: [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] studies decision rights. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] asks whether a valid process also treats people fairly.
EXAMPLE: Residents question a park closure by finding the responsible council body, meeting record, stated reason, and public comment route.
DO: Map the decision path in a hypothetical municipal choice: proposal, authority, record, review.
TRY: Write one factual question a citizen could ask at each stage.
REFLECT: Was I criticizing a person while the relevant decision sat elsewhere in the process?
CHECK: What helps assess a public decision's accountability?::Its authority, procedure, reasons, records, and available routes for review or correction.
MODEL: Locate~Find the deciding body.|Inspect~Read reasons and record.|Question~Use appropriate review channels.

## Look for missing records in a historical story
QUESTION: Whose account survived, and whose experience is harder to see?
CORE: Historical claims depend on sources that were created and preserved unevenly. Officials, publishers, and powerful groups often leave more records than people with less access. Identify the source type, purpose, date, and missing perspectives. An absence is not proof that a specific event occurred, but it can limit confidence in a broad claim. Compare multiple kinds of evidence and state what remains uncertain.
CONNECT: [[36 Literature, story, and human possibility]] distinguishes witness from historical synthesis. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] asks how selection affects an apparent pattern.
EXAMPLE: A policy archive shows officials praising a program, while letters and later interviews may reveal how recipients experienced it.
DO: Analyze a hypothetical historical claim with source, creator, purpose, and missing voice.
TRY: Propose one additional type of record that could change the account.
REFLECT: Which absence had I mistakenly treated as evidence of consent?
CHECK: Why inspect missing voices in history?::The surviving record may reflect unequal power and preserve some experiences more readily than others.
MODEL: Identify~Name surviving sources.|Locate~Find likely gaps.|Qualify~State what the record supports.

## Analyze a campaign's message and channel
QUESTION: How does a public message shape what people notice and whom they trust?
CORE: Public communication can inform, persuade, mobilize, or manipulate. Examine the claim, evidence, emotional frame, audience, sponsor, repetition, and channel. Ask what alternative information is omitted and whether the source invites checking. A message's emotional effect does not make it false, and technical accuracy does not remove selective framing. The goal is an independent judgment about both content and distribution.
CONNECT: [[18 Manipulation, propaganda, and independent judgment]] identifies pressure tactics. [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] shows how wording can set the question.
EXAMPLE: A campaign posts a striking photograph and a single statistic. A reader checks the time period, denominator, sponsor, and what policy the message asks them to support.
DO: Deconstruct a hypothetical public message into claim, frame, sponsor, audience, and omission.
TRY: Write one question that would test the central statistic or image context.
REFLECT: Which part of the delivery made the claim feel trustworthy before I examined it?
CHECK: What should be examined in public persuasion?::The claim, evidence, framing, sponsor, audience, channel, and omitted alternatives.
MODEL: Parse~Identify the message.|Trace~Find sponsor and distribution.|Check~Test evidence and omissions.

## Compare institutional performance with lived access
QUESTION: Does a service's reported success match people's ability to use it?
CORE: Institutions may report total output, cost, speed, or compliance while users experience delays, exclusion, or poor quality. Match each headline measure to the purpose of the service and the groups it serves. Investigate who appears in the data and who never reaches the counting point. Use both records and affected people's accounts, recognizing the limits of each. A fair review asks what action the institution will take when measures and experience diverge.
CONNECT: [[26 Economics, incentives, and public choices]] reads averages and distribution. [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] watches for displaced costs.
EXAMPLE: A clinic reports shorter appointments, while some patients cannot explain the agreed next step; speed alone is an incomplete measure.
DO: Choose a hypothetical public service and list one output, one quality, and one access measure.
TRY: Identify a group that may be absent from the institution's data.
REFLECT: Was the public number measuring the institution's activity or the user's result?
CHECK: How should institutional performance be assessed?::By connecting outputs to quality, access, distribution, and the service's real purpose.
MODEL: Purpose~Define what the service should achieve.|Compare~Use records and lived access.|Correct~Address gaps revealed by both.
