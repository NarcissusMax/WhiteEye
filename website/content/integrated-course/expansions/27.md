## Match a training measure to the goal
QUESTION: What should I observe to know whether this training is helping?
CORE: Body weight, pace, load, movement comfort, and consistency answer different questions. Select a measure tied to the capability or health behavior you chose, and include how recovery and daily life are affected. One difficult session does not show failure; one exceptional session does not establish a trend. Compare observations over a suitable period without turning every bodily change into a score. For health conditions, coordinate measures and training decisions with qualified care.

EXAMPLE: Someone training to walk farther tracks route, comfort, and frequency rather than only a scale reading.

## Distinguish discomfort from a warning sign
QUESTION: When should an exercise be modified or stopped?
CORE: Effort can feel uncomfortable, but persistent, worsening, or unusual symptoms deserve attention. Do not use a motivational slogan to override pain or a health concern. Describe what happened, when, and under which movement or load, then reduce or stop the activity as appropriate and seek qualified advice when needed. A reading course cannot diagnose a symptom or decide a safe return plan for a particular person.
CONNECT: [[08 Practice, performance, and adaptation]] includes recovery in learning. [[37 Health literacy and responsible care]] helps turn a symptom into a useful care question.
EXAMPLE: A runner notices a recurring sharp pain that worsens during sessions. They stop treating it as proof of commitment and seek an appropriate assessment.

## Compare nutrition claims by the question they answer
QUESTION: Does a food claim concern adequacy, performance, disease, or a marketing promise?
CORE: Different nutrition questions need different evidence. A short-term feeling after a meal does not prove a general disease theory; a study average may not dictate one person's complete diet. Ask what outcome was measured, over what period, for whom, and against which alternative. Watch for books that make a single ingredient or exclusion explain many unrelated problems. A practical plan should fit needs, preferences, access, and any clinician-guided restrictions.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] distinguishes correlation and intervention. [[37 Health literacy and responsible care]] asks how a claim should affect a real care decision.
EXAMPLE: Two books make conflicting claims about the same food. You compare their populations, outcomes, and certainty before changing an entire eating pattern.

CHECK: How should broad nutrition claims be examined?::By specifying outcome, population, time, comparison, and evidence quality before applying them to a person.
MODEL: Specify~Name the claimed effect.|Examine~Check study and comparison.|Fit~Consider individual needs and care.

## Plan training through ordinary disruptions
QUESTION: What keeps a practice viable when work, care, travel, or illness changes the week?
CORE: A perfect schedule is fragile if it has no smaller version or return path. Separate essential training from optional volume and design a lower-demand week that preserves safe movement or rest as appropriate. Avoid trying to repay every missed session at once. After illness or injury, individual return decisions may require professional guidance. The purpose of the plan is sustained capability, not an unbroken streak.
CONNECT: [[05 Habits and behavior design]] plans for interrupted routines. [[06 Attention, time, and a workable day]] fits commitments to real capacity.
EXAMPLE: A parent normally attends two supervised sessions. During a difficult week, they reduce the plan and resume gradually when circumstances allow.
