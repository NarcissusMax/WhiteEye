## Learn to read the action before joining it
QUESTION: What cues matter before I commit to a movement or pass?
CORE: Skilled action begins with perception. Identify the positions, motion, rules, and opportunities relevant to the next choice. A cue has value only within the sport and level being practiced; noticing everything at once is neither possible nor useful. Start with a few reliable cues and ask a coach or teammate how they interpret them. Review missed cues without pretending that every later outcome was visible in advance.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] separates observed cues from inference. [[08 Practice, performance, and adaptation]] shows how feedback improves a specific skill.
EXAMPLE: A football player looks over a shoulder before receiving and notices an open teammate, changing the next pass.
DO: Name three cues relevant to one safe, supervised sport situation.
TRY: Watch a short replay or demonstration and pause before the action to predict two possible choices.
REFLECT: Which cue did I notice only after knowing the result?
CHECK: What is tactical perception?::Selecting cues relevant to a decision in the actual sport and using them before acting.
MODEL: Scan~Notice a few relevant cues.|Choose~Select a fitting action.|Review~Compare expectation with outcome.

## Design a drill with a clear transfer target
QUESTION: Which part of real play should this drill improve?
CORE: A drill is useful when it isolates a learnable component and later reconnects it to the conditions of performance. Specify the target decision or movement, the level of challenge, and the feedback available. If a drill removes all the pressure, timing, or choices that define the sport, success in the drill may transfer poorly. Progress from controlled practice to more representative play under qualified supervision and suitable safety rules.
CONNECT: [[07 Learning, memory, and understanding]] distinguishes recall from transfer. [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] asks what observation would show the drill helped.
EXAMPLE: A passing drill begins without defenders, then adds movement and a choice of receiver so the player practices looking before passing.
DO: Describe one drill's target skill, simplified condition, and later real-play condition.
TRY: Choose one observation that would show transfer beyond the drill.
REFLECT: Was I improving the drill score or the sport decision?
CHECK: What makes a drill transferable?::It connects an isolated skill to the cues, timing, and choices of actual performance.
MODEL: Isolate~Practice one component.|Progress~Add representative demands.|Check~Look for change in play.

## Use video and statistics without losing context
QUESTION: What can a recorded clip or number show, and what might it hide?
CORE: Video can reveal positioning and timing that memory misses, while a statistic can track patterns across attempts. Neither explains the whole action on its own. Note opponents, role, score, rule setting, and what the camera could not see. Choose a small number of measures connected to decisions; a highlight reel selects dramatic moments and may hide ordinary performance. Use review to identify a practice target, not to assign fixed labels to a player.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] warns about selected samples. [[26 Economics, incentives, and public choices]] shows that measures can change behavior.
EXAMPLE: A player's low pass completion may reflect attempts into difficult spaces rather than poor technique alone; video helps examine both choice and execution.
DO: Pick a sport statistic and list two contextual facts needed to interpret it.
TRY: Review one clip and write one observation separately from an explanation.
REFLECT: Which conclusion arrived before I had checked the role and context?
CHECK: Why pair sport statistics with context?::Numbers and clips show selected parts of performance and need role, conditions, and opportunity to be interpreted fairly.
MODEL: Measure~Choose a relevant pattern.|Contextualize~Add role and conditions.|Practice~Select a specific next target.

## Make the training setting respectful and safe
QUESTION: What conditions let participants learn without unnecessary harm or humiliation?
CORE: Good coaching matches challenge to experience, explains rules, and welcomes a stop or adjustment when a participant is uncomfortable or injured. Contact sports require qualified supervision, protective norms, and consent to the kind of practice being used. Feedback should describe a changeable action rather than shame a person's identity. A learner can be ambitious while still declining a drill that exceeds their current preparation or the agreed rules.
CONNECT: [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] supports useful feedback. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] treats consent and dignity as constraints on performance goals.
EXAMPLE: A martial arts class pairs beginners for a controlled drill, explains the stop signal, and changes intensity when someone reports a problem.
DO: Write three conditions for a safe learning environment in one sport.
TRY: Rewrite one shaming coaching comment as specific, actionable feedback.
REFLECT: Did the culture of the setting make it easy for people to report a problem?
CHECK: What does a safe sport learning setting provide?::Clear rules, appropriate supervision and progression, consent, specific feedback, and room to stop or adjust.
MODEL: Set~Agree on rules and intensity.|Practice~Match challenge to readiness.|Respond~Adjust when safety or dignity requires it.
