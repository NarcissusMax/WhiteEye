## Reduce the data you leave behind
QUESTION: Which information needs to be shared, stored, or kept at all?
CORE: Privacy improves when fewer unnecessary copies of sensitive data exist. Review what an account or service asks for, how long you retain records, and who can access them. Keep what is needed for lawful obligations and useful recovery, and remove or restrict what has no clear purpose. Deletion is not always complete or immediate, so do not promise invisibility. A practical privacy choice balances function, safety, and the consequences of losing needed records.
CONNECT: [[29 Safety, awareness, and de-escalation]] uses proportionate risk planning. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] asks about other people's information entrusted to you.
EXAMPLE: A community organizer collects a contact method for event updates but avoids requesting unrelated personal details and limits who can open the list.
DO: Review a hypothetical sign-up form and identify fields that lack a clear purpose.
TRY: Write a simple retention rule for the remaining information.
REFLECT: What data did I keep because storage was easy rather than because it served a need?
CHECK: What is data minimization?::Collecting, sharing, and retaining only information needed for a defined purpose and obligation.
MODEL: Purpose~State why data is needed.|Limit~Reduce fields and access.|Review~Remove what no longer serves the purpose.

## Prepare for account recovery before losing access
QUESTION: Could I recover an important account or device without exposing it further?
CORE: Strong account protection is incomplete if recovery depends on a lost device, forgotten code, or unavailable contact. Check recovery methods for essential accounts, keep backup codes or records in a protected place, and ensure trusted contacts have only the access they legitimately need. Test that recovery information is current without deliberately locking yourself out. Treat recovery messages as another place where impersonation can occur; verify through known channels.
CONNECT: [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] treats continuity as part of a handover. [[25 Money, records, and financial judgment]] benefits from reliable access to essential records.
EXAMPLE: Someone replaces a phone and discovers that a second factor was tied only to the old device. A prepared recovery method would have prevented a long lockout.
DO: Make a private inventory of account recovery methods without recording secrets in the course notebook.
TRY: Identify one single point of failure and a safe backup approach.
REFLECT: Which account would cause the most disruption if recovery failed today?
CHECK: Why plan account recovery?::It keeps legitimate access possible when a device or method fails without weakening ordinary protection.
MODEL: Identify~Find critical accounts.|Back up~Protect recovery options.|Check~Keep methods current.

## Review app permissions as a privacy choice
QUESTION: Does an app need the access it currently has to provide the function I use?
CORE: Permissions can grant access to location, contacts, camera, files, or other data. Review them in the actual device settings and compare each with the feature you use. Some access may be needed only while a feature is active; other requests may be unnecessary. Changing a permission can break a feature, so test the result and choose knowingly. Do not infer that a permission alone proves malicious intent; examine the service and its behavior in context.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] cautions against overinterpreting a signal. [[30.05 Understand privacy as a set of choices]] connects privacy to trade-offs.
EXAMPLE: A weather app may use location for local forecasts, but a person can decide whether to give continuous access or enter a city manually.
DO: In a hypothetical app list, label each requested permission by purpose, alternative, and concern.
TRY: Review one real app's permissions locally without sharing personal details in the notebook.
REFLECT: Which permission did I grant once and never reconsider?
CHECK: How should an app permission be judged?::By the feature it enables, the access it grants, available alternatives, and the user's chosen trade-off.
MODEL: Inspect~See what access exists.|Compare~Match access to use.|Adjust~Change and test settings.

## Practice a calm response to a suspicious message
QUESTION: What steps preserve options when a message asks for urgent action?
CORE: Urgency, secrecy, and requests for credentials or payment are reasons to pause and verify. Do not use a link or number supplied in the same suspicious message as your sole proof. Check through a known official channel, keep relevant evidence, and report through the service's established process if appropriate. If you already acted, change the relevant access and contact the affected provider through trusted means. A rehearsed response is more useful than trying to detect every trick by appearance alone.
CONNECT: [[18 Manipulation, propaganda, and independent judgment]] examines pressure tactics. [[29 Safety, awareness, and de-escalation]] favors concrete options over speculation.
EXAMPLE: A text claims a parcel requires immediate payment. The recipient opens the delivery service independently instead of following the text's link.

TRY: Identify what evidence to preserve if the message is reported.
REFLECT: Which element of urgency made the request feel more credible than it was?
CHECK: What is the safest first response to an unexpected urgent account or payment request?::Pause and verify through a known independent channel before acting.
MODEL: Pause~Avoid immediate response.|Verify~Use a trusted route.|Recover~Report or secure access if needed.
