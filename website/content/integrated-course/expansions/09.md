## Stop sunk costs from making the next choice
QUESTION: What should I do when I have already spent time or money on a poor route?
CORE: A past cost can explain why a choice matters emotionally, but it cannot be recovered by repeating a bad choice. Compare the future benefits and costs of continuing with those of changing course. Include switching costs, obligations, and learning already gained; do not pretend they are zero. The key distinction is whether a consideration changes what happens next or only makes you want to justify what happened before.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] provides a decision frame. [[23 Creative work and sustained projects]] adds a limit: finishing a difficult project may still have future value. Sunk-cost reasoning is a warning to assess that value honestly, not a command to quit.
EXAMPLE: You paid for a course that teaches the wrong skill. Completing it might still provide a useful credential, or it may consume six weeks needed for a better route. The fee is gone either way; the coming six weeks are the decision.
DO: Choose one commitment you hesitate to revise because of what you already invested. Separate past costs from future consequences.
TRY: Compare continuing and changing using only what each option can still produce.
REFLECT: Which past investment was I trying to make feel worthwhile?
CHECK: What is the sunk-cost mistake?::Allowing an irrecoverable past investment to decide a future choice when it does not change the options' future value.
MODEL: Separate~Mark costs already incurred.|Compare~Evaluate future options and switching costs.|Choose~Act on the better forward path.

## Check availability before judging frequency
QUESTION: Am I treating a memorable example as a common event?
CORE: Stories that are vivid, recent, frightening, or easy to recall can feel frequent. That feeling may be useful as a signal to investigate, but it is not a count. Ask what population and time period the claim concerns, how cases were selected, and whether a base rate is available. Sometimes reliable statistics are unavailable; in that case, say what you do not know rather than replacing data with a single memorable case.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] explains how to judge a sample. [[29 Safety, awareness, and de-escalation]] reminds you that a low-frequency event may still merit preparation when its cost is high. Frequency and consequence are different dimensions.
EXAMPLE: After seeing a dramatic report about a travel incident, you consider cancelling every trip. You check how often comparable incidents occurred on the route and what practical precautions are relevant. The story remains real without becoming the whole risk estimate.
DO: Identify a judgment influenced by one vivid example. State the population and time frame you would need to estimate frequency.
TRY: Find a reliable count if available; otherwise write an explicit uncertainty statement and a proportionate precaution.
REFLECT: What made the example memorable, and did that change its frequency?
CHECK: Why is recall ease a weak frequency measure?::Vividness and recency affect memory, while frequency requires information about the wider set of cases.
MODEL: Notice~Name the memorable example.|Count~Seek a relevant base rate or sample.|Calibrate~Separate likelihood from consequence.

## Keep hindsight from rewriting the decision
QUESTION: How can I judge an earlier choice after I know how it turned out?
CORE: Once an outcome is known, the path toward it can appear more obvious than it was. Preserve the information available at the decision time: options, forecasts, warnings, and reasons. Then review the decision against that record, not against facts learned later. A lucky outcome can hide a weak process, and an unlucky outcome can follow a sound one. Hindsight is most useful when it identifies a warning you realistically could have noticed.
CONNECT: [[12.03 Separate decision quality from outcome quality]] makes the core distinction. [[40 Integration and the life-practice capstone]] uses dated decision records so later learning does not become a fictional memory of certainty.
EXAMPLE: A project failed after a supplier closed unexpectedly. If the supplier's financial instability was visible beforehand, the plan may have missed a risk. If not, the better lesson may be to build a backup option rather than to claim you should have predicted that closure.
DO: Review one past decision. Reconstruct what you knew before the result without using later information.
TRY: Identify one warning that was genuinely available and one fact that only became known afterward.
REFLECT: Did the outcome make my past confidence look more justified or less justified than it was?
CHECK: How does a decision record limit hindsight bias?::It preserves the information and reasons available before the outcome was known.
MODEL: Freeze~Reconstruct the earlier information.|Review~Separate process from result.|Learn~Change only what could improve a future choice.

## Calibrate confidence with a track record
QUESTION: How can I know whether my certainty is earned?
CORE: Confidence is a feeling about a judgment; calibration asks how often similar judgments prove correct. For repeatable, low-stakes predictions, record your estimate before the result and compare over time. Exact percentages are unnecessary if you lack enough cases; broad bands such as unlikely, uncertain, and likely can still expose a pattern. Look for topics where you are too certain and topics where you underrate yourself. The point is to improve decisions, not to perform permanent doubt.
CONNECT: [[07 Learning, memory, and understanding]] uses feedback to correct errors. [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] supplies the statistical caution: a few outcomes do not establish a stable rate. Use calibration for recurring predictions, not a single unrepeatable life event.
EXAMPLE: You regularly predict that a work task will take one hour. A month of estimates and actual durations shows it usually takes two. Revising the estimate gives better plans without changing your worth.
DO: Record five low-stakes predictions with a confidence band and a result date.
TRY: When results arrive, compare what happened with your bands and identify one domain to estimate differently.
REFLECT: Where did confidence track experience, and where did it track desire?
CHECK: What is calibration?::The match between expressed confidence and how often comparable judgments prove correct over time.
MODEL: Forecast~Record an estimate before the result.|Compare~Check many comparable outcomes.|Adjust~Revise future confidence and plans.

## Notice regression after an extreme result
QUESTION: Did my intervention cause a return to normal, or might the extreme result have changed anyway?
CORE: When a measure varies, unusually high or low results are often followed by more ordinary ones even without a new intervention. This is regression toward the mean. It does not prove an intervention failed or succeeded; it warns against giving it automatic credit for improvement after an extreme event. Compare repeated observations and, when possible, a relevant comparison group. Look for a plausible mechanism and timing.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] explains why a before-and-after story may be weak evidence. [[27 Physical training, recovery, and nutrition literacy]] is a common setting: one exceptional workout or bad week need not define a trend.
EXAMPLE: A team changes its meeting format after its worst month and sees better results next month. The change may have helped, but the previous month was unusually poor; the team needs more data before assigning full credit.
DO: Find a recent extreme result followed by a more ordinary one. List possible causes besides the action taken in between.
TRY: Choose two additional measures or comparison points that would strengthen the explanation.
REFLECT: Did I give the most visible intervention more credit than the evidence allowed?
CHECK: What is regression toward the mean?::Extreme observations in a variable process tend to be followed by less extreme observations, which can mislead simple before-and-after judgments.
MODEL: Spot~Identify the extreme starting point.|Compare~Examine repeated and comparison data.|Infer~Avoid automatic causal credit.
