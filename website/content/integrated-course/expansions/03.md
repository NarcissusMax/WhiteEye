## Turn worry into a bounded planning task
QUESTION: When does thinking about a problem help, and when does it only repeat alarm?
CORE: Worry can draw attention to something that matters, but repeating the same possibilities without new information may not improve a decision. State the concern in one sentence, identify what is known, and separate a next action from a scenario you cannot presently control. Give planning a defined time and result: a question to ask, a contingency to prepare, or a date to revisit. If the concern remains intense or disruptive, that is itself information worth bringing to qualified support.
CONNECT: [[06 Attention, time, and a workable day]] provides a place for the planning task. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] helps choose a reversible action under uncertainty. Neither lesson asks you to suppress the feeling.
EXAMPLE: You keep imagining that a bill will arrive unexpectedly. A bounded task is to check the account, note the due date, and decide whom to contact if the amount differs. Rehearsing catastrophe after those steps adds no new information.
DO: Write one recurring worry as a question. Mark what you know, what you can check, and what is currently unknowable.
TRY: Complete one useful check or contingency, then record whether the next round of thinking adds anything.
REFLECT: What did the worry ask me to protect, and what action actually helped?
CHECK: What makes planning different from rumination?::Planning produces a bounded question, action, or contingency; rumination repeats possibilities without adding usable information.
MODEL: Name~State the concern.|Bound~Choose a check or contingency.|Stop~Return only when new information or the review time arrives.

## Choose an emotion response for its purpose
QUESTION: Which way of responding to a feeling helps this situation?
CORE: There is no single correct move for every emotion. A feeling may call for rest, a clarifying question, a boundary, a repair, or time before deciding. Notice the bodily and mental cues, then ask what the situation requires. Expression can communicate a need; reflection can test an interpretation; acceptance can prevent a struggle with the feeling itself. A strategy that helps in one context can become avoidance in another. Judge it by whether it supports an appropriate next action, not whether it makes the feeling vanish immediately.
CONNECT: [[04 Agency, responsibility, and boundaries]] clarifies which action is yours. [[31 Mind, behavior, and consciousness]] cautions against reducing an experience to one simple brain story. The practical question is what response remains respectful and safe.
EXAMPLE: After a tense meeting, immediate confrontation may be unwise. Taking ten minutes, writing down the disputed point, and asking for a follow-up can preserve the issue without escalating it.
DO: List three responses you commonly use when upset. For each, note when it helps and when it creates a cost.
TRY: In a mild real situation, choose a different response because it fits the purpose, then observe the result.
REFLECT: Was I trying to understand the feeling, communicate something, or get rid of it?
CHECK: How should an emotion response be judged?::By its fit with the situation and the action it supports, not only by immediate relief.
MODEL: Notice~Identify cues and context.|Choose~Match a response to the purpose.|Review~Observe what the response made possible.

## Separate pain from the story about pain
QUESTION: How can I take pain seriously without assuming I know its full cause?
CORE: Pain is a real experience, but its intensity, timing, and meaning can vary with injury, illness, stress, attention, prior experience, and other conditions. A personal explanation may guide a question, yet it should not replace assessment when symptoms are new, severe, persistent, or concerning. Describe what happened, where and when the pain occurs, what changes it, and how it affects function. That record is more useful than a single confident label. Do not use psychological language to dismiss physical care needs.
CONNECT: [[37 Health literacy and responsible care]] covers how to bring observations to a clinician. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] helps separate a sensation, a pattern, and a causal claim. The course does not diagnose pain.
EXAMPLE: You develop recurring shoulder pain while training. Calling it weakness or stress would be premature. Record the activity, movement, duration, and change over time, and seek appropriate assessment rather than increasing the load by guesswork.
DO: For a non-urgent discomfort, write a dated description of onset, pattern, context, and effect on daily activity.
TRY: List two questions a qualified professional could answer and one assumption you should not treat as established.
REFLECT: Which part of my explanation is observed, and which part is a hypothesis?
CHECK: Why is a pain record more useful than a confident label?::It preserves timing, conditions, and effects that can inform assessment without pretending to establish the cause.
MODEL: Describe~Record the experience and context.|Avoid~Do not turn a guess into a diagnosis.|Seek~Use qualified assessment when appropriate.
BOOKS: B280 B098
