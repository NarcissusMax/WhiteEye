## Turn a chapter into answerable questions
QUESTION: How do I find out whether I understood a reading rather than merely recognized it?
CORE: Before or after reading a section, write questions it should help you answer. Include at least one question about a claim, one about how it works, and one about its limits. Close the book and answer in your own words. Check what you missed, especially distinctions you blurred. Recognition while looking at a page can feel like mastery, but retrieval shows what you can use without the page present.
CONNECT: [[10 Logic, arguments, and fair disagreement]] helps ask about support and assumptions. [[08 Practice, performance, and adaptation]] turns retrieval errors into the next practice target. Not every chapter contains an answer to every question; that absence is also useful information.
EXAMPLE: After reading about opportunity cost, you ask what choice is being compared, what the next-best option is, and whether the example includes non-money costs. Your answer reveals that you remembered the slogan but omitted the alternative.
DO: Choose a short chapter and write three answerable questions before rereading it.
TRY: Answer without the text, check each response, and revise one question that was too vague.
REFLECT: Which idea felt familiar but was difficult to explain from memory?
CHECK: Why use self-written questions during reading?::They make the intended understanding explicit and create a retrieval test beyond recognition.
MODEL: Ask~Write claim, mechanism, and limit questions.|Recall~Answer with the text closed.|Check~Correct gaps and improve the questions.

## Design retrieval cues that survive forgetting
QUESTION: What will help me find an idea again after its original context is gone?
CORE: A cue should point clearly to the information you need, yet not contain the whole answer. A location in a memory palace, a distinctive image, a question, or a well-chosen example can make retrieval easier. Test the cue after a delay. If it retrieves several competing items or nothing at all, revise it. Memorized material still needs understanding and external verification when accuracy matters. A vivid cue is a route to an idea, not proof that the idea is true.
CONNECT: [[31 Mind, behavior, and consciousness]] reminds us that memory is reconstructive. [[35 Science, models, and technical literacy]] distinguishes a tool for recall from evidence for a claim. Use cues especially where exact recall serves a real task.
EXAMPLE: To remember three stages of a process, you place distinct images at three familiar locations. The next day, the second image recalls only a vague feeling. You replace it with a more specific image and test again.
DO: Build three cues for three ideas you need next week. Write the intended answer beside each cue.
TRY: Test after twenty-four hours and note which cue was ambiguous. Revise that cue rather than rereading everything.
REFLECT: Did the cue help me retrieve an understood idea or only a label?
CHECK: How do you know a memory cue works?::It reliably retrieves the intended information after a delay without confusing it with similar items.
MODEL: Encode~Choose a distinctive cue.|Delay~Attempt retrieval later.|Repair~Revise ambiguous or missing cues.

## Troubleshoot a memory palace
QUESTION: What should I change when a memory journey stops working?
CORE: A memory palace is a route through familiar locations with items placed at distinct points. Failures can come from crowded locations, an unclear order, similar images, or trying to store material you do not understand. Rewalk the route and identify where retrieval breaks. Repair that point: make the image more distinctive, move it, split a crowded location, or learn the concept before encoding it. Keep a separate record for high-stakes facts, because the method can fail like any other memory aid.
CONNECT: [[07.12 Design retrieval cues that survive forgetting]] focuses on individual cues. [[07.06 Space practice and revisit errors]] adds delayed checks. The useful question is not whether the method is powerful in theory but whether this route works for this material and learner.
EXAMPLE: You remember a list of ten concepts but confuse items four and five because both were placed near the same doorway. Giving each a separate landmark and testing the route backward clarifies the sequence.
DO: Build a five-location route with five familiar items. Test it forward after a delay and mark the first failure.
TRY: Change only the failed location or cue, then test again the next day.
REFLECT: Was the problem the route, the cue, or my understanding of the item?
CHECK: What should be diagnosed when a memory palace fails?::The order and distinctness of locations and images, plus whether the underlying material is understood.
MODEL: Locate~Find the exact retrieval break.|Repair~Change cue, location, or understanding.|Retest~Check after a delay.

## Teach back a procedure without skipping decisions
QUESTION: Can I explain when to use each step, not only repeat its name?
CORE: A procedure includes choices about when to begin, what to check, what to do next, and when to stop or seek help. Teach it to an imagined beginner using a normal case and one exception. If you cannot explain why a step occurs or what changes in the exception, return to the source and identify the missing distinction. Teaching back is a test of understanding, not a license to instruct someone in a high-risk task from memory alone.
CONNECT: [[35 Science, models, and technical literacy]] emphasizes scope and assumptions. [[37 Health literacy and responsible care]] shows why a textbook procedure and qualified supervision are different. The method is most useful for ordinary study and safe practice.
EXAMPLE: You teach someone how to reconcile a small expense record. You can name the entries but cannot explain what to do when a receipt is missing. That exception shows what you need to learn next.
DO: Select a low-risk procedure you are studying. Explain its start condition, steps, checks, and stop condition aloud.
TRY: Add one realistic exception and explain how the procedure changes. Mark any step you cannot justify.
REFLECT: Which decision point did I omit when I first explained the procedure?
CHECK: What does teaching back reveal about a procedure?::Whether you understand its conditions and decision points rather than only remembering step names.
MODEL: Explain~Teach the normal case.|Vary~Introduce one exception.|Check~Return to gaps and limits.
BOOKS: B222 B265
