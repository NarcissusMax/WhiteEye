## Ask which behavior a theory predicts
QUESTION: How could I tell whether a psychological explanation is helping?
CORE: A theory becomes more useful when it predicts a difference you could observe or when it organizes evidence better than alternatives. Name the behavior, the proposed mechanism, and a rival explanation. Avoid labeling a person from one action or using a theory to claim access to hidden motives. Context, self-report, and repeated observations matter. A model can be useful without explaining every case, and a failed prediction is an opportunity to narrow its scope.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] asks for discriminating evidence. [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] protects the person's account from being overwritten by a favored theory.
EXAMPLE: Someone misses a meeting. Competing explanations include fear, scheduling conflict, and simple oversight; a respectful check is more informative than a diagnosis.
DO: Write two explanations for a hypothetical behavior and one different prediction from each.
TRY: Draft a question that checks the person's account without implying a diagnosis.
REFLECT: What did my preferred theory make too easy to assume?
CHECK: What improves a psychological explanation?::Clear scope, competing alternatives, and observations that could distinguish the explanations.
MODEL: Describe~Name the behavior.|Compare~List mechanisms.|Check~Seek relevant evidence and the person's account.

## Notice how body state can shape judgment
QUESTION: Which bodily and emotional conditions may affect a decision without determining it?
CORE: Fatigue, arousal, pain, and emotion can influence what feels urgent or possible. They are part of the decision context, not evidence that a thought is automatically false. Pause before a high-stakes choice when conditions are unusually intense, and record what matters about the situation. If symptoms persist or impair functioning, consider qualified care. A book about brain and emotion offers models, not a way to diagnose yourself from one day.
CONNECT: [[03 Emotions, acceptance, and a steadier response]] makes room to notice feelings. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] adds decision stakes and timing.
EXAMPLE: After a sleepless night, a manager drafts a difficult reply but waits to send it until they can review its facts and tone.
DO: Identify two conditions that affect your decision quality and one low-cost response for each.
TRY: Create a pause rule for a consequential decision when one of those conditions is present.
REFLECT: Did I treat a body state as irrelevant or as a complete explanation?
CHECK: How should body state enter judgment?::As relevant context that may justify a pause or support, without automatically invalidating or determining the decision.
MODEL: Notice~Identify the state.|Pause~Match response to stakes.|Review~Check reasons and options.

## Separate a memory from an inference about it
QUESTION: What part of this recollection do I remember, and what part have I concluded later?
CORE: Remembering reconstructs an event using cues and later knowledge. Confidence and vividness do not always show accuracy. For a consequential event, write the earliest account available, mark direct observations, and note later interpretations separately. Seek independent records when appropriate, while respecting privacy and the limits of those records. Do not use uncertainty about memory to dismiss a person's experience; use it to be precise about what a claim can establish.
CONNECT: [[07 Learning, memory, and understanding]] studies retrieval. [[39 History, citizenship, and public institutions]] asks how records and later narratives interact.
EXAMPLE: A team member remembers that everyone agreed to a deadline, but meeting notes show the date was proposed and never confirmed.
DO: Rewrite a hypothetical recollection into observed detail, later interpretation, and missing information.
TRY: Name one independent record that could help without claiming it captures the whole experience.
REFLECT: Which later conclusion had merged with my memory of the event?
CHECK: Why separate recollection from later inference?::Memory can incorporate later meaning, so the distinction makes a consequential account clearer and more checkable.
MODEL: Recall~State what is remembered.|Mark~Separate observation and inference.|Check~Compare appropriate records.

## Use a mental model without turning it into an identity
QUESTION: When is a label helping me act, and when is it narrowing a person?
CORE: Categories can help organize patterns and ask for relevant support. They can also make changeable behavior appear permanent, hide context, or invite amateur diagnosis. State what the model explains, what it does not, and whose expertise is needed for a clinical claim. Address the specific situation and person in front of you rather than treating a category as a full biography. Let a model earn its usefulness through better questions and outcomes.
CONNECT: [[16 Family, development, and relational patterns]] avoids turning early patterns into destiny. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] treats dignity as a constraint on judgment.
EXAMPLE: A teacher notices a student struggles to start tasks and changes the support offered; they do not infer a diagnosis from one classroom pattern.
DO: Take a broad behavior label and rewrite it as a specific observation with context.
TRY: List two alternative explanations or support questions the label might obscure.
REFLECT: Did the category help me respond or only make me feel certain?
CHECK: What is a safe use of a psychological category?::Use it as a limited question or organizing tool, while preserving context, individual variation, and clinical boundaries.
MODEL: Observe~Describe a specific pattern.|Limit~Name what the label cannot show.|Support~Choose an appropriate next question or resource.
