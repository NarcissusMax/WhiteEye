## Review rituals as evidence of priorities
QUESTION: What do my ordinary routines reveal that my stated priorities miss?
CORE: A ritual is a repeated sequence that gives a day some shape. It can serve a commitment, make a transition easier, or simply repeat because the conditions are familiar. Describe a routine in order, including where it happens, who is present, and what follows. Then compare its actual effect with the purpose you claim for it. A morning ritual that makes you late has a different effect from the calm you hope it creates. Do not judge the routine by its name or by a single unusually good or bad day.
CONNECT: Personal reflection becomes more useful when it meets the behavior loop in [[05 Habits and behavior design]]. A routine can be both meaningful and costly. Record the trade-off before deciding whether to keep, change, or remove it.
EXAMPLE: You say that reading the news every morning keeps you informed. A week of notes shows that it consumes forty minutes and rarely changes a decision. You may still value the practice, but its cost and benefit are now visible.
DO: Record one daily ritual for five days, noting its trigger, sequence, duration, and aftereffect.
TRY: State one reason to keep it and one reason to revise it. Test a small change for two days.
REFLECT: Which routine expressed a value, and which merely occupied the time around it?
CHECK: Why inspect a routine before changing it?::Its actual conditions, effects, and trade-offs may differ from the story you tell about it.
MODEL: Observe~Describe the sequence as it happens.|Compare~Set actual effects beside intended purpose.|Revise~Keep or test one small change.

## Ask whose standard is being applied
QUESTION: When I say I am behind, whose timetable am I using?
CORE: A self-judgment often borrows its standard from a peer group, family expectation, profession, or public image. That standard may contain a useful warning, but it may also assume resources and obligations you do not have. Name the comparison before accepting the conclusion. Ask who set the timeline, what it measures, and which costs it leaves out. A difference between your path and another person's path is not, by itself, a deficit.
CONNECT: [[02 Values, meaning, and a direction worth choosing]] helps decide which standards deserve authority. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] helps check whether the people you compare yourself with form a fair sample. A value and a comparison answer different questions.
EXAMPLE: A friend owns a home while you rent and support a parent. The comparison may prompt a useful financial review. It does not establish that your choices were less responsible, because the obligations and resources were different.
DO: Write down one sentence in which you call yourself late, behind, or unsuccessful. Identify the implied comparison group.
TRY: Replace the borrowed timetable with a criterion tied to your own obligation and next decision.
REFLECT: Which standard would I still choose if nobody could see whether I met it?
CHECK: What should be checked before adopting a comparison as a verdict?::Who set the standard, what it measures, and which differences in circumstances it ignores.
MODEL: Name~Identify the borrowed standard.|Inspect~Check what it includes and omits.|Choose~Keep only a criterion useful for your next decision.
BOOKS: B003 B048

## Ask another person for a concrete outside view
QUESTION: How can I learn from someone else's view without handing them authority over my identity?
CORE: Other people see behavior that you cannot observe directly, especially how your actions affect them. Their reports are valuable but partial: they come from particular encounters, expectations, and memories. Ask for an example rather than a global rating. Invite the person to describe what happened, what helped or hindered, and what they would prefer next time. Compare several accounts where possible. You can take a report seriously without treating it as a complete definition of yourself.
CONNECT: This extends the earlier lesson on feedback into [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]]. The same question can reveal a blind spot and clarify a relationship. It remains important to distinguish what the person observed from their interpretation.
EXAMPLE: Instead of asking whether you are a good teammate, ask a colleague which part of the last shared task made coordination easy or difficult. The answer points to a behavior you can test.
DO: Ask one trusted person for a recent example of something you did that helped them work with you.
TRY: Ask for one example of friction. Write both accounts without defending or immediately accepting either explanation.
REFLECT: What did the outside view show, and what remains uncertain about it?
CHECK: Why request an example instead of a personality verdict?::An example ties feedback to observable behavior and a specific context that you can examine or change.
MODEL: Ask~Invite a concrete recent example.|Separate~Distinguish observation from explanation.|Use~Choose one behavior to test.
BOOKS: B003 B026
