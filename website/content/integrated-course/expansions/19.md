## Decide what a leader should delegate
QUESTION: Which decisions need my judgment, and which need another person's expertise or authority?
CORE: Delegation is more than handing off a task. Clarify the desired result, decision rights, resources, constraints, and check-in point. Give the person enough authority to do the work, and remain accountable for the system you created. Keeping every decision can slow a team; delegating without support can set someone up to fail. The right division depends on risk, capability, and who is affected.
CONNECT: [[17 Influence, persuasion, and negotiation]] helps agree on scope. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] shows why local decisions need a clear connection to the larger goal. Delegation should increase useful judgment across the team.
EXAMPLE: A coordinator asks a specialist to select a tool within a budget and privacy requirement. The specialist compares options; the coordinator secures approval for the budget and communicates the decision to affected users.
DO: Pick one team task and write result, decision rights, available resources, and one check-in date.
TRY: Ask the person doing the work which decision they cannot make under the current arrangement.
REFLECT: Did I retain control out of necessity or discomfort with another person's judgment?
CHECK: What must be clear in a delegated task?::The outcome, authority, constraints, resources, and review point, with accountability retained by the leader for the system.
MODEL: Define~State the result and boundaries.|Empower~Give appropriate decision rights.|Review~Check progress and remove obstacles.

## Make dissent safe before a decision
QUESTION: How can a team surface a concern before agreement hardens?
CORE: A request for honest disagreement is weak if people expect punishment or ridicule for offering it. Ask for specific risks and alternatives before announcing a preferred answer. Give quieter members time to prepare or a channel to respond. State what would change the decision and explain afterward how the input was used. Safety for dissent does not mean every objection wins; it means concerns can be raised and evaluated without personal retaliation.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] describes groupthink and confirmation. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] adds the duty to hear people who bear the consequences. Better decisions need a social process that admits contrary information.
EXAMPLE: A team plans a new schedule. The manager asks each role what could fail and collects written concerns before giving their own preference. One concern about access needs changes the rollout plan.
DO: For one decision involving others, ask what could go wrong before presenting your preferred option.
TRY: Write a short decision note showing which concerns changed the plan and which did not, with reasons.
REFLECT: Did the process make disagreement usable or merely invite it ceremonially?
CHECK: What makes an invitation to dissent credible?::People can raise specific concerns without retaliation and can see how those concerns were evaluated.
MODEL: Invite~Ask for risks before the answer hardens.|Evaluate~Compare concerns with evidence.|Explain~Show the resulting decision and reasons.

## Track the work without turning people into numbers
QUESTION: How can a team use metrics while still seeing the quality of its work?
CORE: A metric measures a selected aspect of a process. It can help detect change, but once used as a target it may invite shortcuts or neglect of unmeasured duties. Pair each measure with a qualitative check, a possible unintended effect, and a review date. Ask people doing the work what the number misses. Stop collecting a measure that does not improve a decision.
CONNECT: [[12.06 Beware local optimization]] describes the systemic risk. [[24 Business, customers, and useful value]] asks whether measured output becomes useful value for a customer. Accountability needs interpretation, not just a dashboard.
EXAMPLE: A service team reduces response time by closing tickets quickly, but customers need repeated follow-ups. Looking at resolution quality and reopen rates reveals the trade-off.
DO: Choose one team metric and list what it captures, what it misses, and how it could be gamed.
TRY: Add one qualitative question or countermeasure that protects the real purpose.
REFLECT: Did the metric help people learn or mainly reward an appearance of success?
CHECK: Why pair a metric with a countercheck?::A single target can improve its number while hiding a decline in the larger outcome it was meant to serve.
MODEL: Measure~Track a useful signal.|Countercheck~Watch quality and side effects.|Revise~Keep measures that improve decisions.
