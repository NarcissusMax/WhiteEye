## Define evidence before a proposed change
QUESTION: What would count as improvement before I know which option wins?
CORE: When the measure is chosen after a trial, almost any result can be called a success. State the outcome, a baseline, and the observation window before acting. Include an experience measure from the people affected when a numerical target could hide inconvenience or exclusion. Decide what change would be meaningful, and mark any evidence that would make you stop. A measure should help judgment, not become a substitute for it.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] separates a measure from the thing measured. [[12.06 Beware local optimization]] shows why a single improved number can mislead.
EXAMPLE: Before changing a sign-up process, a volunteer group records completed sign-ups, the time applicants spend, and reasons for abandonment.
DO: Write one outcome measure, one experience measure, and a time window for a small change.
TRY: State a result that would count against your favored fix.
REFLECT: Would I have been tempted to choose the measure after seeing the result?
CHECK: Why define evidence before a change?::It makes the comparison clearer and reduces the temptation to redefine success after seeing the outcome.
MODEL: Baseline~Record the current result.|Define~Set measures and a review date.|Compare~Judge the result against both benefit and burden.

## Choose a reversible first step
QUESTION: How can I learn while limiting the cost of being wrong?
CORE: Not every solution requires a complete rollout. When uncertainty is high, look for a bounded step that produces information while preserving an exit. Reversibility depends on more than money: trust, privacy, and disruption may be hard to restore. Tell affected people what is being tried and what would trigger revision. Where even a small trial carries substantial harm, seek appropriate expertise and oversight before acting.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] compares options by consequences and reversibility. [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] turns a chosen trial into a coordinated plan.
EXAMPLE: A team tries new meeting notes for two weeks with an agreed review, instead of permanently changing every reporting process at once.
DO: Break one large proposed change into a limited first step with an exit condition.
TRY: List what would be easy and hard to undo after that step.
REFLECT: Did I call a choice reversible because it was convenient for me, while someone else bore a lasting cost?
CHECK: What makes a first step truly reversible?::Its likely costs, including trust and disruption, can be contained or repaired if the idea fails.
MODEL: Bound~Limit the scope.|Protect~Name difficult-to-reverse effects.|Review~Use a prearranged decision point.

## Ask whether a fix works beyond the pilot
QUESTION: Would the same approach work under normal demand and ordinary constraints?
CORE: A pilot often receives unusual attention, motivated participants, or spare capacity. A good result in that setting may not survive routine use. Identify which conditions made the trial possible and whether they exist at a larger scale. Watch for bottlenecks that move when volume rises. Scale in stages and keep a way to notice decline in quality. A failed transfer can reveal a capacity limit rather than an error in the original observation.
CONNECT: [[12.05 Use feedback loops to see change over time]] asks where the constraint moves. [[24 Business, customers, and useful value]] asks whether value can be delivered consistently.
EXAMPLE: A helpful personal reply works for ten customers a week; at two hundred, the team needs a different process or more capacity.
DO: List three conditions that made a successful small test work.
TRY: Imagine demand doubling and identify the first likely bottleneck.
REFLECT: Which part of the pilot depended on special attention that cannot continue?
CHECK: Why can a successful pilot fail at scale?::Its conditions, attention, or capacity may change when the approach becomes routine.
MODEL: Identify~Name pilot conditions.|Stress~Test the likely bottleneck.|Expand~Increase scope while monitoring quality.

## Keep a decision record others can challenge
QUESTION: Can another person see why this fix was chosen and what would change my mind?
CORE: A short decision record captures the problem, options, evidence, uncertainties, affected people, chosen action, and review date. It is a tool for later learning and accountability, not a defense brief written to prove you were right. Name the strongest alternative and an observation that would favor it. Invite someone affected to correct the record. When outcomes arrive, add them without rewriting the earlier reasoning to fit hindsight.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] shows how hindsight distorts memory. [[39 History, citizenship, and public institutions]] shows why decisions that affect others need records that can be examined.
EXAMPLE: A team records why it changed office hours, whose access may be affected, and which attendance and complaint patterns will prompt review.
DO: Write a six-line decision record for a real low-stakes choice.
TRY: Ask one person to identify a missing option or affected group.
REFLECT: What did an outside reader see that my original explanation hid?
CHECK: What makes a decision record useful for learning?::It preserves the original reasons, alternatives, uncertainties, and review conditions for later comparison.
MODEL: Record~Capture the reasoning now.|Challenge~Invite correction.|Review~Compare outcomes without changing the old record.
