## Follow a policy through incentives and response
QUESTION: How might people change behavior when a rule changes?
CORE: A policy can alter prices, access, rewards, and the cost of compliance. Predict more than the intended direct effect: consider how households, firms, officials, and excluded groups might respond. A plausible incentive story is a hypothesis, not evidence that everyone will behave alike. Check actual data and ask whose behavior is easiest to observe. The ethics of a policy also depends on rights and distribution, not only predicted aggregate efficiency.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] studies feedback in systems. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] asks who carries the burden.
EXAMPLE: A fee meant to reduce congestion may change travel times, but it can also change who can afford a route and when deliveries are made.
DO: Map intended and possible unintended responses to a hypothetical rule.
TRY: Name a group whose response or cost your first map left out.
REFLECT: Which behavior did I assume without a way to check it?
CHECK: Why follow incentives through a policy?::People can adjust to changed rules, creating indirect benefits, burdens, or side effects beyond the intended effect.
MODEL: Change~Name the new rule.|Respond~Map likely adaptations.|Evaluate~Check evidence and distribution.

## Separate growth from living standards
QUESTION: What does a rising headline economic number tell me about people's lives?
CORE: A national total can grow while gains are uneven, prices change, or important unpaid work remains invisible. Ask whether a figure is total or per person, adjusted for prices, and distributed across households. Also consider health, access, security, and environmental costs relevant to the question. No single measure can settle every public value. Use several indicators and state what each can and cannot show.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] warns against reading more into a measure than it supports. [[39 History, citizenship, and public institutions]] asks how public choices shape distribution.
EXAMPLE: A city reports growing output, while many residents face longer commutes and higher housing costs. Both belong in an account of living conditions.
DO: Take a hypothetical growth claim and list four questions needed to interpret it.
TRY: Add one measure of distribution or daily life to the headline number.
REFLECT: Whose experience disappeared when I used only an average?
CHECK: Why is a growth total insufficient to describe wellbeing?::It can hide price changes, uneven gains, unpaid work, and other conditions people experience.
MODEL: Specify~Identify what the number measures.|Disaggregate~Look at people and prices.|Contextualize~Add relevant lived conditions.

## Examine a trade barrier from several positions
QUESTION: Who gains, who pays, and what assumptions support the case for a restriction?
CORE: Limits on exchange can protect some jobs or capabilities and raise costs or reduce options elsewhere. Their effects depend on the industry, time horizon, alternatives, and how firms and other countries respond. State the policy goal, the proposed mechanism, and who bears transition costs. A general argument for trade does not erase a local community's hardship, and a local benefit does not settle the whole public choice.
CONNECT: [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] brings distribution into the decision. [[20 Strategy, competition, and cooperation]] asks whether cooperation or protected capacity better serves a defined goal.
EXAMPLE: A tariff might help a local manufacturer but increase input costs for small firms that use its products.
DO: Make a stakeholder map for a hypothetical trade restriction with benefits and costs.
TRY: Identify a non-trade measure that could address the same local harm and compare it.
REFLECT: Which stakeholder's story had I mistaken for the whole economy?
CHECK: What questions belong in a trade policy comparison?::The goal, mechanism, affected groups, transition costs, alternatives, and likely responses.
MODEL: Goal~Define the public purpose.|Map~List gains and burdens.|Compare~Test alternative ways to reach the goal.

## Ask what a historical economic analogy leaves out
QUESTION: When does a country's past offer a useful warning rather than a prediction?
CORE: Historical episodes can reveal mechanisms, such as debt pressure, aging populations, or policy feedback. They also occur within particular institutions, global conditions, and social choices. Before transferring a lesson, identify the proposed similarity and at least two differences that could change the result. Look for competing explanations of the episode and later evidence. A striking label for one nation should not become a stereotype or an inevitable fate for another.
CONNECT: [[20 Strategy, competition, and cooperation]] treats analogies as questions. [[39 History, citizenship, and public institutions]] reads national stories in context.
EXAMPLE: A commentator compares today's housing debt with an earlier country's slowdown. The comparison needs data on lending rules, population, and policy response before it predicts the same path.
DO: Write a two-column comparison of similarities and differences for an economic analogy.
TRY: State one observation that would weaken the analogy.
REFLECT: Was the analogy teaching a mechanism or giving me an easy headline?
CHECK: How should a historical economic analogy be used?::As a hypothesis about a mechanism whose relevant similarities, differences, and evidence must be examined.
MODEL: Identify~Name the claimed mechanism.|Compare~Check context and differences.|Test~Look for evidence against transfer.
