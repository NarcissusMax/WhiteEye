## Prepare a useful appointment question
QUESTION: What information would help a professional understand my concern and my goals?
CORE: A care conversation improves when the concern is described concretely: onset, pattern, severity, relevant changes, medicines or exposures when appropriate, and what you want help deciding. Bring a concise record rather than a self-diagnosis assembled from search results. Ask what possibilities are being considered and what follow-up would change the plan. Emergency symptoms require local urgent care rather than waiting for a routine appointment or a course exercise.
CONNECT: [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] turns a vague concern into a useful question. [[31 Mind, behavior, and consciousness]] keeps reported experience distinct from theory.
EXAMPLE: A person notes when pain occurs, what changes it, and how it affects work, then asks which signs would call for follow-up.
DO: Draft a hypothetical one-minute description of a health concern without naming a diagnosis.
TRY: Add two questions about next steps and uncertainty.
REFLECT: Did I prepare information that helps care or a conclusion I want confirmed?
CHECK: What belongs in a useful care question?::A concrete description of the concern, its pattern and impact, relevant context, and the decision needing help.
MODEL: Describe~State the concern and pattern.|Ask~Clarify possibilities and next steps.|Follow~Record the agreed plan.

## Ask what a test result changes
QUESTION: Will this test help choose a different action, or only produce another number?
CORE: Tests have purposes, limits, and possible harms. Ask what question a test addresses, how reliable the result is in your situation, what a positive or negative result would mean, and what happens next. A normal result may not explain every symptom; an unusual result may need confirmation or context. Interpret specialist imaging, laboratory, or drug information with qualified professionals and current guidance, not from an isolated page of a technical book.
CONNECT: [[35 Science, models, and technical literacy]] explains uncertainty and specialist context. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] asks whether information changes a decision.
EXAMPLE: A clinician and patient discuss whether imaging would change treatment now or whether another first step is more appropriate.
DO: Write four questions to ask before a hypothetical non-urgent test.
TRY: For positive and negative outcomes, state what decision would follow in the hypothetical case.
REFLECT: Was I seeking a decision aid or the feeling that every uncertainty had a number?
CHECK: What makes a health test useful?::It addresses a defined question and has results that can be interpreted and used in a care decision.
MODEL: Purpose~Name the decision.|Interpret~Ask about reliability and limits.|Act~Agree on the next step for each result.

## Compare health advice by population and outcome
QUESTION: Was this advice tested for people and outcomes like mine?
CORE: A study or book may concern a particular age group, condition, training level, or measure. Before applying its conclusion, check who was studied, what was compared, how long follow-up lasted, and which harms were observed. A personal story can suggest a question but cannot establish an average effect. When advice conflicts with an existing condition or care plan, discuss it with a qualified professional. Avoid universal promises from a narrow result.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] examines study design. [[27 Physical training, recovery, and nutrition literacy]] applies the same question to exercise and food claims.
EXAMPLE: A supplement claim based on trained athletes does not automatically describe benefit for an older person with a medical condition.
DO: Create a population, comparison, outcome, and time checklist for a hypothetical health claim.
TRY: Identify one way an effect could differ for another group.
REFLECT: Which similarity between me and the study participants had I assumed rather than checked?
CHECK: Why examine population in health advice?::Effects and risks may differ across people, conditions, and settings from those originally studied.
MODEL: Identify~Find the studied group.|Compare~Check outcomes and alternatives.|Fit~Discuss personal relevance with qualified care.

## Make a follow-up plan understandable
QUESTION: What should I do after receiving care, and when should I seek help again?
CORE: A care plan is easier to use when it states the intended action, expected course, possible concerns, and review point in plain language. Ask who to contact if the plan cannot be followed or symptoms change. Record the instructions accurately and check them with the professional; do not improvise medication changes from a general course. Include practical constraints such as cost, transport, or care duties so the plan can be adjusted responsibly.
CONNECT: [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] treats handover and ownership as part of completion. [[25 Money, records, and financial judgment]] recognizes resource constraints in real decisions.
EXAMPLE: After an appointment, a patient confirms when a follow-up is due and what change would require earlier contact.
DO: Rewrite a hypothetical vague care instruction into actions, timing, and a contact question.
TRY: Identify one practical barrier to following it and a question to raise with the care team.
REFLECT: What part of the plan would I struggle to explain to someone helping me?
CHECK: What makes a follow-up plan usable?::Clear actions, timing, warning conditions, contact route, and accommodation of practical constraints.
MODEL: Confirm~Restate the agreed plan.|Prepare~Identify barriers and contacts.|Review~Know when and how to follow up.
