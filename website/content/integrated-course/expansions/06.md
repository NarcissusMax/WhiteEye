## Make a daily list fit the actual day
QUESTION: How can a task list help without pretending the day has unlimited capacity?
CORE: A list records obligations, but it is not a schedule. Estimate the time and preparation each task needs, then compare the total with available time after fixed duties, breaks, and likely interruptions. Identify one or two outcomes that matter most. Move or renegotiate the rest explicitly. This prevents a long list from making every day look like a failure. It also exposes a structural overload that cannot be solved by writing smaller boxes.
CONNECT: [[04 Agency, responsibility, and boundaries]] supports renegotiating commitments. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] supplies opportunity cost: choosing one task means giving time from another. Use the list to make that trade-off visible.
EXAMPLE: You have six hours free and list eleven hours of work. The useful action is to schedule the two essential tasks, tell one person their request will be late, and move the remaining work rather than silently expecting a miracle.
DO: Estimate time for tomorrow's tasks and compare it with time truly available.
TRY: Select two priority outcomes, then explicitly defer, delegate, or renegotiate at least one other item.
REFLECT: Was the problem my pace, the list, or the amount of work promised?
CHECK: What turns a task list into a realistic plan?::Comparing estimated effort with available time and making explicit choices about what will not fit.
MODEL: Count~Estimate capacity and task effort.|Choose~Protect the highest priorities.|Communicate~Revise the remaining commitments.

## Recognize busywork as avoidance of uncertainty
QUESTION: Am I doing easy visible tasks to postpone a harder important one?
CORE: Small tasks can be necessary, but they also offer quick completion and a feeling of control. Notice when you repeatedly choose them before a task whose next step is unclear, whose result will be judged, or whose consequences matter. Ask what uncertainty you are avoiding. Define the smallest useful step in the important work and give it a short protected window. The purpose is not to despise administration; it is to prevent administration from occupying every hour in which thinking could happen.
CONNECT: [[23 Creative work and sustained projects]] describes resistance during uncertain work. [[03 Emotions, acceptance, and a steadier response]] helps you stay with discomfort long enough to start. A time log can distinguish a real operational duty from an avoidance pattern.
EXAMPLE: You keep reorganizing project folders while a proposal remains unwritten. The first useful step may be listing the audience, decision, and three missing facts. That is less polished than a new folder system but moves the proposal.
DO: Identify an important task repeatedly displaced by small tasks. Write the specific uncertainty you face.
TRY: Work on a ten-minute first step before opening routine messages. Record what became clearer.
REFLECT: Which small task was necessary, and which protected me from an uncertain one?
CHECK: How can busywork differ from useful administration?::It may create visible activity while repeatedly displacing a more important task whose uncertainty has not been addressed.
MODEL: Notice~Find the repeated displacement.|Clarify~Name the uncertainty and first step.|Begin~Protect a short work window.

## Give interruptions an agreed route
QUESTION: How can I protect focus while remaining reachable for real needs?
CORE: An interruption policy works only when other people know how to reach you for genuinely urgent matters. Choose a focus period, a channel for exceptions, and a time when ordinary requests will be handled. Invite colleagues or family to help define what counts as urgent. If your role requires immediate responses, design shorter focus windows rather than pretending the demand does not exist. The goal is a shared arrangement, not disappearing without explanation.
CONNECT: [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] helps negotiate the arrangement. [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] shows how an individual schedule depends on team expectations. Attention is partly a coordination problem.
EXAMPLE: A teammate needs quick answers during a release. You agree that production failures go by phone, routine questions go in a shared queue, and the queue is checked at two set times. Both responsiveness and concentrated work have a route.
DO: List the three most common sources of interruption and which truly need immediate attention.
TRY: Propose one focus window and one urgent channel to the relevant people. Revise it from their feedback.
REFLECT: Which interruptions came from unclear expectations rather than unavoidable events?
CHECK: What must an interruption policy include?::A focus period, a route for genuine urgency, a time for ordinary requests, and agreement with affected people.
MODEL: Sort~Separate urgent from ordinary contact.|Agree~Set channels and response windows.|Review~Adjust when real needs appear.

## Leave a buffer for work that does not announce itself
QUESTION: Why does a tightly filled calendar fail even when my estimates look reasonable?
CORE: Estimates often describe the visible task and omit setup, travel, recovery, clarifying questions, and unexpected changes. A calendar filled to its edges assumes none of these will occur. Look at recent days to estimate ordinary variability, then leave space for it. A buffer is not wasted time; it is capacity for the work as it actually happens. If every buffer is immediately claimed by new commitments, the plan has not gained resilience.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] calls this attention to uncertainty and slack. [[27 Physical training, recovery, and nutrition literacy]] makes a similar distinction between useful load and capacity to recover. A schedule is a model, not a command to reality.
EXAMPLE: Four meetings are planned back to back. Each needs five minutes of preparation and one follow-up. A buffer between two meetings protects those tasks and reduces errors more than pretending the meetings are the only work.
DO: Review three recent days and identify time omitted from the original plan.
TRY: Add one evidence-based buffer to tomorrow's schedule and record whether it was used.
REFLECT: Did I treat necessary transition time as a personal failure?
CHECK: What does a buffer represent?::Capacity for ordinary uncertainty, transitions, and follow-up that a task-only estimate leaves out.
MODEL: Review~Measure omitted time.|Reserve~Protect realistic space.|Adjust~Use actual days to revise estimates.
