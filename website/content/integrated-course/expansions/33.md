## Build an argument from premises to conclusion
QUESTION: What must be true for this philosophical conclusion to follow?
CORE: A philosophical claim is clearer when its supporting steps are visible. Write the conclusion, list the premises, and ask whether the inference is valid and whether the premises are defensible. A valid argument can begin from a disputed premise; a true-sounding conclusion can rest on weak reasoning. Identify hidden assumptions and the best objection. The aim is to understand a position well enough to test it, not to win by terminology.
CONNECT: [[10 Logic, arguments, and fair disagreement]] provides argument structure. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] uses explicit premises when values conflict.
EXAMPLE: An argument that every free choice must be unconstrained depends on a particular definition of freedom; naming it lets others examine the claim.
DO: Turn one philosophical statement into a conclusion and two or three premises.
TRY: Write the strongest objection to a premise rather than to the person's character.
REFLECT: Which assumption was carrying the most weight invisibly?
CHECK: What are the two main checks on a philosophical argument?::Whether the conclusion follows from its premises and whether those premises are justified.
MODEL: State~Identify the conclusion.|Expose~List premises and assumptions.|Test~Check inference and objections.

## Compare accounts of the self without flattening them
QUESTION: What does each account mean by a person continuing over time?
CORE: Philosophical and psychological traditions can emphasize memory, body, social role, consciousness, or practical commitments. They may answer different questions under the same word 'self.' Define the question before comparing answers: legal responsibility, lived identity, moral development, and metaphysical persistence need not have one test. Use an example to show where accounts diverge. Do not turn a complex tradition into a slogan for personal improvement.
CONNECT: [[31 Mind, behavior, and consciousness]] examines experience and explanation. [[34 Religion, spiritual traditions, and belief]] shows that traditions attach different meanings to personhood.
EXAMPLE: A person changes values over decades. One account emphasizes continuity of memory, another the body's life, and another their relations and commitments.
DO: Choose one identity question and compare two accounts in their own terms.
TRY: Find a case where the accounts would give different answers.
REFLECT: Was I asking one question while comparing answers to another?
CHECK: Why define a question before comparing accounts of self?::Different accounts may address different kinds of continuity, responsibility, or experience.
MODEL: Specify~Name the identity question.|Compare~State each account's criterion.|Probe~Find a case that separates them.

## Ask what would count as a good life
QUESTION: Which goods are ends, and which are tools for living well?
CORE: Pleasure, achievement, belonging, virtue, freedom, and meaning can support one another and sometimes compete. Ask what a proposed life ideal includes, what it neglects, and whose circumstances it assumes. Money and status can be useful instruments without defining the entire goal. A personal answer should remain revisable through experience and honest conversation with people affected by it. A tradition can illuminate a choice without settling it for everyone.
CONNECT: [[02 Values, meaning, and a direction worth choosing]] turns priorities into choices. [[25 Money, records, and financial judgment]] asks what resources are for.
EXAMPLE: Someone accepts a promotion for higher income, then weighs the loss of care time and creative work against what that income enables.
DO: List five goods you want in a life and distinguish ends from means.
TRY: Describe a real trade-off between two of those goods without claiming one is always superior.
REFLECT: Which good had I pursued as a measure while forgetting its purpose?
CHECK: What makes a good-life question practical?::It clarifies which goods matter, how they conflict, and what choices express them under real constraints.
MODEL: Name~List valued goods.|Order~Examine trade-offs.|Live~Test priorities through choices and review.
BOOKS: B022 B174 B245

## Practice charitable disagreement with a tradition
QUESTION: Can I state a view fairly before rejecting or adapting it?
CORE: Read a position in its historical and conceptual setting. Identify the problem its author was trying to solve, the strongest reason for the view, and the terms that carry special meaning. Then state the objection. Agreement is not required; charity prevents an easy caricature from substituting for understanding. Also examine harms or exclusions a tradition may contain. A serious reading can preserve what is useful while refusing what is unsupported or unjust.
CONNECT: [[36 Literature, story, and human possibility]] reads works in context. [[39 History, citizenship, and public institutions]] asks how ideas interact with power.
EXAMPLE: A reader examines an older account of duty, finds its concern with loyalty understandable, and still challenges whom the account excluded.
DO: Write a fair three-sentence version of a philosophical view you disagree with.
TRY: Add one historically grounded objection and one idea worth retaining.
REFLECT: Did my criticism address the strongest version of the view?
CHECK: What does charitable disagreement require?::Representing a view's problem and strongest reasons accurately before offering a serious objection.
MODEL: Context~Identify the author's question.|Represent~State the strongest case.|Critique~Examine limits and harms.
BOOKS: B111 B195 B316
