## Track who controls what the reader knows
QUESTION: Which facts are shown, withheld, or filtered through a narrator?
CORE: A story can create meaning by controlling perspective. Note who speaks, when they speak, what they could know, and what another character might report differently. A narrator's confident explanation may be part of the story rather than the author's verdict. Keep a small record of clues and revise an interpretation when later details change it. This practice strengthens attention to language without treating every narrator as deceptive.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] separates report from inference. [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] examines how wording guides attention.
EXAMPLE: A novel's narrator describes a rival as selfish, but scenes show the narrator repeatedly omitting the rival's reasons.
DO: Annotate one short fictional scene with what is observed and what the narrator concludes.
TRY: Rewrite a two-sentence event summary from another character's limited view.
REFLECT: Which detail changed meaning when I asked who was telling the story?
CHECK: Why track a narrator's knowledge?::A story's claims are filtered through a speaker with a particular position, information, and interests.
MODEL: Locate~Identify the speaker.|Compare~Separate scene and interpretation.|Revise~Update the reading when new evidence appears.

## Read a maxim against a counterexample
QUESTION: Where might a memorable line be useful, and where would it mislead?
CORE: A maxim compresses experience into a striking sentence. Its force can make a conditional insight sound universal. State the situation in which it helps, then construct a plausible counterexample and an exception. Compare competing maxims when they recommend opposite actions. The result is not that every short lesson is useless, but that its usefulness depends on judgment about context.
CONNECT: [[10 Logic, arguments, and fair disagreement]] tests general claims. [[20 Strategy, competition, and cooperation]] treats compact strategic rules as prompts, not scripts.
EXAMPLE: 'Act quickly' may help in a reversible opportunity but mislead in a costly medical or contractual choice.
DO: Choose a short moral or practical maxim and write its likely scope.
TRY: Create one counterexample and revise the maxim into a conditional statement in your own words.
REFLECT: What did the memorable wording make me forget about conditions?
CHECK: How should a maxim be used?::As a compact prompt whose scope and exceptions must be tested against the actual situation.
MODEL: Hear~State the maxim's insight.|Counter~Find a limiting case.|Use~Apply it only where conditions fit.

## Compare a character's choices with their explanations
QUESTION: What does a character do when their stated values are tested?
CORE: Literature lets us observe the gap between a person's self-description and their actions across time. Track the decision, available alternatives, pressure, and later explanation. Avoid turning a complex character into a diagnosis or a single moral lesson. Ask how the setting and other people's responses shape the choice. This can deepen moral imagination without treating fiction as direct evidence about all real people.
CONNECT: [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] examines intentions and effects. [[31 Mind, behavior, and consciousness]] warns against overclaiming hidden motives.
EXAMPLE: A character insists they value honesty but conceals a harmful act. The reader examines fear, advantage, and consequence rather than assigning one permanent label.
DO: Make a two-column record of one character's stated belief and consequential actions.
TRY: Identify a scene that complicates your first judgment.
REFLECT: Did I use the story to examine a tension or to confirm a simple theory of people?
CHECK: What can a character's choices reveal?::How stated values, pressures, alternatives, and consequences interact in a particular story.
MODEL: Track~Record decisions and claims.|Contextualize~Name pressures and alternatives.|Reconsider~Use complicating scenes.

## Keep witness, fiction, and history distinct
QUESTION: What kind of claim can this narrative support?
CORE: A novel can illuminate possibilities; a memoir offers situated testimony; a historical study makes claims supported by records and methods. Each can be powerful, but their authority differs. Ask whose voice is missing, what was remembered later, and what can be checked independently. Do not dismiss a witness because one detail is uncertain, and do not generalize one witness's experience to everyone in the setting. Let each genre contribute what it can.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] asks what evidence supports a generalization. [[39 History, citizenship, and public institutions]] reads public claims through records and context.
EXAMPLE: A prison memoir conveys lived experience; a historian may compare archives and many accounts to evaluate an institutional pattern.
DO: Label three claims from a hypothetical novel, memoir, and history by the evidence each offers.
TRY: Name one independent record that could test a broad historical claim.
REFLECT: Which narrative's emotional force made me overlook its evidential limits?
CHECK: Why distinguish narrative genres?::Fiction, testimony, and historical research offer different kinds of insight and support different kinds of claims.
MODEL: Identify~Name the genre and voice.|Check~Ask what evidence it offers.|Limit~Avoid unsupported generalization.
