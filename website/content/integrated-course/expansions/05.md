## Replace a behavior without losing its function
QUESTION: What need does an unwanted habit serve, and what else could serve it?
CORE: Removing a familiar action leaves behind the cue and the reason it was attractive. If the behavior offers a break, companionship, stimulation, or relief, a replacement must address at least part of that function. Describe the moment before the action, the immediate benefit, and the later cost. Choose an alternative small enough to use in that same moment. A substitute that is morally impressive but impractical will rarely survive contact with the original cue.
CONNECT: [[03 Emotions, acceptance, and a steadier response]] helps distinguish relief from a longer-term solution. [[06 Attention, time, and a workable day]] provides places for breaks that do not require a crisis. Treat the replacement as a test, not an identity pledge.
EXAMPLE: You check your phone whenever a difficult paragraph stalls. The immediate function is a break from uncertainty. Standing up, writing the next question, and taking a two-minute walk may provide a break while preserving the path back to work.
DO: For one unwanted habit, record cue, immediate benefit, delayed cost, and the need it appears to serve.
TRY: Test one replacement at the same cue three times. Record whether it served the need well enough.
REFLECT: What did the original behavior do for me that I had ignored?
CHECK: Why should a replacement address the function of a habit?::The cue and need remain after the old action is removed, so an unrelated substitute may not be usable when the moment arrives.
MODEL: Observe~Find cue and immediate benefit.|Substitute~Choose a feasible alternative.|Test~Compare effects in the original situation.

## Change friction before relying on resolve
QUESTION: Which small obstacle or convenience determines what I usually do?
CORE: A behavior can become more or less likely because of a few steps: an open tab, a packed bag, a password prompt, or a tool within reach. Friction is not a moral failing. It is part of the environment in which a decision occurs. Make the chosen action easy to start and the unwanted default slightly less automatic, while checking that the design does not harm others or block legitimate needs. A useful adjustment changes the next choice, not merely your opinion about it.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] treats friction as a design lever. [[30 Digital security and privacy]] adds an important limit: convenience should not weaken account protection or recovery. The right amount of friction depends on the stakes.
EXAMPLE: You intend to practice guitar but keep it in a case behind stored boxes. Moving it to a safe visible stand removes several steps. A session becomes easier without pretending motivation will be constant.
DO: Walk through the first two minutes of one desired behavior and list every step that must happen first.
TRY: Remove one unnecessary step and add one sensible pause before a distracting default. Observe both for a week.
REFLECT: Which action became easier because of the environment rather than a change in willpower?
CHECK: What is friction in behavior design?::The effort or delay between a cue and an action, which can be changed to support a chosen behavior.
MODEL: Trace~List the steps before action.|Design~Remove or add friction deliberately.|Observe~Check whether behavior changes.

## Use accountability without making shame the mechanism
QUESTION: How can a commitment to another person help without turning a missed day into concealment?
CORE: Accountability works when another person can see the agreed action, ask what happened, and help you revise the plan. Vague public declarations often create pressure to appear consistent rather than to learn. Agree in advance what counts, how often you will report, and what response a missed attempt should trigger. A missed day can prompt a question about conditions and the next repetition; it need not become a trial of character.
CONNECT: [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] helps make feedback specific. [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] shows how a metric can distort behavior when it becomes the whole goal. Accountability should make reality easier to report.
EXAMPLE: Two classmates agree to exchange a photo of one solved practice problem each Friday. If one misses, they report the obstacle and choose a smaller problem for next week instead of claiming they studied when they did not.
DO: Draft a one-week accountability agreement: action, proof, check-in time, and response to a miss.
TRY: Share it with a willing partner and ask whether the reporting feels safe enough to be honest.
REFLECT: Did accountability improve the practice or only my appearance of consistency?
CHECK: What makes accountability useful for learning?::Specific actions, honest reporting, and a plan for revision after a miss rather than pressure to hide it.
MODEL: Agree~Define action and check-in.|Report~Show what happened.|Revise~Change the plan using the result.
BOOKS: B150 B288 B293
