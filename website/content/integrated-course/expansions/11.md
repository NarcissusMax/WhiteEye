## Distinguish observation, intervention, and counterfactual
QUESTION: What kind of causal question am I asking?
CORE: Seeing that two things occur together asks about observation. Changing one thing to see what follows asks about intervention. Asking what would have happened to the same case under a different choice asks a counterfactual question that cannot usually be observed directly. These are related but not interchangeable. State which question you need before choosing evidence. A pattern in observational data may help prediction while leaving the effect of an intervention unresolved.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] needs intervention questions when choosing action. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] helps describe patterns without promoting them to causes. The distinction prevents a predictive model from being mistaken for a treatment plan.
EXAMPLE: People who sleep more may perform better on a test. That pattern does not by itself tell you what would happen if every participant slept an extra hour, because other conditions may differ.
DO: Take one causal claim and write an observation question, an intervention question, and a counterfactual question about it.
TRY: Name the kind of evidence each question would require and what remains unobservable.
REFLECT: Which question did the evidence actually answer?
CHECK: Why is observing an association different from intervening?::The groups may differ in other ways, so changing a factor may not reproduce the observed association.
MODEL: Classify~Name the causal question.|Design~Match evidence to it.|Limit~State what remains uncertain.

## Draw a causal path before adjusting for variables
QUESTION: Which variables might explain a relationship, and which might lie on the path I want to study?
CORE: A simple causal diagram can make assumptions visible. Put possible causes and effects in boxes and draw arrows for the directions you think matter. A confounder may influence both the proposed cause and outcome; a mediator may be part of the route by which the cause affects the outcome. Adjusting for variables indiscriminately can obscure the very effect you want to understand. The diagram is a hypothesis that needs domain knowledge, not a picture that proves causation.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] uses diagrams to expose assumptions about change. [[35 Science, models, and technical literacy]] asks where a model's boundaries are. A disputed arrow should remain visibly disputed.
EXAMPLE: You study whether training improves job performance. Prior experience may influence both who receives training and performance; improved skill may be a mediator of training's effect. They play different roles in the question.
DO: Draw a four-box diagram for a low-stakes causal claim. Label one possible confounder and one possible mediator.
TRY: Write one arrow you are uncertain about and what evidence or expertise might clarify it.
REFLECT: Did the diagram reveal an assumption I had hidden in prose?
CHECK: What is the role of a causal diagram?::It makes assumed relationships and variable roles visible for testing; it does not prove that the arrows are correct.
MODEL: Draw~Place variables and proposed arrows.|Classify~Separate confounders and mediators.|Question~Mark uncertain links.

## Separate prediction from explanation
QUESTION: Can a model be useful for forecasting while still giving a poor account of why something happens?
CORE: A model may predict an outcome from a pattern without identifying the mechanism behind it. Conversely, a plausible mechanism may not forecast accurately in a changing setting. Ask whether the task is to anticipate, to explain, or to choose an intervention. Evaluate the model against that task. When a predictive pattern changes, the explanation may need revision; when an explanation is elegant, it still needs contact with observed results.
CONNECT: [[35 Science, models, and technical literacy]] examines what a model represents. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] helps avoid overconfidence when a pattern has only been seen in one sample. The value of a model depends on its purpose and domain.
EXAMPLE: A shop predicts busy days from previous traffic patterns. The prediction helps staffing, but it does not show whether an advertisement caused the demand. A separate comparison would be needed for that question.
DO: Find one model or rule you use. State whether its main purpose is prediction, explanation, or intervention.
TRY: Identify one result that would show it is failing at that purpose.
REFLECT: Have I asked a prediction tool to justify an action it was not built to evaluate?
CHECK: Why can prediction and explanation diverge?::A pattern can forecast outcomes without revealing the causal mechanism, and a plausible mechanism may still predict poorly.
MODEL: Purpose~Name the question.|Test~Evaluate the model for that question.|Restrict~Avoid claims it cannot support.

## Treat surprising data as a question for the model
QUESTION: What should I do when evidence conflicts with my preferred explanation?
CORE: First check the observation: its measurement, timing, selection, and possible error. Then inspect the explanation's assumptions. A single anomaly need not overthrow a well-supported account, but repeatedly dismissing anomalies protects the account from learning. Write down what each plausible explanation would predict next. A good revision may narrow the model's scope rather than replace every part of it.
CONNECT: [[10 Logic, arguments, and fair disagreement]] helps state the competing explanations. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] warns against confirmation bias. Science improves through disciplined correction, not reflexive loyalty to or rejection of a theory.
EXAMPLE: A routine study method works for factual recall but fails on transfer problems. Instead of calling the result noise, you check whether transfer requires comparison and application that the routine never practiced.
DO: Find an observation that does not fit one of your working explanations. Check how the observation was produced.
TRY: Write two possible revisions and one future observation that would distinguish them.
REFLECT: Did I investigate the surprising result as carefully as a confirming one?
CHECK: How should an anomaly be handled?::Check the observation, examine assumptions, and derive tests that could refine the explanation.
MODEL: Verify~Inspect the measurement.|Compare~List competing explanations.|Test~Choose a distinguishing observation.
BOOKS: B027 B291
