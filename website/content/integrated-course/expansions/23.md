## Make a draft that answers one question
QUESTION: What can the next version teach me that thinking alone cannot?
CORE: A draft is an experiment in form. Choose one uncertainty about audience, structure, material, or meaning and make a version that exposes it. A rough draft should be complete enough to receive useful feedback; fragments can hide the difficulty of joining parts. Save discarded ideas without making the draft carry all of them. The goal is a visible object you can inspect, not a performance of natural talent.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] uses bounded tests. [[38 Craft, perception, and material practice]] uses prototypes to learn from materials.
EXAMPLE: A writer unsure about structure produces a full short essay with a beginning, argument, and ending before polishing sentences.
DO: Name one creative uncertainty and make a small complete draft that tests it.
TRY: Ask one intended reader what they understood and where they lost the thread.
REFLECT: Which question did making the draft answer?
CHECK: What is a useful draft?::A complete-enough version that exposes a specific uncertainty to inspection and feedback.
MODEL: Ask~Name the uncertainty.|Make~Create a bounded version.|Learn~Inspect how it works.

## Choose feedback for its job
QUESTION: What kind of response will improve this stage of the work?
CORE: Different feedback serves different decisions. An early draft may need a test of clarity and purpose; a near-final piece may need precision and defects. Specify the audience and the question before asking. Separate a reviewer's observation from their suggested fix: the observation may be accurate even when their proposed solution does not fit. Do not collect endless opinions that prevent you from choosing a direction.
CONNECT: [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] helps receive criticism without surrendering judgment. [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] sets review points while change is possible.
EXAMPLE: A designer asks intended users where they could not find a button, then tests alternative layouts instead of accepting the first requested color change.
DO: Write a feedback request with the stage, intended audience, and two specific questions.
TRY: Sort one response into observation, interpretation, and suggested fix.
REFLECT: Did I ask for reassurance when the work needed information?
CHECK: Why specify the job of feedback?::It directs attention to the decision the creator can still improve and reduces unhelpful opinion collection.
MODEL: Frame~Name the stage and question.|Listen~Separate observation from remedy.|Decide~Choose a revision deliberately.

## Protect a work rhythm without worshipping it
QUESTION: How can regular creation fit a real life that changes?
CORE: A ritual can lower the cost of starting by making time, place, and first action familiar. It is a support, not proof of virtue. Design a minimum version for disrupted days and a return path after absence. Notice whether the schedule produces work, recovery, and a life you still want. If the ritual becomes a way to avoid showing the work to others, it needs revision.
CONNECT: [[05 Habits and behavior design]] explains cues and friction. [[06 Attention, time, and a workable day]] balances focus with other obligations.
EXAMPLE: An illustrator usually works for forty minutes after breakfast; on care-heavy days, ten minutes of sketching keeps contact with the project without pretending the day is ordinary.
DO: Define a usual creative session and a feasible minimum session.
TRY: Plan the exact first action for returning after a missed week.
REFLECT: Is my ritual helping me produce and revise, or merely helping me feel organized?
CHECK: What is the purpose of a creative ritual?::To make starting and returning easier while serving the work and the rest of life.
MODEL: Cue~Choose a repeatable start.|Scale~Set a minimum version.|Return~Resume after disruption without drama.

## Decide when a project is ready to share
QUESTION: What standard of completeness fits the purpose and the stakes?
CORE: Perfection is an impossible release criterion, but care still matters. Define the essential promise to the audience: what the work must do, what errors would cause harm, and what can be improved later. Test those essentials. If stakes are high, use stronger review before release. If stakes are low and revision remains possible, sharing a sound version can produce the feedback that private polishing cannot.
CONNECT: [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] uses acceptance checks. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] helps judge harms that cannot be dismissed as minor defects.
EXAMPLE: A community event guide needs accurate accessibility and time information before sharing; its decorative layout can improve in a later edition.
DO: Write three release criteria for a current project, including one non-negotiable accuracy or care check.
TRY: Decide what can be revised after people begin using it.
REFLECT: Which unfinished detail really matters to the audience, and which only delays exposure?
CHECK: How should a creator choose a release standard?::By the work's purpose, audience, consequences of error, and ability to revise after release.
MODEL: Promise~Define essential use.|Check~Test consequential details.|Share~Release at a fitting standard and learn.
BOOKS: B055 B202 B224
