## Keep the scope of a negative clear
QUESTION: What exactly is being denied in this statement?
CORE: A negative can apply to an entire claim, one term, or one part of a condition. Those meanings can lead to different conclusions. Rewrite a complicated sentence in plain alternatives, then test each with a concrete case. In ordinary conversation, ask the speaker which meaning they intend rather than winning an argument against an ambiguity. Clear negation matters in instructions, contracts, reports, and personal expectations.
CONNECT: [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] addresses wording. [[17 Influence, persuasion, and negotiation]] shows why ambiguous terms can hide an apparent agreement. Logic is useful when it makes the real choice visible.
EXAMPLE: 'You do not have to attend both sessions' might mean neither is required, or only that one session is enough. Asking whether at least one is required prevents a mistaken plan.
DO: Find a sentence with 'not', 'unless', or 'either' that could be read two ways. Write both readings.
TRY: Test each reading with a concrete scenario and ask which is intended.
REFLECT: Which disagreement was partly about the position of a negative?
CHECK: What is the scope of a negative?::The part of a statement that the negative applies to, which can change the statement's meaning.
MODEL: Locate~Find the negative word.|Rewrite~State plausible readings.|Test~Use a case to distinguish them.

## Test all, some, and none with small cases
QUESTION: How do quantity words change what follows from a claim?
CORE: 'All' says every member of a defined group has a property; 'some' says at least one does; 'none' says no member does. The group and the property must be clear. A claim that all members of one group belong to another does not automatically reverse. One counterexample can disprove a universal claim, while one supporting example cannot prove it. In ordinary language, 'some' may suggest 'not all', but in basic logic it need not.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] helps find representative evidence. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] reminds you that a logically valid form can still rest on a harmful or false premise about people.
EXAMPLE: 'All registered volunteers completed training' does not imply 'All people who completed training are registered volunteers.' A small diagram or list of names makes the missing step visible.
DO: Rewrite one broad claim using an explicit group, property, and quantity word.
TRY: Draw a three-person case that supports it, and another that disproves it if possible.
REFLECT: Did I reverse a one-way claim or treat one example as universal proof?
CHECK: Why does one example not prove an 'all' claim?::A universal claim covers every member of the group; one example checks only one member.
MODEL: Define~State group and property.|Quantify~Choose all, some, or none.|Test~Use a small case or counterexample.

## Map premises before debating a conclusion
QUESTION: Which reasons are doing the work in this argument?
CORE: A conclusion can be supported by several reasons, and each reason may depend on another claim. Draw the structure before deciding whether you agree: what is the main conclusion, which premises support it, and which premises are assumed? Mark whether reasons work independently or only together. This can reveal a missing bridge, a useful point of agreement, or a factual question that could settle part of the dispute.
CONNECT: [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] helps state each claim fairly. [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] helps assess premises that concern the world. A neat argument map does not make its premises true; it makes their role inspectable.
EXAMPLE: A proposal says the team should change software because the current tool is slow and the new tool is cheaper. The cost claim may be independent of the speed claim, but the conclusion also assumes migration will not disrupt essential work.
DO: Map one real argument with a conclusion, two stated premises, and one plausible unstated assumption.
TRY: Ask which premise needs evidence and what evidence would matter.
REFLECT: Did mapping expose a shared concern hidden by disagreement over the conclusion?
CHECK: What is the purpose of an argument map?::To show how premises and assumptions support a conclusion so each part can be tested fairly.
MODEL: Extract~State conclusion and reasons.|Connect~Show independent or joint support.|Check~Examine assumptions and evidence.

## Use contradiction as a test, not a trick
QUESTION: How can an impossible consequence show that an assumption needs revision?
CORE: Sometimes you can test an assumption by following it carefully until it conflicts with a known fact or with another consequence of the same assumption. The contradiction shows that at least one premise in the chain cannot stand as stated. It does not automatically reveal which premise is wrong if several were used. Trace each step and check whether a term changed meaning along the way.
CONNECT: [[35 Science, models, and technical literacy]] distinguishes formal proof from empirical investigation. A contradiction in a model may expose its assumptions; a surprising observation in life also requires checking the observation and context.
EXAMPLE: You assume a meeting can finish in thirty minutes, but its required agenda contains four ten-minute reports and a discussion. The assumption conflicts with the stated minimum time. Either the agenda, timing, or meeting goal must change.
DO: Choose one plan with an apparently impossible combination of commitments. Write the assumptions that create the conflict.
TRY: Change one assumption and show whether the revised plan becomes feasible.
REFLECT: Which premise had I treated as fixed without examining it?
CHECK: What does a contradiction establish?::That the set of premises and steps cannot all be correct as stated, not necessarily which single premise failed.
MODEL: Assume~State the starting claims.|Derive~Follow their consequences.|Revise~Locate a premise or step to change.
BOOKS: B079 B027
