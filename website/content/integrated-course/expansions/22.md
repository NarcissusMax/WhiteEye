## Plan capacity with uncertainty in view
QUESTION: Can the people and resources assigned actually carry the plan?
CORE: A schedule is only credible when work fits available capacity. Estimate effort as a range, include recurring duties, and ask where specialized attention is required. Do not fill every hour with ideal work and then call interruptions a failure of discipline. A reserve is useful when uncertainty is real; its size should follow the stakes and the cost of delay. Revisit the plan when new information arrives instead of hiding slippage.
CONNECT: [[06 Attention, time, and a workable day]] treats available time as constrained. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] distinguishes a plan from a wish.
EXAMPLE: A small nonprofit plans a launch around two volunteers' actual weekly availability and leaves room for approvals rather than assigning five full days of work to each.
DO: Estimate a current deliverable as a range and list the people or resources it depends on.
TRY: Remove or defer work until the plan fits realistic capacity.
REFLECT: Where had optimism been written into the schedule as certainty?
CHECK: What makes a delivery schedule credible?::Its work, dependencies, and uncertainty fit the capacity actually available.
MODEL: Estimate~Use effort ranges.|Fit~Match work to capacity.|Revise~Update the plan when facts change.

## Make feedback useful before the finish
QUESTION: When can feedback still change the outcome?
CORE: Feedback delivered after a project is finished can explain a disappointment, but it cannot cheaply improve the current version. Set review points when work is still changeable. Show an artifact or observed result rather than asking whether an abstract plan sounds good. Ask reviewers what they tried to do, where they became unsure, and what mattered to them. Record a decision about each significant finding, including why a change is declined.
CONNECT: [[23 Creative work and sustained projects]] uses revision to improve the artifact. [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] helps hear feedback without treating it as a verdict on identity.
EXAMPLE: A service team asks three intended users to try a form while there is still time to change its wording and flow.
DO: Choose one project review point before final delivery and name the artifact to show.
TRY: Write three questions that ask about use and difficulty rather than praise.
REFLECT: Was I seeking feedback early enough to act on it?
CHECK: When is project feedback most valuable?::When a concrete result can be reviewed while the relevant choices are still changeable.
MODEL: Show~Present a usable draft.|Observe~Ask how it works for recipients.|Decide~Change or explain the choice.

## Recover from a missed commitment openly
QUESTION: What should happen when a promised step will not be delivered on time?
CORE: An overdue task is information about the plan and its dependencies. Tell affected people promptly what is known, what is uncertain, and what options remain. Separate the impact from excuses or blame. Replan the next useful step, assign an owner, and confirm the new expectation. Repeated delay may signal a structural issue such as overloaded capacity or unclear approval, not simply a need for more reminders.
CONNECT: [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] links accountability with system design. [[17 Influence, persuasion, and negotiation]] helps renegotiate a term without pretending the old promise was met.
EXAMPLE: A report cannot be finished because a data review is delayed. The owner alerts the recipient, shares the completed sections, and agrees on a revised date after checking the dependency.
DO: Draft a four-sentence update for a hypothetical missed milestone.
TRY: Name one underlying condition to investigate if the same delay repeats.
REFLECT: Did I communicate early enough for others to adjust their plans?
CHECK: What does responsible recovery from delay include?::Early notice, the actual impact, workable options, a new owner or date, and a check on recurring causes.
MODEL: Notify~State the delay and impact.|Replan~Choose a feasible next step.|Learn~Investigate repeated causes.

## Deliver a handover someone else can use
QUESTION: What must the next person know to maintain the result?
CORE: Delivery includes the information needed to use, maintain, and correct what was produced. A handover should identify the final artifact, owner, location, known limits, routine checks, and how to raise a problem. Keep it short enough to be used. If the recipient cannot perform the next ordinary action without the original maker present, the work may be finished for the maker but unfinished for its user.
CONNECT: [[24 Business, customers, and useful value]] judges value by use. [[38 Craft, perception, and material practice]] judges workmanship partly by whether an object serves its setting over time.
EXAMPLE: A volunteer rota tool is handed over with access permissions, a two-minute update guide, and a contact for errors, not merely a link to the finished screen.
DO: Make a one-page handover checklist for a current or past deliverable.
TRY: Ask a recipient to complete one routine action using only the handover.
REFLECT: What knowledge existed only in my head?
CHECK: What makes a handover complete?::The recipient can locate, use, maintain, and report problems with the delivered result.
MODEL: Package~Identify artifact and owner.|Explain~Give routine instructions and limits.|Verify~Watch a recipient use it.
