## Choose a setting that suits the real goal
QUESTION: Can I change where and when I act instead of only increasing effort?
CORE: A plan can improve by changing the setting: audience, channel, timing, partnership, or scope. Before assuming you must work harder against the strongest obstacle, ask whether another route can reach the legitimate goal at lower cost. Avoid importing military language literally into ordinary relationships. In cooperative settings, the better move may be a shared design rather than an opponent's weakness.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] identifies constraints. [[24 Business, customers, and useful value]] asks where a service genuinely fits. Context is a strategic variable, not a moral judgment about people.
EXAMPLE: A community workshop struggles at a weekday location. Moving it to a library near public transport may do more for attendance than more advertising to the same audience.
DO: Name one goal and three settings in which it could be pursued.
TRY: Compare access, cost, risk, and who benefits in each setting before choosing one.
REFLECT: Was I treating the current setting as fixed because I had already invested in it?
CHECK: What can changing the setting do for a strategy?::It can alter constraints and opportunities so a legitimate goal becomes more reachable without merely increasing effort.
MODEL: Define~State the goal.|Vary~Compare settings and routes.|Choose~Select the route with justified fit.

## Use information without pretending it is complete
QUESTION: What must I know before acting, and what will remain uncertain?
CORE: Strategy needs information about objectives, constraints, other participants, and the environment. More information can help, but waiting for perfect knowledge can delay a reversible opportunity. Identify the unknowns that could change the choice, how they might be checked, and the cost of checking. Distinguish a trustworthy signal from a rumor designed to provoke action. Privacy, consent, and lawful conduct still limit how information may be gathered.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] helps weigh uncertain observations. [[30 Digital security and privacy]] defines boundaries for information gathering. A good information plan is targeted and ethical.
EXAMPLE: Before opening a pop-up service, you need to know foot traffic, permission requirements, and basic costs. You do not need to predict every customer's preference; a small pilot can answer some questions more cheaply.
DO: For a current plan, list three unknowns and rank them by how much they could change the decision.
TRY: Choose one low-cost, lawful way to learn about the highest-ranked unknown.
REFLECT: Which fact did I seek because it mattered, and which because certainty felt comforting?
CHECK: What makes information gathering strategic?::It targets unknowns that could change the action and weighs the cost and ethics of obtaining them.
MODEL: Rank~Find decision-changing unknowns.|Check~Gather proportionate information.|Act~Use what is known while preserving revision.

## Distinguish a strategic analogy from a recipe
QUESTION: What can a historical or business case teach without dictating my behavior?
CORE: Analogies reveal a structure: scarce resources, timing, cooperation, or a bottleneck. They also conceal differences in stakes, rules, consent, and what counts as success. State the feature you are borrowing and the features that do not transfer. A successful tactic in one domain is evidence that it worked there, not proof it belongs in another. Human relationships especially require care that a competitive analogy may miss.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] asks whether a borrowed idea survives a real test. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] asks whether a transferred method respects people outside the original case. A good analogy helps formulate a question.
EXAMPLE: A business turnaround may show the value of choosing a decisive constraint. In a family disagreement, treating another person as a constraint to overcome would be harmful; the useful transfer is to notice what limits the conversation before choosing a response.
DO: Take one strategy analogy you like. List two structural similarities and three important differences from your real situation.
TRY: Rewrite its lesson as a question you can investigate rather than a rule to copy.
REFLECT: Which difference between domains most changed the advice?
CHECK: What is the safe use of a strategic analogy?::Borrow a specific structural insight while naming differences in rules, stakes, and ethics before applying it.
MODEL: Compare~Identify the shared structure.|Limit~Name non-transferable features.|Test~Use the analogy to ask a better question.
