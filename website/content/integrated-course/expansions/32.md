## Test a principle on the hard case
QUESTION: Would I accept this rule when it burdens someone I care about or benefits someone I oppose?
CORE: A principle is easier to endorse in a case where it favors us. Test it on cases with reversed identities and inconvenient consequences. Then ask whether differences between the cases are morally relevant rather than merely emotionally salient. Consistency does not mean treating unlike situations as identical; it means explaining the difference openly. A hard case can refine a rule without proving that all rules are empty.
CONNECT: [[10 Logic, arguments, and fair disagreement]] examines consistency in reasoning. [[39 History, citizenship, and public institutions]] shows why public standards need to survive changes in who holds power.
EXAMPLE: A group supports a right to challenge decisions when its own members are affected. It then asks whether the same right applies to a rival group.
DO: Write a principle behind a real judgment and reverse the roles in a hypothetical case.
TRY: Name any difference that still justifies treating the two cases differently.
REFLECT: Did my principle change because the facts changed or because my loyalty did?
CHECK: What does a hard-case test reveal?::Whether a proposed principle survives role reversal or needs a clearly relevant exception.
MODEL: State~Write the principle.|Reverse~Change the affected identities.|Explain~Defend any relevant distinction.

## Include absent people in a decision
QUESTION: Who will experience the effects but cannot speak in the room?
CORE: Decisions often favor the people present, powerful, or easy to count. List those who will bear costs later, including dependents, future users, and people excluded by access barriers. You cannot speak for them with certainty, but you can seek representative input, relevant evidence, and safeguards. Record which interests remain uncertain. A fair process makes this uncertainty visible rather than assuming silence means consent.
CONNECT: [[26 Economics, incentives, and public choices]] examines distribution. [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] assigns responsibility for affected people beyond the immediate decision makers.
EXAMPLE: A school changes a digital process after consulting staff, then discovers that some families have limited device access. Their interests needed attention before rollout.
DO: List three absent groups in a hypothetical institutional decision.
TRY: Propose one feasible way to learn about each group's likely experience.
REFLECT: Which people were absent because reaching them would slow the decision?
CHECK: Why identify absent stakeholders?::A decision can shift costs onto people who are not represented in the discussion.
MODEL: List~Name affected nonparticipants.|Learn~Seek relevant input.|Protect~Add review or safeguards.

## Distinguish a good intention from a justified action
QUESTION: What else must be examined when someone meant well?
CORE: Intention matters for understanding a choice, but it does not erase foreseeable harm, ignored warnings, or a duty to repair. Examine what the person knew, what alternatives were available, how the action affected others, and how they respond after learning more. Avoid reducing an entire person to one outcome while still taking that outcome seriously. An ethical review should support learning and accountability together.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] looks for side effects and displaced costs. [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] shows how acknowledgment and repair can follow a mistake.
EXAMPLE: An organizer changes a meeting time to help most participants but overlooks those with care duties. The intention explains the choice; it does not remove the need to address the exclusion.
DO: Analyze a hypothetical well-meant action under intention, foreseeable effects, and available alternatives.
TRY: Write one repair action that responds to the actual burden.
REFLECT: Did I use intention to understand the choice or to excuse its effect?
CHECK: Why are good intentions ethically insufficient?::They do not by themselves settle consequences, ignored information, alternatives, or duties of repair.
MODEL: Understand~Identify intention and knowledge.|Assess~Examine effects and options.|Repair~Respond to harm.

## Give a moral disagreement a useful shape
QUESTION: Are we disputing facts, values, priorities, or what a principle means here?
CORE: A disagreement becomes more tractable when its source is named. Participants may share a value but disagree about evidence, or accept the same facts while weighing duties differently. Ask each side to state its strongest reason and what would change its view. Some conflicts remain after clarification; a fair process can still decide action while recording dissent. Avoid using forced agreement as a measure of respect.
CONNECT: [[10 Logic, arguments, and fair disagreement]] separates argument structure from rhetoric. [[17 Influence, persuasion, and negotiation]] seeks workable decisions without pretending all interests match.
EXAMPLE: Two volunteers agree that an event should be accessible but disagree whether a proposed venue meets that standard. They need evidence about access before debating priorities.
DO: Sort one hypothetical disagreement into facts, meanings, values, and priorities.
TRY: Write a fair version of each side's strongest reason.
REFLECT: Was I arguing about a value when the missing issue was a fact, or the reverse?
CHECK: How can a moral disagreement be clarified?::Identify whether the dispute concerns facts, meanings, values, or priorities, then examine the strongest reasons on each side.
MODEL: Locate~Name the source of disagreement.|Represent~State each reason fairly.|Decide~Record action and remaining dissent.
