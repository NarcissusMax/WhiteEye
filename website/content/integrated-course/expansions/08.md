## Separate practice from performance
QUESTION: Why should training look different from the moment I must deliver?
CORE: During practice, you can pause, repeat, isolate errors, and accept a temporarily awkward method. During performance, you must often act with the skill available now and keep attention on the task. Trying to repair every detail in the middle of performance can interfere with it; never reviewing errors afterward prevents improvement. Plan both modes. Use practice to change a specific component and performance to test how the whole skill holds together.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] makes the distinction visible: a pilot can be paused and revised, while a live service must work with its current design. [[23 Creative work and sustained projects]] makes room for drafting before release. The modes support one another when you review the transfer.
EXAMPLE: A speaker rehearses pauses and transitions slowly. During the talk, they focus on the audience and the next idea. Afterward, they review the recording and choose one component for the next rehearsal.
DO: Identify one skill and write what practice permits that performance does not.
TRY: Schedule one isolated drill and one whole-task attempt, then compare what transferred.
REFLECT: Was I trying to learn during performance or only to look good during practice?
CHECK: What is the practical difference between practice and performance?::Practice can isolate and repair components; performance uses the current skill in a whole task under real constraints.
MODEL: Isolate~Choose a component to improve.|Perform~Use the whole skill in context.|Review~Find the next practice target.

## Use a brief pre-performance routine
QUESTION: How can I prepare for a demanding attempt without making preparation an endless ritual?
CORE: A pre-performance routine can reduce last-minute decisions by checking equipment, reviewing the task, and choosing one process cue. Keep it short, repeatable, and tied to what the performance actually demands. A routine cannot guarantee calm or success. It should help you begin with usable attention, even if nerves remain. If preparation expands until it delays the attempt, it has become another problem to examine.
CONNECT: [[06 Attention, time, and a workable day]] supplies a bounded preparation window. [[03 Emotions, acceptance, and a steadier response]] helps you act without demanding that anxiety disappear first. The cue should point to an action, not a mood.
EXAMPLE: Before a difficult call, you check the agenda, write the first question, take a pause, and dial. You do not spend another hour trying to feel perfectly confident.
DO: Design a three-step routine for one upcoming performance: necessary check, process cue, and start signal.
TRY: Use it twice and note whether each step helped the task or merely postponed it.
REFLECT: Which part of preparation put my attention on the work?
CHECK: What makes a useful pre-performance routine?::It is brief, repeatable, and directs attention to the task without requiring a particular feeling or guaranteeing an outcome.
MODEL: Check~Prepare what is necessary.|Cue~Choose one process action.|Begin~Start at the planned time.

## Train the weak link without making it your identity
QUESTION: How do I improve a limiting component of a skill?
CORE: A whole performance may fail because of one component: reading the situation, preparing, executing, or recovering. Identify the limiting step from evidence, then design a drill that targets it. Keep the drill close enough to the real task that improvement can transfer. Retest in a whole attempt. Do not turn a current weakness into a permanent self-description; the point of isolating it is to make change possible.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] uses the idea of a decisive constraint. [[27 Physical training, recovery, and nutrition literacy]] adds that increasing load without capacity can cause harm. A weak link is a training hypothesis, not a reason to ignore rest or context.
EXAMPLE: A player can pass accurately in a stationary drill but loses the ball after receiving under pressure. More stationary passes may not solve the limiting step; a supervised drill with scanning and movement may be needed.
DO: Review a recent whole-task attempt and name the earliest step where performance broke down.
TRY: Design one safe, short drill for that component and one whole-task retest.
REFLECT: Did improving the component change the whole performance, or was another constraint more important?
CHECK: Why retest a weak-link drill in the whole task?::Improvement in an isolated component matters only if it transfers to performance in context.
MODEL: Diagnose~Find the limiting step.|Drill~Practice that component safely.|Retest~Check transfer in a whole attempt.
