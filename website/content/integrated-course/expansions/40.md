## Choose a project with an honest boundary
QUESTION: What can this course help me test without claiming to solve my whole life?
CORE: A capstone should be large enough to connect several abilities and small enough to review with evidence. Define the situation, people affected, what is within your control, and what requires consent or specialist help. Name the outcome and a time boundary. Do not convert every relationship or health issue into an experiment. A bounded project makes a result interpretable and leaves room for dignity, uncertainty, and ordinary life.
CONNECT: [[01 Self-knowledge and your starting point]] begins from an actual baseline. [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] turns a gap into a testable question.
EXAMPLE: A student improves a weekly study process over six weeks, rather than promising to become an entirely different person by the end of term.
DO: Draft a capstone brief with scope, people, outcome, time, and limits.
TRY: Remove one aim that cannot be evaluated within the chosen period.
REFLECT: Was I choosing a project I could learn from or a grand identity promise?
CHECK: What makes a capstone project bounded?::A defined situation, outcome, time period, affected people, and clear limits on control and risk.
MODEL: Define~Name the improvement question.|Bound~Set time and ethical limits.|Test~Choose observable actions.

## Connect lessons by the jobs they perform
QUESTION: Why does each selected idea belong in this project?
CORE: A list of favorite lessons is not integration. Assign each idea a task: diagnose the problem, design a habit, gather evidence, protect a boundary, communicate with others, or review the outcome. State where two ideas support each other and where they pull in different directions. If an idea has no clear role, leave it out. Integration should make the action and its limits clearer rather than produce an impressive diagram.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] joins diagnosis and action. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] checks the cost of the plan to others.
EXAMPLE: A team project uses a baseline lesson to define the issue, a time-planning lesson to fit the work, a listening lesson to gather feedback, and an evidence lesson to review the change.
DO: Select four lessons from different modules and write one job for each.
TRY: Name one tension between two selected ideas and how it will be monitored.
REFLECT: Which idea was present only because I admired it, not because the project needed it?
CHECK: What makes course ideas integrated?::Each has a specific job in the same project, with interactions and tensions made explicit.
MODEL: Assign~Give each lesson a function.|Link~Show how the functions interact.|Limit~Name tensions and exclusions.

## Show evidence without overstating the result
QUESTION: What can I conclude from the trial I actually ran?
CORE: Compare the observed result with the baseline and intended outcome. Describe what changed in your action, what else changed in the setting, who was affected, and how certain the observation is. A single successful week may justify continuing a practice, not claiming a universal method. A disappointing result can reveal a weak hypothesis, a poor implementation, or an unsuitable measure. Keep the original decision record visible so hindsight does not improve your earlier reasoning on paper.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] limits causal inference. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] protects against a confident story after the fact.
EXAMPLE: A new planning routine coincides with more finished work for three weeks, but the learner also had fewer obligations; the report names both changes.
DO: Write a three-paragraph trial report: action, observation, alternative explanations.
TRY: State one claim the evidence supports and one stronger claim it does not yet support.
REFLECT: Was I reporting what happened or selling the plan to myself?
CHECK: How should a capstone result be reported?::With baseline, observed change, implementation, alternative explanations, uncertainty, and a limited conclusion.
MODEL: Compare~Set result against baseline.|Qualify~Name confounders and limits.|Conclude~Claim only what evidence supports.

## Design the next cycle from what was learned
QUESTION: What should continue, change, stop, or be investigated next?
CORE: The capstone ends with a decision, not a final judgment on the person. Keep actions that served the chosen aim at an acceptable cost. Change parts that failed for a plausible reason, stop parts that caused harm or added little value, and investigate uncertainties that could alter the next plan. Choose a future review date and a way to notice drift. Share the field guide in terms another person can understand, while protecting private information and context.
CONNECT: [[05 Habits and behavior design]] supports continuation. [[22 Planning, collaboration, and delivery]] turns learning into a usable handover.
EXAMPLE: A learner keeps a weekly review, shortens a daily checklist nobody used, and tests whether a different meeting time improves participation.
DO: Make a keep, change, stop, and investigate table for a hypothetical capstone.
TRY: Schedule a next review and name the evidence to bring to it.
REFLECT: Did I let the result shape the next cycle, or did I return to the original plan by habit?
CHECK: What is the capstone's final product?::A defensible next cycle grounded in the trial's result, costs, uncertainties, and a future review.
MODEL: Sort~Keep, change, stop, investigate.|Plan~Choose the next bounded action.|Return~Set evidence and date for review.
