## Compare scent before reading the label
QUESTION: How does a name or expectation change the words I use for a sensation?
CORE: Sensory judgment can be shaped by labels, memory, and changing perception over time. In a safe setting, compare familiar, non-irritating scents without reading their names first; describe what you notice, then read the labels and revisit your description. Do not use unknown chemicals or treat scent as a medical test. The aim is to separate the first experience from the story a name invites, while accepting that sensory vocabulary and sensitivity differ between people.
CONNECT: [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] separates description from expectation. [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] shows how words help and limit perception.
EXAMPLE: A person smells two familiar teas before seeing their names and finds that the labels change which notes they notice on a second pass.
DO: With safe familiar materials, write three descriptive words for two scents before checking their names.
TRY: Revisit later and note which description stayed stable and which changed.
REFLECT: Did the label help me notice a feature or make me invent one?
CHECK: Why compare a scent before reading its label?::It helps reveal how expectation and naming can influence a sensory description.
