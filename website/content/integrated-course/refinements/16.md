## Give a child room for a safe attempt
QUESTION: What can the child try, and what support makes the risk appropriate?
CORE: Learning often requires a child to make choices, experience small mistakes, and try again. An adult still has responsibility for safety, consent, and the child's level of development. Define the boundary, offer enough help to start, and observe before taking over. Ask what the child noticed rather than praising a fixed trait or treating every mistake as a lesson that must be engineered. Different children and situations need different support.
CONNECT: [[07 Learning, memory, and understanding]] values feedback from an attempt. [[28 Sport, movement, and supervised skill]] matches challenge with supervision and readiness.
EXAMPLE: A child helps prepare a simple snack with safe tools and adult oversight, then explains what they would change next time.
DO: Describe a low-risk task a child could partly own, including the adult's safety boundary.
TRY: Write one question that invites the child's observation after the attempt.
REFLECT: Was I protecting the child from harm or from the discomfort of trying?
CHECK: What makes a child's attempt appropriately supported?::A task fitted to development, clear safety boundaries, room to choose, and responsive adult help.
MODEL: Bound~Set the safe limits.|Try~Let the child act.|Reflect~Ask what they noticed.

## Make family expectations explicit
QUESTION: Which rule do people assume everyone knows, though no one has agreed on it?
CORE: Families often develop implicit rules about availability, privacy, money, and care. These can organize life until circumstances change or members interpret them differently. State the expectation as a request or agreement rather than a test of love. Ask what each person can realistically do and revisit the rule when roles or needs shift. Explicit agreements do not remove affection; they reduce avoidable resentment and leave room for different capacities.
CONNECT: [[15 Relationships, consent, and mutual choice]] discusses expectations before resentment grows. [[04 Agency, responsibility, and boundaries]] separates a limit from rejection.
EXAMPLE: Adult siblings assume the same person will always arrange transport for a parent. They discuss availability and share tasks instead of treating a missed ride as proof of indifference.
DO: Identify one unspoken family expectation and rewrite it as a discussable request.
TRY: Name two different capacities that an agreement should account for.
REFLECT: What did I call obvious because it was familiar to me?
CHECK: Why name an implicit family rule?::It lets people compare interpretations and capacities before a repeated mismatch becomes resentment.
MODEL: Surface~Name the assumed rule.|Discuss~Hear capacities and needs.|Agree~Choose a revisable arrangement.
