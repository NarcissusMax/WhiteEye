## Check whether a measure is only a proxy
QUESTION: What real outcome am I hoping this number represents?
CORE: A proxy is a measurable stand-in for a harder-to-observe outcome. It can help track change, but it can also improve while the real purpose does not. State the relationship you assume between the proxy and the outcome, then look for cases where it breaks. Pair the number with a direct observation where feasible. If people are rewarded for the proxy, watch how their behavior changes and whether the measure becomes less informative.
CONNECT: [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] uses goals without letting them replace judgment. [[26 Economics, incentives, and public choices]] studies incentives around measures.
EXAMPLE: More course logins may signal engagement, but learners may still be unable to use what they read. A short application task tests the deeper aim.
DO: Choose one common metric and name the outcome it is meant to represent.
TRY: Give a case where the metric improves while that outcome worsens.
REFLECT: Which number had I treated as the goal rather than an imperfect sign of it?
CHECK: What is the main risk of a proxy measure?::The proxy can improve while the underlying outcome stays the same or gets worse.
MODEL: Name~Define the real outcome.|Compare~Test proxy against direct evidence.|Adjust~Use more than one signal.

## Treat a null result as information
QUESTION: What did a test rule out, and what remains possible when it shows little change?
CORE: A test that finds no clear effect is not automatically useless. First check whether the intervention happened, the measure was sensitive enough, and the period was long enough. Then ask which explanations remain. Do not turn a small or noisy trial into proof that an effect is impossible. Report the result plainly and decide whether a better test, a different intervention, or stopping is justified by the stakes.
CONNECT: [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] reviews implementation and outcome separately. [[09 Observation, bias, and calibrated judgment]] keeps confidence matched to the evidence.
EXAMPLE: A reminder email does not change attendance in a two-week trial. The team checks whether people received it and whether scheduling, not forgetting, was the main barrier.
DO: Write three interpretations of a hypothetical null result, including one about the test design.
TRY: Choose the least costly next observation that would distinguish them.
REFLECT: Was I tempted to call the idea proven wrong or secretly successful without checking the trial?
CHECK: Why is a null result not a final verdict?::Its meaning depends on whether the change occurred, the measure and duration could detect an effect, and alternative explanations.
MODEL: Verify~Check implementation.|Interpret~List possible reasons.|Decide~Retest, revise, or stop.
