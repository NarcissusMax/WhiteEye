## Separate a confident speaker from a supported claim
QUESTION: What reasons remain if I set aside the speaker's status and delivery?
CORE: Expertise can be relevant, but a title, fluent voice, or admired history does not make every claim sound. Identify the exact conclusion and evidence, then ask whether the person's expertise covers this question and whether the evidence can be checked. Do not reject a claim merely because an authority stated it; use authority as a clue about where to look, not a replacement for the argument.
CONNECT: [[18 Manipulation, propaganda, and independent judgment]] examines authority cues. [[35 Science, models, and technical literacy]] respects specialist knowledge while checking scope and method.
EXAMPLE: A successful entrepreneur gives nutrition advice. Their business record may be genuine but does not establish expertise on that health claim.
DO: Rewrite a persuasive statement as a claim plus its offered reasons, leaving out status words.
TRY: Name what expertise and evidence would be relevant to evaluating it.
REFLECT: Did the speaker's confidence change my standard of proof?
CHECK: How should authority affect an argument?::It can guide where to investigate, but the claim still needs relevant scope and support.
MODEL: Isolate~State the claim.|Scope~Check the expertise.|Evaluate~Examine relevant evidence.

## Ask whether a reason reaches the conclusion
QUESTION: Even if this fact is true, does it support the proposed action?
CORE: An argument may use true details that are emotionally vivid but irrelevant to the decision. Write the conclusion, then explain the bridge from each reason to it. If the bridge depends on an unstated value or causal assumption, name it. A good counterquestion tests that bridge without attacking the person. This is especially useful when a story is moving but supports several different responses.
CONNECT: [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] makes value premises visible. [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] uses a precise question to reopen reasoning.
EXAMPLE: A speaker describes one bad customer experience and concludes that a service should close. The story may justify repair, staffing change, or further investigation; closure needs more support.
DO: Find the missing bridge in a hypothetical argument from a true event to a broad recommendation.
TRY: Ask one neutral question that would test the bridge.
REFLECT: Which detail was relevant to emotion but not yet decisive for the conclusion?
CHECK: What is a relevance check?::Testing whether a stated reason actually supports the conclusion through a defensible premise or mechanism.
MODEL: State~Name the recommendation.|Bridge~Expose the connecting premise.|Test~Seek evidence or a counterexample.
