## Notice the evidence that is missing
QUESTION: If my explanation were right, what else would I expect to see?
CORE: Observation includes absences, but an absence matters only when there was a clear reason to expect something. State the prediction before treating missing evidence as a clue. Check whether the setting would have made the evidence visible and whether another explanation predicts the same absence. This keeps 'what is not there' from becoming a license for imaginative suspicion. A useful missing clue narrows a question; it rarely settles a person's motive by itself.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] asks what a hypothesis predicts. [[29 Safety, awareness, and de-escalation]] favors concrete behavior over speculative threat reading.
EXAMPLE: A delivery report is absent from a shared folder. The team checks whether it was due, whether the folder was used, and whether email was an alternative route before inferring neglect.
DO: Write one hypothesis and one observation you would expect if it were true.
TRY: Describe how you would know whether the expected evidence could have been seen.
REFLECT: Did I treat an absence as proof without defining an expectation?
CHECK: When is missing evidence informative?::When a hypothesis predicts observable evidence that should have appeared under the actual conditions.
MODEL: Predict~Name the expected sign.|Check~Confirm it could be observed.|Interpret~Compare alternatives.

## Audit a first impression with a short record
QUESTION: How accurate are my quick judgments when I can check them later?
CORE: An impression may be useful or misleading depending on training, feedback, and context. Write the impression, the specific cues, and a confidence estimate before learning the outcome. Later, compare it with what actually happened and note what remained unknowable. Do this for appropriate low-stakes situations, not as a method of judging strangers' character. The purpose is calibration: learning when to trust a cue and when to ask another question.
CONNECT: [[31 Mind, behavior, and consciousness]] distinguishes behavior from an explanation. [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] offers direct conversation when an impression concerns another person.
EXAMPLE: A project lead predicts that a new handoff will confuse users because two labels overlap. A short user test confirms or challenges that impression.
DO: Record three low-stakes predictions with cue, confidence, and later outcome.
TRY: Find one case where an omitted fact changed your interpretation.
REFLECT: Which kind of cue has earned confidence, and which only feels vivid?
CHECK: What is the purpose of an impression audit?::To compare recorded predictions with outcomes and calibrate which cues are reliable in which settings.
MODEL: Capture~Write impression and confidence.|Check~Observe the outcome.|Calibrate~Adjust future trust in the cue.
