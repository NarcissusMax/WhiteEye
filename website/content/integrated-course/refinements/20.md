## Ask when a clever tactic damages the relationship
QUESTION: Could this short-term win make later cooperation harder?
CORE: A tactic can achieve an immediate goal and still weaken trust, reputation, or the conditions for future exchange. Name the people whose cooperation you may need again and what they will infer from the tactic. Compare the gain with the longer relationship, and consider whether a direct, fairer approach can reach the same aim. Competitive language should not make ordinary partners into enemies. Some conflicts are real, but even then the method matters.
CONNECT: [[17 Influence, persuasion, and negotiation]] examines implementable agreements. [[32 Ethics, fairness, and difficult choices]] tests whether a win respects others' choice and dignity.
EXAMPLE: A supplier could exploit an unclear term to save money once, but a transparent discussion may protect a long working relationship.
DO: Choose a hypothetical clever tactic and list its immediate gain and likely later effects.
TRY: Design a direct alternative and compare both against the real goal.
REFLECT: Was I impressed by the tactic because it was smart, or because it served the whole strategy?
CHECK: What should a tactic be judged against?::The full goal, including future cooperation, trust, costs, and ethical limits.
MODEL: Gain~Name the immediate benefit.|Trace~Consider later responses.|Choose~Use the method that fits the whole aim.

## Use a warning signal without becoming alarmist
QUESTION: Which early change would justify revising the plan?
CORE: Strategic surprise is easier to manage when a team has named a small set of signals connected to its major assumptions. A signal is a prompt to investigate, not proof that a forecast has come true. Define the threshold for review and the options available at that point. Avoid collecting every possible warning into an overwhelming dashboard. The aim is timely adjustment with a clear record of why the plan changed.
CONNECT: [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] uses scenarios and decision thresholds. [[21 Practical problem solving and experiments]] separates a measured change from its possible causes.
EXAMPLE: A service expansion depends on stable staffing. A sustained rise in unfilled shifts triggers a capacity review before new bookings are promised.
DO: Name one strategic assumption, one early signal, and a review threshold.
TRY: Write two alternative explanations for the signal before deciding what it means.
REFLECT: Did I treat a signal as a cue to learn or as a certainty about the future?
CHECK: What is a strategic warning signal for?::To prompt timely review of a consequential assumption, not to prove a prediction automatically.
MODEL: Assume~State what the plan relies on.|Watch~Track a relevant signal.|Review~Investigate when the threshold is met.
