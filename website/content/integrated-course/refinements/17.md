## Build a professional connection by usefulness
QUESTION: What can I learn or contribute without turning the person into a target?
CORE: Networking works better as an exchange between people with real work than as a hunt for access. Learn what the other person does, ask a relevant question, and offer help only when it is genuinely useful and within your capacity. Follow up with a specific reason rather than a generic demand for attention. Accept a decline without escalating pressure. A relationship may become valuable over time without needing an immediate transaction.
CONNECT: [[24 Business, customers, and useful value]] asks what help actually serves another person. [[15 Relationships, consent, and mutual choice]] protects the other's choice.
EXAMPLE: After a public workshop, an attendee thanks the organizer for a particular insight and shares a resource directly relevant to the session, without asking for a job in the same message.
DO: Draft a short professional follow-up with a specific observation and no implied obligation.
TRY: Identify one useful contribution you could offer only if it fits the other person's work.
REFLECT: Was I interested in their work or only in what their position could give me?
CHECK: What makes a professional connection ethical?::Specific interest, relevant contribution, respect for the person's time and choice, and no hidden demand for immediate return.
MODEL: Learn~Understand the person's work.|Offer~Contribute where relevant.|Respect~Accept their response.

## Make appreciation specific and fair
QUESTION: What did the person do, and why did it matter?
CORE: Specific appreciation gives useful recognition without flattery. Name the action, its effect, and the context, while avoiding a claim about a person's entire character from one event. In a team, notice work that is less visible but necessary. Do not use praise as a preface to a hidden request or a way to manage someone's mood. Recognition should be truthful and proportionate, and feedback about improvements can remain separate.
CONNECT: [[19 Leadership, teams, and institutions]] asks who receives recognition for shared outcomes. [[14 Listening, conflict, and repair]] keeps communication honest rather than strategic performance.
EXAMPLE: A coordinator thanks a volunteer for checking accessible directions before publication and explains that it prevented confusion for participants.
DO: Write a two-sentence appreciation naming action and effect.
TRY: Identify one quiet contribution in a shared project that was easy to miss.
REFLECT: Was my praise accurate, or was I trying to obtain a response?
CHECK: What makes appreciation useful and fair?::It truthfully names a specific action and effect without exaggeration or a hidden demand.
MODEL: Observe~Identify the contribution.|Name~Explain its effect.|Keep~Separate recognition from requests.
BOOKS: B151 B113 B272
