## Build a small vocabulary for a complex topic
QUESTION: Which things, types, and relationships need clear names before we can reason together?
CORE: A useful classification scheme defines its domain, categories, relationships, and examples. Start small: list the things people discuss, distinguish a type from an individual item, and name relationships such as 'part of' or 'causes.' Test edge cases with other readers. A tidy diagram can still encode a disputed assumption, so record who chose the terms and where the scheme fails. The goal is clearer shared work, not a claim that the vocabulary perfectly mirrors reality.
CONNECT: [[13 Language, questions, and clear expression]] defines terms doing the work. [[12 Decisions, strategy, and systems]] uses maps whose boundaries affect conclusions.
EXAMPLE: A library team distinguishes a 'book edition' from a 'physical copy' so its catalogue does not treat every copy as a different work.
DO: Choose a small domain and define four categories, three relationships, and one example of each.
TRY: Ask another person to classify an edge case and note any disagreement.
REFLECT: Which assumption became visible only when I tried to define the categories?
CHECK: What belongs in a small ontology?::A defined domain, categories, relationships, examples, and explicit limits or disputed cases.
MODEL: Name~List concepts and instances.|Relate~Connect them precisely.|Test~Use edge cases to revise.

## Trace a technical claim to its origin
QUESTION: Which step in the chain turned a finding into this headline or diagram?
CORE: A claim may pass from study to summary to presentation to a popular example. At each step, scope and uncertainty can change. Find the original question and method when the claim matters, then compare the later version with its actual result. Record the date because technical knowledge and standards can change. A source trail supports accurate interpretation without requiring a general reader to become a specialist in every field.
CONNECT: [[11 Evidence, science, and causal claims]] examines method and inference. [[18 Manipulation, propaganda, and independent judgment]] notices selective framing.
EXAMPLE: A slide calls a pattern a proven cause; the underlying study only compared two groups at one time. The reader marks the stronger causal word as a later addition.
DO: Draw a three-step source trail for a hypothetical technical headline.
TRY: Identify one word or number that changed meaning along the chain.
REFLECT: Was I trusting the polished presentation more than the original question and method?
CHECK: Why trace a technical claim's provenance?::To see where scope, certainty, or wording changed between the original finding and a later presentation.
MODEL: Locate~Find the originating work.|Compare~Track wording and method.|Qualify~State the supported claim.
